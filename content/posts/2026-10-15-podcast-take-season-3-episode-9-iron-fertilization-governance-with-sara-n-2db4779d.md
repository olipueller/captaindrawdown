> Take on a podcast episode from **Ocean Solutions: a NOISE Lab podcast**, originally published Wed, 30 Se.
> Listen: <https://soundcloud.com/morgan-reed-raven/season-3-episode-9-iron>

**TL;DR**
- ExOIS (Woods Hole) is targeting the Northeast Pacific for ocean iron fertilization field trials and still fundraising; Nawaz pushed them to stand up a governance advisory board.
- "Nutrient robbing" is the distinguishing risk: iron added in one basin can suppress productivity decades later off, say, Peru. Useful framing for permanence-adjacent liability.
- Workshop finding: publics liked macroalgae sinking until shown it at scale, then preferred offshore direct air capture with sub-seabed mineralization. First time I've seen this "betrayal effect" named.
- The 2012 Haida/Russ George dump became a geoengineering scandal because of the carbon-credit financing, not the iron. Nawaz says it froze German ocean alkalinity funding for ~15 years.
- Baseline public knowledge of marine CDR in BC/Washington: effectively zero. Cautious interest, not opposition.

Ocean Solutions (NOISE Lab / UC Santa Barbara Bren School) closes season three with hosts Lina and Alex interviewing Sara Nawaz, research director at American University's Institute for Responsible Carbon Removal. The hour covers how she runs deliberative workshops on marine carbon dioxide removal (mCDR) in the Pacific Northwest, her advisory work on the Exploring Ocean Iron Solutions (ExOIS) programme, and why iron fertilization carries governance baggage no other ocean pathway does. [Episode here.](https://soundcloud.com/morgan-reed-raven/season-3-episode-9-iron)

The substantive content is on iron. Nawaz says ExOIS, based at [WHOI](https://www.whoi.edu), has settled on the Northeast Pacific as its preferred trial site and is raising money for it; her contribution was a set of social-science and governance recommendations, followed by a push for a broader advisory board rather than relying on her single take. Her argument for starting community engagement now — "yesterday is the time" — is practical rather than ethical: coastal communities in Alaska won't allocate attention to a hypothetical, so a scheduled trial is itself the engagement trigger. The risk she singles out is nutrient robbing — iron-driven blooms in the Southern Ocean or Pacific draw down nitrogen and phosphorus that would otherwise upwell elsewhere, with impacts "a decade, 50 years down the line" on fisheries in Global South waters. For a buyer, that's a liability profile with a time horizon longer than any credit contract, and no clear party to compensate.

The second thing worth taking away is the betrayal effect. In scaled-up 2050 scenario exercises, participants who initially warmed to macroalgae bundling and sinking as "natural" flipped once they saw the footprint, and ended up more comfortable with an offshore direct air capture plus sub-seabed mineralization concept because it felt contained. Nawaz's broader point — views are formed in the conversation, not pre-existing to be surveyed — undercuts the standard social-license survey most developers commission.

The Haida case is told from the BC side. Alex frames it as an entrepreneur dumping 2,000 tons of iron (the host's figure; published accounts put it around 100 tonnes). Nawaz's account: the Haida band wanted salmon back, Russ George pitched carbon credits as the funding mechanism, and that credit link is what escalated a local restoration deal into a global geoengineering story. Her expert interviews found the fallout caused the German government to stop funding ocean alkalinity enhancement research — "one bad experience can really spoil the pot," since publics don't distinguish between things put in the ocean.

Context for CDR readers: [C]Worthy, the ocean-modelling group doing marine CDR measurement work, co-produced the explainer video Nawaz references, and she now sits in the loop between modellers, ExOIS, and coastal First Nations. Her publication list is on [her site](https://sara-nawaz.github.io/). She also says plainly that private-sector-led mCDR research is received worse by coastal publics than academic work — relevant to anyone whose MRV (monitoring, reporting and verification) case rests on company-run trials.

Useful for marine CDR developers scoping trials, buyers pricing long-tail ecological liability, and anyone writing community-engagement requirements into a protocol.
