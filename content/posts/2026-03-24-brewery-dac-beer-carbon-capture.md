<p>The CO₂ in your beer probably came from an ammonia plant. Or an ethanol refinery. Or — if you&rsquo;re drinking Almanac Beer Company&rsquo;s new &ldquo;Flow – Clean Air Edition&rdquo; — straight from the sky above a parking lot in Alameda, California.</p><p>Almanac just launched what appears to be the world&rsquo;s first commercial beer carbonated entirely with CO₂ captured from ambient air via direct air capture. Their partner, Berkeley-based <a href=https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/aircapture-and-almanac-beer-co-launch-worlds-first-commercial-beer-carbonated-with-co-captured-from-the-atmosphere-with-direct-air-capture-302722380.html>Aircapture</a>, installed a modular DAC unit in the brewery&rsquo;s parking lot — it looks like an oversized HVAC unit with a chimney — that pulls CO₂ from the air and liquefies it to beverage-grade purity (99.999%). The beer is already available at 800+ retailers across California, including Safeway, Whole Foods, Total Wine, and BevMo.</p><p>This is cute, sure. But the business logic is sharp.</p><h2 id=the-co-supply-chain-is-broken>The CO₂ supply chain is broken<a hidden class=anchor aria-hidden=true href=#the-co-supply-chain-is-broken>#</a></h2><p>In 2022, a <a href=https://www.nytimes.com/2026/03/23/climate/beer-carbon-capture.html>nationwide CO₂ shortage</a> hammered breweries across the country. Most commercial CO₂ is a byproduct of fossil fuel processes — ammonia production, ethanol refining. Ethanol plants alone supply about 35% of North American CO₂. But here&rsquo;s the twist: those same ethanol plants are now sequestering their CO₂ underground to claim tax credits (thanks, 45Q) instead of selling it to beverage companies. The incentive structure is literally pulling CO₂ out of the commercial supply chain.</p><p>So breweries face a supply problem that DAC can solve. Not as a climate gesture — as a supply chain fix.</p><h2 id=why-this-matters-beyond-beer>Why this matters beyond beer<a hidden class=anchor aria-hidden=true href=#why-this-matters-beyond-beer>#</a></h2><p>Aircapture isn&rsquo;t chasing DOE grants or carbon credit revenues. They&rsquo;re targeting the <a href=https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/aircapture-and-almanac-beer-co-launch-worlds-first-commercial-beer-carbonated-with-co-captured-from-the-atmosphere-with-direct-air-capture-302722380.html>$20 billion commercial CO₂ market</a> — food-grade, medical-grade, industrial-grade CO₂ where customers already pay real prices for reliable supply. Their scaling strategy: &ldquo;not by making it bigger, but by making more of it.&rdquo; Modular units. Deploy everywhere.</p><p>While the big DOE-funded DAC hubs stall under political headwinds and funding freezes, this modular approach sidesteps the whole mess. No federal funding needed. No carbon credits required. Just a product people already buy, made from a feedstock that&rsquo;s literally everywhere.</p><p>As Matthew Realff at Georgia Tech <a href=https://www.nytimes.com/2026/03/23/climate/beer-carbon-capture.html>told the NYT</a>: &ldquo;DAC creates the option to not only address current and future emissions but also historical additions.&rdquo;</p><p>A portion of Flow – CAE proceeds goes to <a href=https://carbon180.org/>Carbon180</a>, which is a nice touch. But the real story is that DAC just found a business model that works without anyone in Washington lifting a finger. The $20B commercial CO₂ market might be the beachhead that makes the whole industry viable.</p><p>Sometimes the path to gigatonne scale starts in a parking lot.</p>
