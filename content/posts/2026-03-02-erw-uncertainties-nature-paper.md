<p>A new paper in <em>Nature Reviews Earth & Environment</em> has mapped out the uncertainties that still plague <a href=/posts/what-is-enhanced-weathering/>enhanced rock weathering</a> (<a href=/posts/what-is-enhanced-weathering/>ERW</a>) as a carbon dioxide removal strategy. Meanwhile, Germany&rsquo;s Thünen Institute — a federal agricultural research body — has gone further, calling <a href=/posts/what-is-enhanced-weathering/>ERW</a> &ldquo;not yet a reliable climate protection measure.&rdquo;</p><p>This is getting attention, and it should. But let&rsquo;s read past the headlines.</p><h2 id=what-the-paper-actually-says>What the Paper Actually Says<a hidden class=anchor aria-hidden=true href=#what-the-paper-actually-says>#</a></h2><p>The Nature paper doesn&rsquo;t claim <a href=/posts/what-is-enhanced-weathering/>ERW</a> doesn&rsquo;t work. It catalogs the variables that make precise quantification difficult: soil type, mineral grain size, local climate, microbial activity, and leaching dynamics all influence how fast basalt dissolves and how much CO₂ is actually captured and stored.</p><p>In other words: <a href=/posts/what-is-enhanced-weathering/>ERW</a> works in principle, but the <em>how much</em> varies enormously depending on where and how you deploy it. That&rsquo;s a measurement challenge, not a fundamental flaw.</p><h2 id=the-thünen-perspective>The Thünen Perspective<a hidden class=anchor aria-hidden=true href=#the-thünen-perspective>#</a></h2><p>The German researchers are particularly cautious about over-promising. Their concern is that <a href=/posts/what-is-enhanced-weathering/>ERW</a> could enter carbon credit markets before measurement, reporting, and verification (MRV) methods are robust enough to guarantee what&rsquo;s being sold. That&rsquo;s a legitimate worry — one shared by serious people in the CDR space.</p><h2 id=where-cdis-research-fits>Where CDI&rsquo;s Research Fits<a hidden class=anchor aria-hidden=true href=#where-cdis-research-fits>#</a></h2><p>This is exactly the kind of uncertainty that Carbon Drawdown Initiative has been working to resolve. CDI&rsquo;s <a href=https://www.carbon-drawdown.de/blog/2025-12-3-lifetime-carbon-balance-of-enhanced-rock-weathering-explained-part-1>EW greenhouse experiment</a> includes a detailed four-part series on the lifetime carbon balance of <a href=/posts/what-is-enhanced-weathering/>ERW</a>, addressing the LCA (life-cycle assessment) methodology that separates real CDR from hopeful estimates.</p><p>CDI&rsquo;s ongoing <a href=https://carbondrawdown.substack.com/p/69-leachate-electrical-conductivity>leachate proxy series</a> is also tackling MRV head-on — testing whether electrical conductivity, pH, and other proxies can reliably track alkalinity changes at macro and micro scales. The findings are nuanced: EC tracks alkalinity well at aggregate level, but breaks down under specific conditions like fertilizer events.</p><h2 id=uncertain--useless>&ldquo;Uncertain&rdquo; ≠ &ldquo;Useless&rdquo;<a hidden class=anchor aria-hidden=true href=#uncertain--useless>#</a></h2><p>Every CDR pathway started uncertain. Direct air capture was once a lab curiosity. Biochar permanence was debated for a decade. The question isn&rsquo;t whether <a href=/posts/what-is-enhanced-weathering/>ERW</a> works — it&rsquo;s whether we can measure it precisely enough to build credible markets around it.</p><p>The Nature paper is a map of the work still needed.
