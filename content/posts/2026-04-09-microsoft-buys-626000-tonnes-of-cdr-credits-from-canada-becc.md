Microsoft's latest move, securing 626,000 tonnes of durable carbon dioxide removal (CDR) credits over 15 years from the North Star bioenergy with carbon capture and storage (BECCS) project in Saskatchewan, Canada, is a noteworthy development in the nascent CDR market. This deal, struck with North Star Carbon Solutions LP—a partnership between the Meadow Lake Tribal Council (MLTC) and Svante Technologies—marks Microsoft's first Canadian BECCS CDR offtake and, critically, the first such deal in Canada to involve Indigenous ownership.

The project itself is a fascinating blend of renewable energy and carbon removal. It will be co-located at the MLTC Bioenergy Centre, a facility already generating power from waste biomass from an adjacent sawmill. This integration is smart, leveraging existing infrastructure and a sustainable biomass feedstock. Once fully operational, planned for early 2029, the carbon capture plant aims to generate up to 90,000 tonnes of CDR credits annually, with captured CO2 slated for permanent geological storage. Svante is providing initial funding until a construction decision, which is a common model for de-risking early-stage, capital-intensive projects.

From a CDR analyst's perspective, several aspects stand out. Firstly, the sheer volume, 626,000 tonnes over 15 years, is substantial, providing a significant anchor for the North Star project. This long-term commitment from a major corporate buyer like Microsoft is precisely what early-stage CDR projects need to secure financing and move from concept to commercial operation. Phillip Goodman, Microsoft's director of carbon removal portfolio, rightly emphasizes the need to scale solutions with "durable storage" and "rigorous monitoring and verification," which BECCS, with geological sequestration, is designed to provide.

Secondly, the Indigenous ownership component via the Meadow Lake Tribal Council is a powerful and positive precedent. Jeremy Norman, tribal chief of MLTC, highlighted the project's alignment with MLTC Nations' commitment to land stewardship and sustainable forest management, and its potential to create "meaningful employment and economic opportunities." This model of equitable partnership, where local communities, particularly Indigenous groups with deep ties to the land, are not just stakeholders but owners, could be a blueprint for large-scale CDR projects globally. It addresses concerns around land use, ensures local benefits, and can help build social license, which is often a significant hurdle for infrastructure projects. The article notes around 50 jobs during development and construction, and up to 10 permanent roles, which, while not massive, represents tangible local economic activity.

Finally, this deal underscores the growing maturity of the BECCS pathway. While direct air capture (DAC) often grabs headlines, BECCS offers a compelling alternative, particularly when integrated with sustainable biomass sources and robust carbon capture technology like Svante’s. It demonstrates a clear market signal for the readiness of Canadian BECCS projects and the appetite from corporate buyers for diverse, high-quality CDR solutions. The development timeline to early 2029 is ambitious but achievable, and the success of projects like North Star will be crucial in demonstrating the scalability and economic viability of BECCS as a vital component of the global CDR portfolio.
