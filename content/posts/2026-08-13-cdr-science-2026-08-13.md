Three new preprints landed on CDRXIV that, taken together, sketch out something the ERW field has been hungry for: field-scale, direct-measurement evidence of what basalt actually does when you put it on real working farms — and a lab technique that could make the porewater side of MRV a lot less hostage to the weather. That's why this cluster caught my eye.

The context matters. A [recent 3-year field trial reported in ES&T](https://cen.acs.org/environment/climate-change/enhanced-rock-weathering-co2-sequestration-climate-change/103/web/2025/12) found CO₂ removal rates less than 10% of modeled expectations, and a [Frontiers in Climate paper](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/climate/articles/10.3389/fclim.2025.1657058/full) reported basalt showing no detectable signal in a Midwestern trial while steel slag did. The [Nature Reviews Earth & Environment overview](https://www.nature.com/articles/s43017-025-00713-7) and [Project Drawdown's own assessment](https://drawdown.org/explorer/deploy-enhanced-rock-weathering) both flag MRV complexity and unpredictable field effectiveness as the central bottlenecks. So every honest field dataset — positive, null, or messy — is valuable right now.

Kirstine Skov and colleagues at UNDO (with collaborators at the James Hutton Institute and Newcastle) tackle a very specific pain point in the [SAT-C paper](https://cdrxiv.org/preprint/496): in-field rhizon sampling of soil porewater depends on soils being wet enough to yield fluid, which in dry summers or coarse soils simply fails. Their SATuration-Centrifugation approach saturates a defined soil core with deionized water and spins it, giving a porewater sample independent of ambient moisture. They report strong concordance with rhizon-derived concentrations of key elements across two grassland ERW trials, and — importantly — charge-balance-inferred bicarbonate tracks measured alkalinity. If that holds up across more soil types, it's a real unlock: consistent porewater chemistry regardless of when you sample.

The two Yale-led preprints from Quinn Zacharias and colleagues at [Sleepers River Research Watershed in Vermont](https://cdrxiv.org/preprint/461) do something the field has needed more of — long-baseline, catchment-scale monitoring on a working dairy. They applied 20 t/ha of finely milled, calcium-rich meta-basalt to pastures and hayfields, established a full year of pre-treatment baseline, then tracked topsoil chemistry for 13 months. Soil pH rose 0.15–0.24 units (p < 0.05), exchangeable calcium climbed as much as 12%, and — the detail I found most interesting — pH gains scaled inversely with initial pH, largest where soils were most acidic. K and Mg responses were modest, which they attribute to the slow dissolution of metamorphic K/Mg-bearing minerals. It's a clean "slow-release liming agent" story in a well-buffered temperate soil.

The [companion trace-metal paper](https://cdrxiv.org/preprint/463) is arguably the more consequential one for anyone worried about ERW risk. Extractable Ni, Cr, Pb, Al, Mn, Zn, Cu, and Cd did not increase in treated fields versus controls; forage yields and tissue metal concentrations were unchanged after one year. Downslope riparian soils actually showed 60–80% declines in extractable Ni, Al, and Pb, coincident with a transient rise in extractable Ca:Al — consistent with basalt-derived alkalinity reducing metal lability where weathering products accumulate. They propose pH and Ca:Al as practical field indicators for evolving metal availability. Given that Fe–Al-rich basalts sometimes raise eyebrows on trace-metal grounds, a clean one-year signal at agronomic rate is worth noting.

On companies: SAT-C is UNDO's own methodological contribution, and it speaks directly to the porewater-based MRV stacks that operators like [Lithos](https://www.lithoscarbon.com/), [InPlanet](https://inplanet.earth/), [Alt Carbon](https://altcarbon.com/), and [Terradot](https://terradot.earth/) all have to grapple with in one form or another. The Vermont work isn't tied to a specific developer, but its findings — slow-release liming behavior, catena-position effects, riparian metal stabilization — bear on basically every basalt-on-pasture deployment.

What I'll watch next: whether SAT-C gets picked up by independent labs and whether charge-balance bicarbonate holds across more mineralogies; whether the Yale team's Sleepers River monitoring eventually resolves an alkalinity export signal at the catchment outlet (that's the CDR question the fertility and metals papers don't yet answer); and whether pH-scaled response and Ca:Al ratios become standard reporting fields in MRV protocols. Small preprints, but the direction of travel is toward the kind of grounded, direct-measurement evidence this pathway needs.
