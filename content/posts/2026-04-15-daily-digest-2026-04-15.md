The CDR industry is splitting into two tracks: one where permits and technology keep advancing, the other where the money hasn't caught up. That tension defined today's news cycle. Kansas just got its first Class VI CO2 storage permit. ZEN Carbon moved into live industrial deployment at a concrete plant. ETH Zurich published work on a living building material that captures CO2 and self-repairs. The engineering side of CDR is not waiting around.

But the financing side tells a different story. As Captain's Log #105 put it bluntly: the permits keep coming, but the checks have stopped. Voluntary buyers are pulling back. The gap between technical readiness and commercial demand is widening, not narrowing.

The question for the rest of 2026 is whether new market structures can close that gap before momentum stalls.

## Concrete is becoming CDR's quiet workhorse

Three separate stories today pointed at the same material: concrete. CarbonCure won the 2026 Climate Technology Company of the Year award. ZEN Carbon announced its first live deployment at a concrete plant. And Captain's Log #104 made the case that mineralization in concrete is CDR's "escape hatch," a pathway that embeds removal into something people already buy.

Cella and TotalEnergies also announced a partnership targeting CO2 mineralization at industrial sites. When a fossil major shows up in a CDR story, it's worth naming the constraint: CDR is for residual emissions that can't be eliminated, not a reason to delay phasing out fossil fuels. That said, the mineralization pathway is interesting precisely because it locks CO2 into solid form permanently, and it piggybacks on existing industrial supply chains.

## Policy signals from Europe are getting louder

Germany now estimates it needs up to 51 million tonnes of CO2 removal per year by 2045. Delays risk missing that target entirely. A separate analysis found that if CDR joins the EU carbon market, the bloc could reach 60 million tonnes of removals by 2050. Denmark is running its first certified enhanced rock weathering (ERW) field pilots, backed by €1.6 million for Rock Flour Company. And a new paper lays out what a short-term EU purchasing programme for permanent CDR would need to look like to actually work.

Meanwhile, Carbon180 published the first social and environmental guardrails specifically designed for CDR projects. This matters because as the industry scales, the communities hosting these projects need clear protections, not just promises.

Lessons from Denmark's experience offer a reality check for the UK, which is still figuring out its own CDR procurement strategy. The takeaway: early movers learn things that latecomers pay for.

## New financial plumbing is being installed

ClimeFi structured the first publicly announced transaction for CRCF (Carbon Removal Certification Framework) carbon removal units. That's the EU's new standard. First transactions under new standards are small, but they set precedents that shape everything after.

JPMorganChase bought more than 85,000 tonnes of CDR credits using a dynamic baseline approach. Altitude is scaling biochar credit purchases to unclog supply bottlenecks. A €300 million fund is tying manager compensation to biodiversity outcomes, which signals that co-benefits are becoming bankable, not just nice-to-have.

Verra launched a buyer tool to check whether carbon credits meet compliance eligibility. And a new study found that REDD+ credits overstated avoided deforestation by a factor of 10.7. That finding doesn't directly involve CDR, but it shapes the credibility environment that all carbon credits operate in. Buyers burned by overcredited avoidance projects become harder to convert into removal buyers.

Captain's Log #102 framed the co-benefits angle sharply: when your biggest customer walks away, co-benefits become a lifeline. Log #103 went further, arguing that a post-patronage CDR stack, one not dependent on a handful of big tech buyers, is already being built.

## What's next

Watch the EU's next moves on integrating CDR into the carbon market. The 60 Mt by 2050 number only works if permanent removal gets a compliance-grade price signal, and that decision hasn't been made yet. Closer to home, watch whether JPMorganChase's 85,000-tonne purchase triggers other financial institutions to follow. Banks buying CDR credits at scale would change the demand picture faster than any policy.
