<p>An Italian infrastructure group has earmarked €1 million to bolster its internal carbon removal unit, as disclosed in its 2025 integrated annual report. While the article from Carbon Pulse doesn&rsquo;t name the specific group, the allocation itself, despite being a relatively modest sum in the broader climate finance landscape, offers a telling signal for the CDR sector. This isn&rsquo;t an external purchase of credits on the voluntary carbon market (VCM); it&rsquo;s a direct, internal investment into developing or scaling proprietary removal capabilities.</p><p>This move underscores a growing trend where companies are not just looking to offset emissions but are actively seeking to build out their own removal infrastructure or capacity. The article categorises this under &ldquo;Engineered Removals&rdquo; and &ldquo;Nature-based Carbon (Forestry),&rdquo; suggesting a potential hybrid approach or at least a broad consideration of different removal pathways within the group&rsquo;s strategy. For a CDR-focused audience, the distinction between an internal allocation and a VCM purchase is critical. An internal unit represents a higher degree of commitment and a more hands-on approach to carbon management, potentially driven by a desire for greater control over methodology, permanence, and additionality.</p><p>The €1 million, while perhaps a seed fund, could be directed towards R&amp;D, pilot projects, or even early-stage deployment of specific technologies like direct air capture (DAC), bioenergy with carbon capture and storage (BECCS), <a href=/posts/what-is-enhanced-weathering/>enhanced rock weathering</a>, or advanced afforestation/reforestation projects with robust monitoring, reporting, and verification (MRV). It signals that the infrastructure group sees value not just in the outcome of carbon removal, but in the <em>process</em> and <em>ownership</em> of that process. This could be a strategic play to mitigate future carbon liabilities, enhance corporate reputation, or even explore new revenue streams from carbon removal services or intellectual property.</p><p>What this implies for the wider CDR field is a gradual shift towards more direct corporate engagement. While the VCM remains vital for liquidity and standardisation, we might see more large industrial players, especially those with significant emissions profiles or land assets, begin to integrate CDR into their core business operations. This internalisation could lead to more stable, long-term demand for CDR technologies and services, as companies move beyond purely transactional credit purchases to strategic asset development. Such internal investments could also de-risk nascent CDR technologies, providing initial capital and real-world operational experience that external investors often demand before committing larger sums. It&rsquo;s a pragmatic step, reflecting a deeper understanding that robust climate action often necessitates direct investment in removal capacity, not just avoidance or reduction.</p>
