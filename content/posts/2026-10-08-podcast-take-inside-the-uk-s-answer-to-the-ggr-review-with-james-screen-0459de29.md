> Take on a podcast episode from **The CDR Policy Scoop**, originally published Tue, 06 Oc.
> Listen: <https://shows.acast.com/the-cdr-policy-scoop/episodes/the-uk-governments-answer-to-the-ggr-review-with-james-scree>

**TL;DR**

- UK government's GGR review response is a "staging post," not a strategy; hard questions deferred to the Carbon Budget 7 delivery plan (expected 2026).
- Geological net zero rejected mainly for political reasons: no appetite to reopen what net zero means. Candid, and worth knowing.
- UK ETS removals integration slips from "end of 2028" to "as soon as practicable" — Screen says don't over-read it, but UK-EU linking is a live variable.
- Carbon contracts for difference (CCfDs) can serve both voluntary and ETS sales, but the taxpayer subsidy implied doesn't scale. Honest framing.
- GGR business model still has no financial close; the transport & storage network model does (Liverpool Bay).

[The CDR Policy Scoop](https://shows.acast.com/the-cdr-policy-scoop/episodes/the-uk-governments-answer-to-the-ggr-review-with-james-scree) hosts Eve Tamme and Sebastian Manhart interview James Screen, who until recently ran the greenhouse gas removals (GGR) team at the UK Department for Energy Security and Net Zero (DESNZ). Recorded 29 September, the episode dissects the government's response to Alan Whitehead's independent GGR review — what was accepted, what was parked, and why — from someone who drafted it and can now speak personally.

The most useful content is Screen's explanation of the mechanics behind the UK's two-layer support stack. The transport and storage network (pipes and stores) gets its own government-backed "T&I model," which has already proven bankable: Liverpool Bay reached financial close. GGR business models — the CCfDs — layer on top for individual capture projects. That second layer is not yet proven bankable, and Screen is specific about why it's harder than offshore wind CfDs: the reference price. A removal project might sell into the voluntary market at one price and the UK ETS at a far lower one, so the government needs protections against a developer arbitraging between the two while it tops up the gap. He's confident the model works for both markets, but adds the line practitioners should sit with: "at some point, that gets to a scale that looks quite difficult for taxpayer to swallow." CCfDs are a bridge for early projects into the early 2030s, not the enduring demand mechanism — and the review's proposed link between aviation emissions and GGR demand, which would be one such mechanism, was not accepted as written.

On technology scope, Screen confirms the UK play is CCUS-enabled removals — bioenergy with carbon capture and storage (BECCS) and direct air capture with storage (DACCS) — and explains the rationale as a confidence-on-permanence judgment plus policy lag: neither the UK nor EU can yet say enhanced rock weathering or ocean alkalinity enhancement science is "translatable into firm policy commitments." Hints of broadening are real but modest: the British Standards Institution tasked with a biochar methodology, non-pipeline transport (truck and rail) pilots to open up biomethane facilities producing cheap CO2, and the carbon management innovation challenges for non-CCUS pathways crossing the valley of death. The hosts split on whether this is a dangerously narrow bet (Manhart) or a broad family under one label spanning cement, waste-to-energy, and pulp and paper (Tamme) — a worthwhile two-minute exchange.

Two softer signals worth noting. Screen personally sees woodland fitting in the UK ETS with longevity "mechanisms" to manage it — expect that view to be contested by anyone holding a durability line. And on the post-response reshuffle (Miatta Fahnbulleh replacing Ed Miliband), he expects continuity but names the headwind clearly: the UK case for net zero is increasingly made on energy security and bills, and most CDR can't make that argument.

For context, the Scoop covered the Whitehead review itself roughly a year earlier and the original 2028 UK ETS integration plan before that; both are findable on the [show page](https://shows.acast.com/the-cdr-policy-scoop). The UK-EU ETS linking discussions referenced here are the thing to watch if you're modelling UK removals demand timelines.

Useful for anyone structuring BECCS or DACCS project finance in the UK clusters, or tracking how a CCfD-plus-ETS model might translate to the EU debate.
