Grégory Pierre Mialdea, an agronomist at the Carbon Drawdown Initiative, spent this month asking a boring question with expensive consequences: can you measure alkalinity in leachate water without shipping it to a wet-chemistry lab and titrating it by hand?

That is the whole of [Part 4 of the XXL Lysimeter Experiment](https://www.linkedin.com/feed/update/urn:li:share:7496189729466572801/) - a working note from Mialdea's team about whether cheaper proxies can stand in for slow benchtop chemistry. It sounds like a lab-methods post. It is actually a cost-of-goods post for enhanced rock weathering (ERW), the CDR pathway that spreads crushed silicate rock on farmland to pull CO2 out of the air.

**The arc.** Mialdea works on what the Carbon Drawdown Initiative calls XXL lysimeters - oversized instrumented soil columns that catch every drop of drainage water leaving a plot of rock-amended soil. The setup exists because ERW's carbon claim lives or dies on one number: how much dissolved bicarbonate actually leaves the root zone. Everything else - application rate, rock chemistry, satellite maps of fields - is upstream of that measurement. Mialdea's job is to run the columns and read the drainage.

**The work, in his team's words.** "Alkalinity is the number we actually care about, and it's slow and expensive: pump the leachate, ship it to a lab, titrate," the Carbon Drawdown Initiative team [wrote on LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/feed/update/urn:li:share:7496189729466572801/). Part 4, they said, "asks a more practical question: can we get to the number faster and cheaper?" That is the entire economic frame. Every ERW verification tonne today carries a measurement pathway that does not obviously scale to millions of hectares. Somebody has to figure out whether an electrical-conductivity probe, an ion-selective sensor, or a model-plus-spot-check can carry the load.

**The lens.** What makes Mialdea interesting for CDR market watchers is that he sits at the exact point where field science becomes unit economics. Consider the scale gap. Terradot just announced it has [deployed 1,000,000 tonnes of rock across 60,000 hectares](https://www.linkedin.com/feed/update/urn:li:ugcPost:7497691090175647744/). The [Rock Flour Company is scaling glacial flour ERW in Nuuk](https://www.linkedin.com/feed/update/urn:li:ugcPost:7497643557348884481/). New modeling work like [ForestERW](https://doi.org/10.3389/fclim.2026.1855001) keeps enlarging the paper-addressable market for US forests. All of that expansion runs on models that were calibrated against a small handful of instrumented field sites producing real drainage-water chemistry. Mialdea runs one of those sites. That does not make him the person who sets the ERW price. It does make him one of a small number of operators whose data gets folded back into the assumptions everyone else is quoting.

Compare that to what a durable-CDR seller can already claim. Charm Industrial has been [publicly emphasising 175 measurement types per tonne](https://www.linkedin.com/feed/update/urn:li:share:7496052087542300672/) as part of its product. ERW cannot make that pitch yet, because per-tonne verification still leans on titrated leachate samples. The measurement is real. It is just artisanal. As I put it [in a recent post about Mialdea's setup](https://bsky.app/profile/captaindrawdown.bsky.social/post/3mtv2dxc7fk2g), the XXL lysimeter is "the closest thing ERW has to a ground-truth" number for alkalinity flux. That is a strong claim about physical measurement. It is not a claim that one lab in Germany decides what a tonne costs.

**Where he is heading.** The bet embedded in Part 4 is that a defensible proxy exists and can be validated against titration in the same soil columns. If the answer is yes, ERW developers get a path to continuous or semi-continuous monitoring, and registries have something concrete to consider when they next revise protocols. If the answer is no, verification stays a slow line item, and the gap between deployment tonnage and confirmed tonnage keeps widening. Neither outcome is a price forecast. Both change what the sector's cost curve can plausibly look like a few years out.

**Why the voice matters.** Most of the CDR conversation about ERW right now is either megatonne announcements or model papers. Mialdea's contribution is the third leg: someone patiently reporting what the drainage water actually says, and asking out loud whether the measurement itself can be made cheaper. The people writing MRV protocols read those notes. The buyers eventually will too.

For readers who want the pathway basics before following his work, I keep a primer on enhanced weathering up to date. The open question I am watching: which ERW developer is first to publish a side-by-side of proxy sensors against titrated leachate, at field scale, with the raw data attached. That is the handoff worth waiting for.
