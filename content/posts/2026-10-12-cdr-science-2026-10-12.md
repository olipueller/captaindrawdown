Three code and data archives landed on Zenodo over five weeks this autumn. None contains a finished paper, and the headline numbers aren't in the excerpts I have. Read together, though, they show where ocean alkalinity enhancement (OAE) research is heading. Two groups are refining global maps of how much CO2 an alkalinity addition actually pulls from the air. A third is asking how much of that removal anyone could confirm from observations alone. That pairing of "how efficient" with "how provable" caught my eye.

## The three releases

- **[Zhou, Timmermans, Yankovsky, Tyka and Long](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.22669221)**, published 9 September 2026. This is code for a manuscript titled "Global intercomparison of ocean-based geochemical carbon dioxide removal efficiencies." Affiliations span Yale, Google and [C]Worthy. The package holds notebooks to reproduce every figure, plus a conda environment named `mcdr-atlas`. The analysis data are archived separately. As the title says, this compares global efficiency estimates rather than producing one more.

- **[Xie, Spence, Corney and Bach](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23073669)**, published 30 September 2026. This is figure source data for "Global assessment of marine CO2 removal efficiency in the coastal ocean," from a University of Tasmania–led team. It focuses on coastal waters and uses particle tracking ("absorber trajectories") to compare absorber-based efficiency estimates with conventional ocean-model estimates. It maps global coastal mCDR efficiency, its spatial and seasonal variability, and the dominant controls, and tests sensitivity to absorber parameters and particle counts.

- **[Xu, Sun and Lin](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23265970)**, published 10 October 2026. This is the reproducibility package for a manuscript submitted to *Nature Communications*, from Xiamen University and China's Third Institute of Oceanography. It asks for the **minimum OAE removal that is verifiable from observations alone**, as a function of the declared verification domain. It then asks how many independent site-years are needed to reach that minimum. Inputs include SOCAT v2026.

## Where this sits

The broad case for OAE is well established. The [Chemical Reviews overview](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/10.1021/acs.chemrev.5c00433) traces decades of foundational chemistry and notes the possible local benefit against acidification. The open question is measurement. The digital report covered by [WHOI](https://www.whoi.edu/press-room/news-release/mcdrreport/) and [Columbia's State of the Planet](https://news.climate.columbia.edu/2026/09/21/new-report-ocean-carbon-removal-shows-promise-but-big-questions-remain/) in September 2026 named it as a central uncertainty: whether removal "can be reliably measured and verified."

Efficiency maps are the field's current answer. Because the extra CO2 uptake is too diffuse to measure directly, projects lean on models to estimate how much of the added alkalinity converts to atmospheric drawdown, and how fast. The Zhou and Xie archives extend that work in two directions. One compares existing global products against each other. The other zooms into the coast, where most real deployments happen, using a particle-based framing.

## What feels genuinely new

The Xu, Sun and Lin framing is the piece I find most interesting. Most verification discussion asks how good the model is. Their framing asks how big a signal must be before observations, with no model, can see it. And it asks how many site-years of deployment that takes.

Making the answer depend on the "declared verification domain" matters. The detection threshold depends on the box you draw around the deployment. That turns a vague worry ("can we ever verify this?") into a design variable a project or registry could actually choose.

I can't report their thresholds or site-year counts, because the excerpt doesn't include them. The two-tier reproduction setup is notably careful, though. An offline tier recomputes every manuscript number from frozen outputs. A second tier rebuilds those outputs from third-party archives, with every input pinned by version, DOI and SHA-256 hash.

## How the field is framing it

A recent [ScienceDirect review of reporting, verification and accounting frameworks](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2211715626008349) puts the principle plainly: "the physical response must be measured using observations appropriate to the storage reservoir and removal mechanism." The observational detection-limit approach speaks directly to that standard. The efficiency maps supply the model side that any credible accounting will still need.

## What it touches

This bears on any company crediting OAE, such as [Planetary](https://www.planetarytech.com/). Their removal claims rest on some combination of modeled efficiency and field measurement. If coastal efficiency varies strongly by place and season, siting decisions carry more weight. If observational verification requires many site-years at a given scale, single short pilots may struggle to stand alone as proof. I'm drawing that inference from the stated scope of these analyses, not from results specific to any company.

## What I'll watch next

First, the manuscripts themselves. I want the Xu et al. detection thresholds and site-year counts. I also want to see whether the Zhou and Xie efficiency maps agree on the coastal zone, and where they disagree. The real payoff would be putting the two lines of work side by side: overlay where efficiency is high with where removal is observationally detectable, and see how much of the coastal ocean falls in both.
