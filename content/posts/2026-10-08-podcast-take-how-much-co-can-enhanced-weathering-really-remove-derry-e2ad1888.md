> Take on a podcast episode from **Reviewer 2 does geoengineering**, originally published Mon, 28 Se.
> Listen: <https://podcasters.spotify.com/pod/show/reviewer2geoengineering/episodes/How-Much-CO-Can-Enhanced-Weathering-Really-Remove----Derry-e3pgntl>

**TL;DR**

- Derry's whole-system argument: shallow-soil cation depletion (top 10–30 cm) measures the first step, not alkalinity export. Direct challenge to current ERW measurement, reporting, and verification (MRV).
- Three sinks consume alkalinity downstream: ion exchange in acidic soils, secondary clay precipitation (generates acidity), carbonate precipitation. "Not delayed, it's been consumed."
- Water budget check: ~250 mm/yr runoff in US corn belt would need 25–30 mmol/L alkalinity to move 5 t CO₂/ha/yr; real streams run 1–3. Useful framing.
- Natural basalt catchments max out near 1 t CO₂/ha/yr (wet tropics), typically ~0.2. Global basalt weathering ~0.1–0.15 Gt CO₂/yr. First time I've seen this benchmark set against ERW claims.
- Host tone is snarky; guest is measured and explicitly open to rebuttal. Take the substance, discount the wrapper.

Andrew of *Reviewer 2 does geoengineering* hosts Louis Derry, Cornell geochemist (30 years, also affiliated with IPGP Paris), on a [new Nature paper](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-10936-3) with Kate Maher and Oliver Chadwick arguing that critical-zone processes cap alkalinity export from basaltic systems. The [episode](https://podcasters.spotify.com/pod/show/reviewer2geoengineering/episodes/How-Much-CO-Can-Enhanced-Weathering-Really-Remove----Derry-e3pgntl) is essentially a mechanistic case for why enhanced rock weathering (ERW) removal claims of 5–10 t CO₂/ha/yr are physically implausible in row-crop agriculture.

The load-bearing claim is that initial dissolution in tilled topsoil is not the rate-limiting step. Derry walks through what happens to alkalinity moving down the profile: ion exchange on acidic soils swaps Ca/Mg for H⁺ and turns bicarbonate back into CO₂; smectite clays like saponite strip Ca and Mg directly, and even Ca-free clays like kaolinite generate acidity on precipitation; carbonate precipitation takes a further cut. He cites the Landscape Evolution Observatory at Biosphere 2 (Jon Chorover's group), where poorly crystalline clay precursors form on week-scale transport times — so this isn't a thousand-year problem. His pointed reframe for practitioners: when field trials show cations gone from the topsoil but nothing in the drainage, "it's not delayed, it's been consumed, and it's not coming back until you re-acidify the system."

The second argument is a back-of-envelope hydrology constraint that any buyer can check. Upper Mississippi basin runoff is ~250 mm/yr. To export 5 t CO₂/ha/yr through that water column you'd need roughly 25–30 mmol/L alkalinity; real basaltic streams sit at 1–3 mmol/L, and you'd precipitate minerals long before reaching the higher figure. Derry says a company presented a 5 t/ha/yr North Carolina field result at Goldschmidt in Montreal that fails this test. Against that, the natural analogs: Philippines, wet-side Hawaii, Réunion, Guadeloupe, even freshly erupted Pinatubo deliver ~0.2 t CO₂/ha/yr typically, ~1 at the ceiling. Basalt covers ~6% of land and still only weathers ~0.1–0.15 Gt CO₂/yr globally; doubling that would be a real achievement and still modest. He concedes the obvious rebuttal — fields with crushed rock aren't 100%-basalt watersheds — but says nobody has articulated why the same physical chemistry and hydrology wouldn't apply.

Context: this lands on top of Tyler Kukla's work (discussed on the same show) questioning whether grinding finer buys as much as early models assumed, and Maher's recent paper on the time- and depth-dependence of soil buffering. Derry says he's engaged with [Isometric](https://isometric.com) and smaller developers and senses "talking past each other" — the ERW literature largely doesn't cite the reactive-transport hydrology work his field has built over 15 years. He also notes the sector's visible pivot toward co-benefits, and flags a tension: agronomically optimal basalt doses are much lower than the doses needed for headline CDR numbers. Worth noting he was a commenter on Kantola et al. (2023, *Global Biogeochemical Cycles*), the first comment he's ever written on another paper.

Useful for anyone writing or buying against ERW protocols that credit on topsoil cation loss, and for MRV teams who need a defensible answer to the runoff-budget question before a buyer asks it.
