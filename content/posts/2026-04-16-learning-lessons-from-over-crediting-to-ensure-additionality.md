First-generation REDD+ projects, the flagship forest conservation credits traded on the voluntary carbon market, claimed 10.7 times more avoided deforestation than independent evaluations actually support. That's not a rounding error. It's an order-of-magnitude gap between what was sold and what was delivered, and a new synthesis published in *Nature Communications* pins the blame squarely on how projects chose their comparison areas and models, not on the underlying forest data itself.
## Why it matters
REDD+ credits (Reducing Emissions from Deforestation and Degradation) have been one of the most widely purchased offset types in the voluntary carbon market. Buyers, from airlines to tech companies, have relied on them to claim climate neutrality. If the credits represent only a tenth of the deforestation reduction they promise, that means a huge volume of emissions were never actually offset. This paper puts hard numbers on a problem that has been suspected for years and identifies the specific mechanisms that allowed it to happen.
## The details
A team led by Tom Swinfield and Andrew Balmford at the University of Cambridge, along with researchers including Thales West and Julia Jones, combined six independent ex post (after-the-fact) evaluations covering 44 REDD+ projects. The key finding: most of these projects did reduce deforestation to some degree. They weren't total failures. But the credits they issued vastly overstated the benefit.
The aggregate over-crediting factor was 10.7x. In plain terms, for every ton of avoided deforestation claimed and sold as a credit, only about one-tenth of a ton was actually achieved according to independent analysis.
The critical question the paper answers is *why*. Two possible explanations exist. First, maybe the satellite forest cover datasets used by projects were wrong, leading to inflated deforestation numbers. Second, maybe the projects picked comparison areas and modeling approaches that made deforestation look worse than it really was in the absence of the project, a problem known as selection bias in counterfactual baselines.
The paper finds the second explanation is the dominant driver. The choice of forest cover data was not the main issue. Instead, projects selected control areas that had higher deforestation rates than what the project area would have experienced without intervention. This made the "avoided" deforestation look much larger than it actually was. The modeling approaches used to project future deforestation compounded the problem.
This is a structural issue, not a data quality issue. It means better satellite imagery alone won't fix the problem.
## Implications
The paper's conclusions point in a clear direction: ex post certification against credible counterfactuals is necessary if carbon markets are going to represent real, causal reductions in deforestation.
What does that mean in practice? Right now, many crediting methodologies allow project developers to set their own baselines, essentially predicting how much forest would have been lost without their intervention. The paper argues that recent reforms, like transferring assessment to independent third parties and restricting methodological flexibility, are "critical but insufficient." The fundamental fix requires evaluating projects after the fact using rigorous counterfactual methods, comparing what happened in the project area to what happened in genuinely comparable areas that didn't receive intervention.
This matters for several groups. Credit buyers need to understand that a large share of historical REDD+ credits likely don't represent the climate benefit printed on the label. Registries and standard-setters like Verra (which certifies most REDD+ credits) face pressure to adopt the kind of ex post evaluation methods the paper describes. And project developers who are doing legitimate conservation work need better frameworks so their real impact can be properly valued rather than lumped in with inflated claims.
For CDR specifically, the lessons are transferable. Any removal or avoidance credit that depends on a counterfactual baseline, what would have happened without the project, faces the same risk of selection bias. Enhanced rock weathering field trials, soil carbon projects, and biochar credits all involve baseline assumptions that could be gamed or inadvertently inflated. The REDD+ experience is a cautionary tale that the CDR field should study carefully as it builds its own measurement, reporting, and verification (MRV) infrastructure.
## Caveats
A few things this paper does not show. It does not say REDD+ projects are worthless. Most of the 44 projects studied did reduce deforestation. The problem is the magnitude of the claims, not the direction. Forest conservation remains critically important for both climate and biodiversity.
The paper is also a synthesis of six existing evaluations, not a single unified analysis with one methodology applied consistently across all projects. Different evaluations used different approaches, though the convergence of their findings strengthens the overall conclusion.
Finally, these are first-generation projects. Methodologies have already begun to tighten. Whether second-generation projects and reformed standards will close the 10.7x gap remains an open and urgent question. The paper's authors clearly believe current reforms don't go far enough, and the data backs them up.
