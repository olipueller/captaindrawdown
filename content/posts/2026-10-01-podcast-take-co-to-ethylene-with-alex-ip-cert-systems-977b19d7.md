> Take on a podcast episode from **Bite-Size Climate Tech**, originally published Tue, 29 Se.
> Listen: <https://podcasters.spotify.com/pod/show/lydiaclinzi/episodes/CO-to-Ethylene-with-Alex-Ip--CERT-Systems-e3piese>

**TL;DR**

- CERT Systems makes ethylene from CO₂, water and electricity via direct electrolysis; drop-in identical to fossil ethylene. This is utilization, not removal — keep that straight.
- Market framing: ~200 Mt ethylene/yr, ~250 Mt CO₂ emitted making it. Useful order-of-magnitude anchor.
- Scale today: a direct-air-capture-to-ethylene pilot at ~1 kg CO₂/day. Target: tens of tons of ethylene/yr "in the next couple of years." That's ~2–3 orders of magnitude.
- Cost: competitive with bio-based polyethylene, still a premium over fossil. Parity thesis rests on refinery integration (O₂, H₂, heat) and cheaper electricity. Plausible, unproven.
- Tencent CarbonX award funds the commercial demo and pushes CO₂-based materials into consumer products via CarbonXMade. Notable: Chinese corporate capital, not Western buyers.

Lydia Clinzi's Bite-Size Climate Tech hosts Alex Ip, co-founder and CEO of Toronto-based [CERT Systems](https://co2cert.com/), in a roughly 25-minute Season 5 conversation ("Climate Tech Is Just Good Business"). The [episode](https://podcasters.spotify.com/pod/show/lydiaclinzi/episodes/CO-to-Ethylene-with-Alex-Ip--CERT-Systems-e3piese) covers the electrolyzer mechanism (the "sandwich" of catalyst-coated electrodes stacked into a reactor), the drop-in argument, current scale, the cost path, and what the CarbonX win unlocks. It is pitched at a general audience, with an eight-year-old explainer segment.

The first thing worth knowing is what this is not. The host gestures at a "carbon sequestration type of element," but CERT's product goes into polyethylene packaging, polyester and aviation fuel. Carbon in a shampoo bottle is stored until the bottle is incinerated; carbon in jet fuel is re-emitted on the first flight. This is fossil-feedstock substitution — avoidance accounting — and no serious durable-removal buyer standard counts it as removal. Ip, to his credit, doesn't claim otherwise; he sells it as decarbonizing a hard-to-abate chemical sector. The relevance for removal practitioners is upstream: CERT is a potential offtaker for captured CO₂, including from direct air capture, and offtake demand is one of the things the DAC pipeline is short on.

The second is the scale and cost picture, stated plainly. Last year's air-to-ethylene pilot ran at roughly a kilogram of CO₂ per day; Ip reports performance held or improved versus lab scale. The near-term target is tens of tons of ethylene per year via replicated modules rather than a new reactor design — the standard electrolyzer scaling argument. On cost, the quote that matters: "There is currently a premium over the fossil-based ones... fossil has been optimized to death over the past maybe 100 years." The route to parity is co-location with chemical producers (selling by-product oxygen and hydrogen, heat integration) plus a bet on falling renewable electricity prices — the same bet every electrochemical CO₂ pathway is making. Ip also frames the Strait of Hormuz disruptions and Asian refinery shutdowns as a security-of-supply argument for distributed feedstock production, which is a sharper commercial hook than the climate case.

Adjacent context: the CarbonX program from Tencent sits in the same lane as Western CDR prizes and advance-market commitments, but with a distinctive materials-to-consumer-product angle through CarbonXMade, which has already placed CO₂-derived materials into products in China. For the removal field, the open question is how DAC-to-utilization pathways like this interact with removal accounting as policy matures — if captured CO₂ gets a premium as ethylene feedstock, that pulls molecules away from geological storage, and the two uses will eventually need distinct credit treatment. Ip's claim that data-center load growth will ultimately accelerate grid build-out and help electrochemistry is the optimistic reading; the near-term effect on industrial power prices cuts the other way.

Worth an hour for DAC developers scouting offtake and for anyone tracking CCU-versus-CDR accounting. Not useful if you're after measurement, reporting, and verification (MRV), credit markets or storage.
