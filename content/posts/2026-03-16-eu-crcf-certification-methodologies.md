<p>Here&rsquo;s something that doesn&rsquo;t get enough attention: the EU just gave carbon removal credits their first government-issued quality label.</p><p>The European Commission has adopted a <a href=https://www.globalelr.com/2026/03/carbon-removals-and-carbon-farming-recent-eu-developments/>delegated act</a> setting out certification methodologies for permanent carbon removals under the Carbon Removals and Carbon Farming (<a href=/posts/eu-first-cdr-certification-standard/>CRCF</a>) Regulation. It&rsquo;s voluntary, it&rsquo;s technical, and it might be the most important policy development in CDR markets this year.</p><h2 id=what-the-crcf-actually-is>What the <a href=/posts/eu-first-cdr-certification-standard/>CRCF</a> Actually Is<a hidden class=anchor aria-hidden=true href=#what-the-crcf-actually-is>#</a></h2><p>The <a href=/posts/eu-first-cdr-certification-standard/>CRCF</a> — adopted in December 2024 as part of the EU&rsquo;s climate neutrality 2050 strategy — establishes a standardized framework for certifying carbon dioxide removal activities across Europe. Think of it as a government-backed quality stamp for CDR credits.</p><p>Here&rsquo;s what it covers:</p><p><strong>Permanent carbon removals</strong> — storage of carbon for several centuries (think geological storage from DAC, mineralization, deep ocean injection).</p><p><strong>Carbon storage in products</strong> — processes that lock carbon away for at least 35 years (think biochar in concrete, cross-laminated timber, carbon-enriched building materials).</p><p><strong>Carbon farming</strong> — terrestrial and coastal management practices over at least five years that result in net carbon uptake or reduced soil emissions.</p><p>For each category, the delegated act now specifies how to measure, verify, and certify the removal. Independent certification bodies — accredited by the Commission and member states — handle the verification.</p><h2 id=why-this-matters>Why This Matters<a hidden class=anchor aria-hidden=true href=#why-this-matters>#</a></h2><p>The voluntary carbon market has a trust problem. Buyers can&rsquo;t easily tell the difference between a high-integrity CDR credit and one backed by questionable methodology. Existing labels — Verra&rsquo;s CCB, the ICVCM&rsquo;s Core Carbon Principles — are industry-run. Useful, but not government-backed.</p><p>The <a href=/posts/eu-first-cdr-certification-standard/>CRCF</a> changes that. As <a href=https://www.globalelr.com/2026/03/carbon-removals-and-carbon-farming-recent-eu-developments/>Latham & Watkins notes</a>, it&rsquo;s &ldquo;to our knowledge, the first government-issued label for voluntary credits.&rdquo; Government-accredited certifiers. Standardized methodologies. Transparent quality criteria.</p><p>That carries weight. If you&rsquo;re a corporate buyer trying to meet net-zero commitments, a <a href=/posts/eu-first-cdr-certification-standard/>CRCF</a>-certified credit comes with a level of scrutiny that purely private labels can&rsquo;t match. And where scrutiny goes, premium pricing follows. Project developers who get certified early could command higher prices — which means more revenue flowing into CDR deployment.</p><h2 id=the-ets-connection>The ETS Connection<a hidden class=anchor aria-hidden=true href=#the-ets-connection>#</a></h2><p>Here&rsquo;s where it gets really interesting. The Commission must assess by <strong>July 31, 2026</strong> how permanently stored carbon removals can be integrated into the EU Emissions Trading System (ETS). If <a href=/posts/eu-first-cdr-certification-standard/>CRCF</a> credits become eligible for use in the ETS — even partially — that would connect CDR to the largest carbon market in the world.</p><p>The EU ETS covers roughly 40% of the EU&rsquo;s greenhouse gas emissions. Current carbon prices hover around €65–75/tonne. If a DAC operator or <a href=/posts/what-is-enhanced-weathering/>enhanced weathering</a> company could sell <a href=/posts/eu-first-cdr-certification-standard/>CRCF</a>-certified credits into that market, the economics of CDR deployment change dramatically.</p><p>That&rsquo;s still a big &ldquo;if.&rdquo; The assessment isn&rsquo;t a commitment. But the regulatory architecture is being built, and the direction of travel is clear.</p><h2 id=whats-missing>What&rsquo;s Missing<a hidden class=anchor aria-hidden=true href=#whats-missing>#</a></h2><p>The <a href=/posts/eu-first-cdr-certification-standard/>CRCF</a> is voluntary. Certification schemes <em>may</em> apply for Commission recognition but aren&rsquo;t required to. That means adoption depends on whether buyers value the label enough to pay for it — and whether project developers find the certification process worth the administrative overhead.</p><p>There&rsquo;s also the question of international recognition. A <a href=/posts/eu-first-cdr-certification-standard/>CRCF</a> credit that&rsquo;s only valued within the EU is useful but limited. Whether other jurisdictions — the UK, Japan, South Korea, eventually the US — recognize <a href=/posts/eu-first-cdr-certification-standard/>CRCF</a> certification will determine how much it shapes global CDR markets.</p><p>And the methodologies themselves will need to evolve. CDR science moves fast. <a href=/posts/what-is-enhanced-weathering/>Enhanced weathering</a> MRV protocols are still being debated. Ocean alkalinity enhancement barely has consensus measurement approaches yet. The <a href=/posts/eu-first-cdr-certification-standard/>CRCF</a> will need to keep pace with the science, not just lock in today&rsquo;s best understanding.</p><h2 id=the-bottom-line>The Bottom Line<a hidden class=anchor aria-hidden=true href=#the-bottom-line>#</a></h2><p>Regulated markets attract serious capital. The voluntary carbon market is worth roughly $2 billion. The EU ETS trades over €700 billion per year. If the <a href=/posts/eu-first-cdr-certification-standard/>CRCF</a> creates a credible bridge between CDR and compliance markets, it could unlock investment at a scale that voluntary commitments alone never will.</p><p>This isn&rsquo;t exciting the way a new DAC plant is exciting. It&rsquo;s a delegated act with certification methodologies. But market infrastructure is how technologies go from demonstration to deployment. The EU is building that infrastructure.</p><p>Watch this space.</p><hr><p><em>Source: <a href=https://www.globalelr.com/2026/03/carbon-removals-and-carbon-farming-recent-eu-developments/>Latham & Watkins via Global Environmental Law Review</a>.
