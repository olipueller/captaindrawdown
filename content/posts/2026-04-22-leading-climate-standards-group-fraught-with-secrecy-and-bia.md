Whistleblowers from the Greenhouse Gas Protocol's forest carbon working group say the standards body that underpins corporate climate accounting worldwide is suppressing documents, sidelining scientists, and letting industry representatives push a forest carbon method that lets timber companies look like net carbon removers on paper while changing nothing about their operations. The allegations, detailed in a report shared with Heatmap by political scientist Kate Dooley of the University of Melbourne, land at a moment when the European Union and California are writing the Protocol's standards directly into mandatory disclosure law.
## Why it matters for CDR
Corporate carbon removal claims are only as credible as the accounting rules behind them. If the Greenhouse Gas Protocol, the de facto global standard used by more than 22,100 companies in 2025, lets forest owners count natural tree growth or atmospheric CO2 fertilization as their own removals, it inflates corporate "net" numbers without actually pulling more carbon out of the sky. That damages the credibility of every genuine removal purchase, from reforestation credits to engineered pathways, by muddying what "removal" even means on a balance sheet.
The moral hazard is obvious. Bad forest accounting can let a company claim it is already carbon-negative through paper sequestration, reducing pressure to cut fossil emissions or buy high-integrity removals for residual emissions.
## The details
Dooley joined a 17-person technical working group in December 2024 tasked with resolving how companies that own forests or buy wood products should account for forest carbon. The group included academics, industry representatives from IKEA and the American Forest Foundation, and experts from the Natural Resources Defense Council.
Two methods were on the table.
The first is the **managed land proxy**, or MLP. It lets a company count all carbon sequestered on its land, netted against emissions from logging. A timber firm could buy additional forest and appear to reduce emissions on paper without changing a single harvesting practice. It also lets companies use regional averages rather than the carbon flows from their actual supply source. A modified version, "MLP+," added some guardrails.
The second is **activities-based accounting**. It requires landowners to estimate what growth would have happened without human interference and subtract that baseline from claimed removals. Harder to do. Makes corporate forest emissions look much larger. Scientifically more defensible, because it does not credit a company for trees regrowing after a previous owner clear-cut them, or for growth driven by elevated atmospheric CO2.
The working group split 8 to 7 in favor of MLP+, with two members abstaining. The Independent Standards Board then failed to reach consensus too. In January 2026, the Protocol finalized its land sector standard with no guidance at all on forest carbon accounting, telling companies to pick whatever method they want as long as they disclose it.
That is the worst possible outcome: a standard that is not a standard.
## Inside the room
Dooley alleges the process turned combative. She says Nathan Truitt, executive vice president of climate funding at the American Forest Foundation, argued that scientific papers she cited in support of rejecting MLP actually made the opposite case. She called it "Kafka-esque" and in a long email to the group accused Truitt of industry bias. The American Forest Foundation works with private landowners to support forest product markets, which gives its members a direct financial interest in accounting rules that credit standing forest growth to landowners.
The broader complaints go beyond forestry. The whistleblowers describe proposals, voting records, and decision documents being withheld from the public and even from working group members who spent unpaid months on the effort. Decisions made behind closed doors reportedly went undocumented. This is happening inside the governance overhaul the Protocol launched in 2022 specifically to boost credibility.
## Implications
Regulators should pay attention. If California and the EU are bolting Greenhouse Gas Protocol standards onto binding disclosure laws, they are importing whatever governance problems the Protocol has. A standard that says "use any method, just disclose it" for forest carbon will produce disclosures that cannot be compared across companies and cannot be audited against a common benchmark.
For the durable CDR market, the read is mixed. Engineered removal buyers already argue their tonnes are more measurable and harder to game than land sector claims. This story is evidence for that argument. It also raises a harder question: if forest carbon accounting at the corporate inventory level is this contested, what does it mean for the forest-based credits sold into voluntary markets under related logic?
## Caveats
This is one set of whistleblower accounts, primarily from scientists who lost the internal vote. Truitt and other MLP+ supporters would frame the same events differently, and the Protocol has not publicly responded in detail. A 8-7 vote is not a conspiracy. It is a genuinely hard technical disagreement about attribution, baselines, and feasibility.
The underlying science question, how to separate human-caused forest carbon flux from natural background flux, is legitimately unresolved. Activities-based accounting is harder to implement and the tooling is not mature. The Protocol's decision to punt could reflect that reality as much as industry capture.
What to watch: the Protocol's promised request for information on forest carbon accounting, whether the EU and California write specific methodological guardrails into their disclosure rules rather than outsourcing them, and whether any working group members go public with documents to substantiate the secrecy claims.
Corporate climate accounting only works if the people writing the rules can be trusted. That trust is now an open question.
