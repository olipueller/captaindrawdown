Two papers landed eight days apart this October that, read together, poke at the edges of how enhanced rock weathering gets counted. Noah Jemison and colleagues at Pitt, UMass Amherst, Brown, Rice, and LBNL posted a [reactive-transport study of clay formation and net alkalinity](https://doi.org/10.5194/egusphere-2026-5323) to EGUsphere on 5 October 2026, and Eric Slessarev and co-authors at Yale, UC Davis, LLNL, and Oregon State published [Weathering-derived calcium supports carbon storage in grassland soils](https://doi.org/10.1038/s43247-026-04142-2) in Communications Earth & Environment on 13 October 2026. One is about a term that can subtract from the alkalinity ledger; the other is about a carbon pool the alkalinity ledger ignores entirely.

## Where the field stood

Most ERW accounting — the framework behind Project Drawdown's pathway page, the CDRXIV evidence review, and the field-trial literature like the Midwestern trial that found removal from steel slag but not basalt — tracks cations and alkalinity moving from crushed rock through soil to drainage. Secondary clay formation sits in that chain as a known loss term: cations that end up in clay structures or on exchange sites aren't exported downstream. And soil organic carbon has been treated as a separate, hoped-for co-benefit, with mixed field results. Recent reviews arguing ERW may prove more valuable as a farming practice than as a removal technology lean on exactly that ambiguity.

## What Jemison et al. add

The clay story turns out to be more generous than the loss-term framing suggests. Running CrunchTope models of idealized shale, granite, and basalt, Jemison's team find that clay formation usually *increases* net alkalinity, because clays strip aqueous Al and Si and keep plagioclase undersaturated so it keeps dissolving. In batch runs with 15% plagioclase at 15,000 ppm CO2, kaolinite formation eventually yields 8.5× more alkalinity than a clay-free run. In the 1-D rock profiles, clay precipitation drives roughly 370%, 180%, and 360% more plagioclase dissolution in shale, granite, and basalt respectively.

The per-mole bookkeeping is the useful part for practitioners. Kaolinite and gibbsite consume no alkalinity. Smectite and illite take 0.14 and 0.48 mol alkalinity per mol Al removed — less than the 1 mol per mol Al that plagioclase dissolution supplies, so they remain net positive. Chlorite is the exception at 5 mol per mol Al, a genuine sink. In these models chlorite only forms when pH climbs above ~9, which happened in basalt at low soil pCO2 (1,500 ppm) — not in granite or shale. Clay enhancement is strongest at slow flow and low CO2, when thermodynamics would otherwise stall dissolution; at fast flow, clay-free and clay-bearing runs look similar. The authors flag two things the models leave out: surface passivation by clay coatings and permeability loss as clays clog pores. Both would push the other way.

## What Slessarev et al. add

Slessarev's group built a continental geochemical model of calcium release across the conterminous USA (explaining 69% of spatial variation in soil pH) and regressed A-horizon SOC stocks against modeled exchangeable Ca and eight confounders. Exchangeable Ca ranked third behind NPP and soil temperature. The counterfactuals are the headline: in Mollisol regions, eliminating carbonate weathering cuts preindustrial A-horizon SOC by 5% (0.4 Pg C), eliminating silicate weathering by 3% (0.2 Pg C), and eliminating both by 15% (1.2 Pg C) — close to the 20% (1.6 Pg C) effect of removing grassland vegetation. The non-additivity comes from carbonate and silicate weathering suppressing each other via pH; cut one and the other partly compensates.

Mollisols cover 7% of land but hold 32% of agricultural soil organic carbon, and the authors estimate agriculture has already raised Ca inputs to Mollisol croplands 4–5-fold through liming, while acid inputs accelerated native carbonate dissolution by 28%. They're careful about ERW: they cite the two-year study showing reduced mineral-associated carbon accrual alongside a meta-analysis finding a positive average effect, and note that intermittent liming — only 5–20% of US cropland is limed in any year — may behave differently from steady weathering.

## Reaction and relevance

The Jemison preprint is still in open discussion and I haven't seen substantive public commentary on either paper yet. For companies spreading basalt on Corn Belt Mollisols — Lithos's US deployments sit squarely in Slessarev's study region — the calcium finding sharpens a question about whether SOC should enter the MRV conversation at all, and if so, how to separate a Ca-mediated stabilization signal from the noise. Jemison's chlorite result matters less for farmland (soil CO2 is usually high) than for low-CO2, high-pH settings; the base-case 1-D runs used a 0.01 mm/yr erosion rate chosen explicitly to mimic flat ERW application sites.

What I'll watch: whether the discussion-phase reviews press on passivation and permeability, and whether anyone takes Slessarev's Ca-SOC regression to a tropical, highly weathered setting — where Mollisols don't form and where InPlanet and Terradot operate — to see if the relationship holds outside the post-glacial midcontinent.
