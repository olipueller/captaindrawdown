A Berlin startup called Ucaneo is building a 150-tonne-per-year DAC plant in the Marzahn district, with doors set to open on July 2, 2026. The company uses an electrochemical pH-swing process that operates at room temperature, a meaningful departure from the high-heat approaches that dominate the DAC field today. And the founders aren't stopping there: they're already designing a follow-up plant capable of removing 1,000 to 1,500 tonnes of CO₂ per year, with plans to franchise the technology globally.
## Why it matters
Most DAC systems rely on heating chemical sorbents to extreme temperatures to release captured CO₂, which creates a painful energy penalty and often requires fossil-derived heat. A process that runs at room temperature on electricity alone changes the math. It means the plant can run on surplus wind and solar power, sipping cheap electrons when the grid has more than it needs. If the economics hold up at scale, this approach could make DAC far more compatible with renewable-heavy grids like Germany's.
## The details
Ucaneo GmbH was co-founded by Florian Tiller and Carla Glassl. The company is a member of DVNE, a German association focused on carbon removal. Groundbreaking for the Marzahn plant took place in September 2025, and despite some weather delays during the Berlin winter, construction is on schedule.
The technology is inspired by how the human lung works. Instead of using a "chemical sponge" that binds CO₂ and then needs intense heat to release it, Ucaneo bubbles CO₂ into and out of a solution, similar to how carbonated water works. The key control mechanism is the pH value of the solution, which is adjusted electrochemically. The company pairs this with what it calls (bio)catalytic solvents.
Because the process is purely electrical and runs at ambient temperature, the plant can flex its operations to match renewable energy availability. When there's surplus wind or solar on the grid, the plant ramps up. When electricity is scarce, it can dial back. This is a real operational advantage in a country like Germany, where renewable generation can swing dramatically hour to hour.
Another interesting angle: Ucaneo says many of its components come from established industries like water treatment. That's a deliberate choice. Off-the-shelf components from mature supply chains tend to be cheaper and more available than custom-built parts, which matters a lot when you're trying to scale quickly.
## The franchise model
The most ambitious part of Ucaneo's strategy isn't the Berlin plant itself. It's what comes after. The founders explicitly compare their scaling plan to McDonald's franchising model. The idea is to license the technology so that hundreds of plants could be built worldwide over the next five to ten years.
This is a bet that the core technology can be standardized and replicated without heavy customization at each site. If true, it could dramatically compress deployment timelines compared to the approach of building each plant as a bespoke engineering project. The franchise analogy is catchy, but the real question is whether the unit economics work at the 1,000 to 1,500 tonne scale they're designing next.
Ucaneo is also building a visitor campus at the Marzahn site, planned to open in mid-2026. The goal is to make carbon removal tangible for the public. The site is accessible by public transit, about 20 to 30 minutes from central Berlin.
## Implications
At 150 tonnes per year, this plant is small in absolute terms. For context, global CO₂ emissions run around 37 billion tonnes annually. But the plant is described as one of the largest industrial DAC facilities in Europe, which tells you more about how early the European DAC industry still is than about Ucaneo's size.
The real significance is in the technology pathway. An electrochemical process that avoids high temperatures could have a fundamentally lower energy cost per tonne of CO₂ captured. If Ucaneo can demonstrate reliable operation at the Marzahn plant and then prove the economics at the 1,000 to 1,500 tonne scale, the franchise model becomes genuinely interesting.
A quick but important note: CDR technologies like DAC exist to address residual emissions that can't be eliminated through decarbonization alone. They are not a substitute for cutting fossil fuel use. Every tonne of CO₂ that doesn't get emitted in the first place is cheaper and more certain than a tonne removed from the atmosphere after the fact.
## Caveats
We don't have published data on Ucaneo's energy consumption per tonne of CO₂, cost per tonne, or the durability and lifespan of their solvents. "Room temperature" and "purely electrochemical" sound promising, but the proof will be in verified operational data after the plant starts running in July.
The jump from 150 tonnes to 1,000 to 1,500 tonnes is not trivial. Scaling electrochemical systems can introduce challenges around electrode degradation, solvent management, and heat buildup that don't appear at smaller scales. And the franchise model assumes that regulatory environments, energy costs, and CO₂ storage or utilization options will be favorable across many different countries.
The July 2 opening date will be the first real test. After that, the numbers will start to speak for themselves.
