Microsoft, the buyer behind roughly 90% of all carbon removal purchases worldwide last year, is pausing future CDR buying. If that number doesn't stop you cold, consider this: the company accounts for somewhere between 79% and 90% of all historic carbon removal purchases ever made. This isn't one customer pulling back. This is *the* customer pulling back.

## Why it matters

The carbon removal industry has a concentration problem, and everyone knew it. What nobody wanted to game out publicly was what happens when the single entity propping up almost the entire market decides to step away. Now we're about to find out. Every DAC developer, every enhanced rock weathering (ERW, the practice of spreading crushed minerals to absorb CO2) startup, every bioenergy with carbon capture and storage (BECCS) project that counted on continued Microsoft demand is recalculating its future this week.

## The details

Heatmap News broke the story, citing two people informed of Microsoft's plans. Microsoft's spokesperson pushed back on the characterization that this is an indefinite pause, saying the company "continually review[s] and assess[es] our carbon removal portfolio along with market conditions." But the signal to suppliers and partners was clear enough that multiple sources confirmed it.

The scale of Microsoft's dominance is hard to overstate. The company has purchased 45 million tons of carbon removal, per its own releases. The next largest buyer, Frontier (a coalition led by payments firm Stripe), has bought 1.8 million tons. That's a 25-to-1 ratio. Microsoft didn't just buy credits. It published guidelines defining what "ideal" carbon removal projects should look like, effectively setting early standards for DAC, soil carbon, and ERW. It was both the market and the regulator of that market.

The timing is brutal. Just three days before the pause became public, Microsoft announced a purchase of more than 620,000 tons of credits from an indigenous-owned BECCS project in Saskatchewan, Canada. That deal now reads like a punctuation mark at the end of an era.

Microsoft's CDR buying was driven by its pledge to become "carbon negative" by 2030, meaning it would remove more greenhouse gases than it emits. It also committed to eliminating its entire half-century of historic emissions by 2050. Those goals haven't been formally abandoned, but they've been running headlong into the reality of powering energy-hungry AI data centers. Every new data center makes the math harder.

## What this changes

The immediate fallout is financial. CDR startups have been raising money, hiring engineers, and building pilot plants on the assumption that corporate voluntary demand would keep growing, with Microsoft as the anchor. That assumption just evaporated. Companies that haven't diversified their customer base are in serious trouble.

The secondary effect is psychological. Microsoft's buying sent a signal to other corporations that CDR was a legitimate, investable category. Without that signal, the already-thin pipeline of new corporate buyers gets thinner. Google, Meta, and Amazon have made far smaller commitments. Heatmap's own polling of climate insiders rated Meta and Amazon as failing on climate. There is no obvious replacement buyer waiting in the wings at anything close to Microsoft's scale.

The federal government becomes more important by default. The Trump administration has been hostile to CDR funding, going so far as to redirect more than $500 million in carbon removal money toward propping up aging coal plants. Congress has been slightly more supportive: the 2026 federal spending law included over $116 million for carbon removal research and a federal purchasing program. That $116 million matters more now, but it's a rounding error compared to what Microsoft was spending.

## Caveats

A few things this doesn't tell us. First, Microsoft hasn't said it's canceling existing contracts. The pause appears to apply to future purchases. Projects already under agreement may continue. Second, the spokesperson's denial that this is an indefinite halt leaves room for a restart, perhaps after an internal review or when market conditions shift. Third, we don't know the internal reasoning. It could be cost pressure from AI capital expenditure (the money spent building data centers and infrastructure). It could be a reassessment of credit quality. It could be both.

What this does NOT mean is that carbon removal is unnecessary. The IPCC considers it essential to meeting Paris Agreement targets. Independent estimates put the need at 7 to 9 billion tons of CO2 removal per year by 2050. The science hasn't changed. The funding picture has.

And a necessary note: CDR exists to address residual emissions that can't be eliminated through decarbonization. It is not a substitute for cutting fossil fuel use. Microsoft's massive CDR buying was always meant to complement emissions reductions, not replace them. Any company that treats this pause as evidence that CDR "doesn't work" and therefore emissions cuts are the only path is drawing the wrong lesson. Both are required. Neither alone is sufficient.

The real lesson is simpler and more uncomfortable. An entire industry built on one customer was always fragile. Now it's fractured. The question is whether anyone, government or corporate, steps in before the fracture becomes permanent.
