Germany just committed roughly €500 million to carbon dioxide removal through 2033, making it the largest dedicated national CDR funding program in Europe. The German Bundestag approved the 2026 federal budget on November 28, 2025, and for the first time, it includes a specific funding line for negative emissions.
## Why it matters
Until now, European CDR policy has been a patchwork of pilot grants, research funding, and voluntary market signals. No single country had put this kind of money on the table with a clear, multi-year commitment to building a functioning CDR market. Germany just changed that calculus. For CDR developers who have been waiting for a demand signal from a major European government, this is it.
## The numbers
The budget breaks down into several distinct buckets:
- **€98 million** for supporting and scaling CDR projects
- **€11.5 million** for purchasing high-quality CO₂ removal credits
- **€2 million** for project administration
- **€44.6 million** to strengthen soils as long-term carbon sinks
- **€320 million** in forward-looking authorizations extending through 2033
Add those up and you land just under €500 million. The immediate 2026 allocations total about €156 million, but the forward authorizations are arguably more important. They tell project developers and investors that Germany isn't making a one-off gesture. It's signaling sustained demand over nearly a decade.
The credit purchase line, at €11.5 million, is modest. But the fact that a national government is explicitly budgeting for buying removal credits is significant. It creates a floor of guaranteed demand that private buyers and project developers can plan around.
## What the people involved are saying
Stefan Schlosser, Managing Director of the German Association for Negative Emissions (DVNE), called the allocation "a clear signal" that Germany is moving forward with CDR. His framing was notably candid about the broader climate policy context: "In terms of climate policy, we are currently experiencing stagnation or even regression. This makes it all the more important that we can now move forward into the future with negative emissions."
That's a telling statement. It acknowledges that CDR funding is arriving at a moment when other parts of climate policy are losing momentum. Schlosser also emphasized that the real challenge is execution: "The real work now lies in turning these financial resources into concrete projects, scaling up negative emissions and thus achieving climate protection effects."
## What this changes
A few things to watch.
**For CDR developers operating in or eyeing Germany:** €98 million in project support funding is a meaningful pot. Combined with the forward authorizations, it should reduce the financing risk for early-stage CDR projects. Lenders and equity investors care about policy durability, and a commitment through 2033 provides that.
**For the European CDR market more broadly:** Germany has a habit of setting precedents that other EU member states eventually follow. If this program produces visible results, expect pressure on France, the Netherlands, and the Nordics to match or exceed it. Schlosser explicitly said DVNE wants Germany to become "an inspiring role model for other countries."
**For the soil carbon piece:** The €44.6 million earmarked for strengthening soils as long-term carbon sinks is interesting. Soil carbon is one of the trickier CDR pathways because of permanence and measurement challenges. How Germany structures this funding, what verification standards it requires, and whether it treats soil carbon on par with more durable removal methods will be worth tracking closely.
**For credit buyers:** Government purchases of removal credits, even at €11.5 million, create a reference point. They validate the concept of sovereign CDR procurement and could encourage other governments to follow.
## The moral hazard question
Any time a government commits serious money to CDR, it's worth asking: does this create cover for delaying fossil fuel phase-out? The answer should always be no. CDR exists to handle residual emissions, the hard-to-abate stuff that persists even after aggressive decarbonization. It is not a substitute for cutting emissions at the source.
Schlosser's own comments hint at this tension. He notes that climate policy is experiencing "stagnation or even regression." If CDR funding becomes a consolation prize for weakening emissions reduction targets, that's a problem. The €500 million is welcome precisely to the extent that it complements, rather than replaces, Germany's broader climate commitments.
## Caveats
Budget authorizations are not the same as money spent. Forward authorizations through 2033 depend on future budget cycles and future political coalitions. German politics has been volatile. These commitments could be revised.
We also don't yet know the details of how the €98 million in project funding will be allocated. Which CDR methods qualify? What permanence thresholds apply? How will measurement, reporting, and verification (MRV) be handled? The design of the program will matter as much as the headline number.
And €500 million over roughly eight years, while significant by European standards, is still small relative to the scale of removal the climate math demands. It's a start, not a finish line. The question now is whether Germany can turn this political commitment into tons of CO₂ permanently removed from the atmosphere.
