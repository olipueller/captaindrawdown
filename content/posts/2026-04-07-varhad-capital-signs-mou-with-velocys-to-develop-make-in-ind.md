This MoU between Varhad Capital and Velocys to develop biomass-to-Sustainable Aviation Fuel (SAF) projects in India is a fascinating development, especially when viewed through a CDR lens. The core of the agreement revolves around leveraging Velocys's Fischer-Tropsch (FT) technology to convert biomass into SAF, with a strong emphasis on "Make-In-India" and cost-competitiveness. This is primarily an aviation decarbonization story, aiming to reduce lifecycle emissions from air travel, but it carries significant implications for carbon dioxide removal, even if not explicitly highlighted in the announcement.

From a CDR perspective, the key takeaway here is the potential for integrated Bioenergy with Carbon Capture and Storage (BECCS). Velocys's FT process produces syngas from biomass, which is then converted into liquid fuels. During syngas production and subsequent refining, biogenic CO2 is generated. If this CO2 is captured and permanently stored, the entire process can become carbon-negative, effectively removing CO2 from the atmosphere. While the article doesn't detail plans for carbon capture, a CDR-focused audience should immediately recognize this inherent opportunity. The scale of future SAF production, even at a single plant, could represent significant capture volumes. For instance, a typical large-scale bio-refinery might produce hundreds of thousands of tonnes of biogenic CO2 annually.

The "Make-In-India" aspect is particularly interesting. It suggests a focus on developing local supply chains, not just for the biomass feedstock but potentially for the engineering, procurement, and construction of the facilities. This could foster a robust domestic ecosystem that, if carbon capture is integrated, could also support the development of India's nascent carbon management infrastructure. India is a vast country with significant agricultural waste, offering a substantial and potentially sustainable biomass feedstock base, which is crucial for any BECCS play to be truly credible. However, the sustainability of biomass sourcing – avoiding land-use change and ensuring minimal emissions from cultivation and transport – remains a critical due diligence point for CDR projects.

The emphasis on "cost-competitive" is vital. For SAF to scale, and by extension, for BECCS linked to SAF production to become viable, the economics must work. Government policies like India's National Biofuel Policy or potential future SAF mandates will be crucial in bridging any cost differential with fossil fuels. We've seen similar dynamics in other regions, with mandates and incentives driving SAF production. For instance, the US Inflation Reduction Act's 45Q tax credit for carbon capture and 45Z for clean hydrogen production, alongside other SAF incentives, is creating a powerful pull for integrated solutions. India would need similar supportive policies to make large-scale BECCS an attractive proposition alongside SAF.

Ultimately, this MoU represents a foundational step towards large-scale biomass utilization in India. While the immediate focus is on decarbonizing aviation, the underlying technology and feedstock availability present a ripe opportunity for future integration with carbon capture. As CDR analysts, we should be watching closely to see if Varhad and Velocys, or future partners, choose to explicitly pursue the carbon-negative pathway by adding CCS to these projects. The scale of India's energy demand and biomass resources means that even a fraction of such projects adopting BECCS could contribute meaningfully to global carbon removal efforts.
