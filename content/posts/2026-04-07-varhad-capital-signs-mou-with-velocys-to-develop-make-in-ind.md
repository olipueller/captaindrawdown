The recent MoU between Varhad Capital and Velocys to develop biomass-to-Sustainable Aviation Fuel (SAF) projects in India offers an interesting lens through which to view the evolving landscape for carbon dioxide removal, even if it’s not a direct CDR play itself. The core of the agreement is leveraging Velocys's proprietary Fischer-Tropsch (FT) technology, which converts biomass-derived syngas into SAF, with a strong emphasis on "Make-In-India" and cost-competitiveness. This isn't strictly CDR, as the carbon in the SAF is released upon combustion, but it's a critical step in decarbonizing hard-to-abate sectors like aviation, and its foundational technology has significant implications for future CDR integration.

What's particularly notable here is the geographical focus on India. A rapidly growing economy with substantial agricultural biomass resources, India presents a massive opportunity for bio-based industries. The "Make-In-India" component suggests a push for local manufacturing and supply chains, which could help drive down costs and foster domestic expertise. For a CDR-focused audience, the availability of sustainable biomass feedstocks is paramount. Projects like this, even if primarily aimed at SAF production, help de-risk the biomass supply chain and develop the logistics and infrastructure necessary for larger-scale bioenergy applications. This groundwork is essential for any future BECCS (Bioenergy with Carbon Capture and Storage) projects, which *are* a direct form of CDR.

However, the article doesn't explicitly mention carbon capture and storage, which is the crucial differentiator for making biomass-to-fuels a CDR pathway. Without CCS, these facilities are emissions reduction projects, not removal. This highlights a common challenge: the immediate economic drivers are often focused on fuel production and decarbonization credits (like SAF mandates or tax credits such as the US 45Z for clean fuels), rather than the potentially more complex and less immediately lucrative CDR market. While Velocys's technology *could* be integrated with CCS, as seen with their proposed Altalto project in the UK (which aims to capture CO2), it’s not a given for every deployment. The current focus on "cost-competitive" SAF suggests that adding the significant capex and opex of CCS might be a secondary consideration, only pursued if policy mechanisms like a robust carbon price or dedicated CDR procurement strongly incentivize it.

From a CDR perspective, this MoU is a signal of the growing maturity and deployment of biomass conversion technologies. As these plants scale up and optimize their operations, the incremental cost of adding CCS will decrease, making BECCS more viable. The development of a robust biomass supply chain in India, driven by SAF demand, could pave the way for future dedicated biochar or BECCS facilities. We need to watch how policy evolves in India, specifically if and when direct CDR incentives emerge. For now, this is a positive step for bio-industrial development, laying groundwork that future CDR projects could potentially build upon, even if it's not directly removing CO2 from the atmosphere today.
