Three preprints landed on CDRxiv that, taken together, mark a real inflection point for how the field can talk about the environmental safety of ocean alkalinity enhancement (OAE) and marine enhanced rock weathering (mERW). Emilia Jankowska and colleagues at Hourglass Climate, with collaborators at Stony Brook, Woods Hole, Dartmouth, and Cornell Cooperative Extension, have published the first field-trial results on [oyster impacts](https://cdrxiv.org/preprint/326) and [benthic community responses](https://cdrxiv.org/preprint/327) from an olivine deployment on a Southampton, NY beach. And Lennart Bach, Michael Tyka, Bin Wang, and Katja Fennel offer a companion piece that reframes how we should even design the toxicity experiments in the first place: ["Lethal by design?"](https://cdrxiv.org/preprint/457). After years of lab and mesocosm work, this is the field record catching up.

### What the status quo looked like

Until now, most of what we knew about the ecological safety of OAE and olivine-based mERW came from bottle experiments, mesocosms, and modeling. The National Academies' 2021 report and subsequent reviews (see Ocean Conservancy's overview and the recent [Chemical Reviews synthesis](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/10.1021/acs.chemrev.5c00433)) all flagged the same open questions: does olivine's nickel and chromium load bioaccumulate in shellfish? Does dumping ground silicate rock onto a beach smother the infauna? And are the alkalinity perturbations tested in labs anywhere close to what organisms would actually see in the ocean? NOAA's OAP has been explicit that short-term, local-scale trials are where these answers have to come from. The Carbon to Sea Initiative has been funding exactly this kind of validation pipeline.

### What's new

For oysters: after a year of co-deployment with olivine, Eastern oysters showed no significant biomass difference from controls, and mean trace metal accumulations (Ni, Cr, Co, Mn) stayed below FDA warning thresholds and within global natural ranges. That's the first field-scale answer to the nickel bioaccumulation question that has hung over olivine since Schuiling and Tickell first proposed coastal spreading.

For the benthos: using a before–after control–impact design, Jankowska et al. found that abundance and species richness at olivine-treated sites recovered to control levels within roughly two months, with diversity and evenness unchanged. Community composition did shift after placement — but the shift was consistent across olivine and control plots, pointing to the nourishment event itself rather than an olivine-specific signal. Multivariate analysis picked up reassembly that univariate metrics missed, which is a useful methodological note for anyone designing follow-on trials. Sediment metal concentrations tracked controls over the year.

Bach and coauthors then supply the interpretive scaffolding. Using a dilution equation plus regional and global models, they show that ΔTA >1000 μmol kg⁻¹ — a perturbation commonly used in OAE toxicity experiments — only occurs for minutes in a vanishingly small fraction of a realistically dispersed plume. The ubiquitous range is 1–100 μmol kg⁻¹, which has been comparatively understudied. The implication is not that high-ΔTA experiments are wrong; it's that they need contextualization or they'll systematically overestimate harm.

### Who this bears on

The most direct read-across is to companies pursuing coastal olivine or mineral-based OAE — [Planetary](https://www.planetarytech.com/), [Adept Aqua](https://www.adeptaqua.com/), and [Arbon Earth](https://arbon.earth/) all operate in the mineral-alkalinity space where these safety questions dominate permitting conversations. Hourglass Climate itself is the operator behind the Southampton trial. The Bach et al. guidance touches every OAE pathway, electrochemical approaches like [Banyu Carbon](https://www.banyucarbon.com/) included, since anyone commissioning ecotoxicity work now has a defensible framework for choosing perturbation magnitudes.

### What I'm watching next

Three things. First, whether other operators replicate the BACI design in different sediment types and hydrodynamic regimes — Southampton is one beach, one year, one grain size. Second, longer-term monitoring: two months of benthic recovery is encouraging, but multi-year records will matter for regulators. Third, whether the Bach framework gets adopted by the labs currently running OAE toxicity screens, because if it does, we should see a wave of more realistically-scoped ecotoxicity papers in the next 18 months — and the interpretation of older results will need to be revisited with the ΔTA context in hand.
