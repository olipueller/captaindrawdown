<p>Materials science just produced one of those results that makes you do a double take.</p><p>Researchers at Japan&rsquo;s Gifu University created a rubber — technically a &ldquo;CO₂-curable elastomer&rdquo; — that absorbs carbon dioxide from its surroundings and transforms into a rigid, acrylic-like plastic. Published in <a href=https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-025-65495-4>Nature Communications</a>.</p><h2 id=how-it-works>How It Works<a hidden class=anchor aria-hidden=true href=#how-it-works>#</a></h2><p>The material combines polyethyleneimine (PEI), which reacts with CO₂, with polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS), a silicone polymer that CO₂ passes through easily. On its own, PEI absorbs ~1mg of CO₂ per gram. Bonded with PDMS, it absorbs <strong>220mg per gram</strong> — the PDMS creates internal &ldquo;passageways&rdquo; that let CO₂ reach PEI deep inside the material.</p><p>After absorption, the rubber becomes <strong>more than 1,000 times harder</strong>. Slip-proof rubber surface → smooth plastic surface.</p><p>The most intriguing part: it&rsquo;s reversible. Heat it to 70–100°C and the CO₂ escapes, reverting the plastic back to rubber. Under UV light, absorbed CO₂ produces blue fluorescence — a built-in visual indicator.</p><h2 id=carbon-removal-or-carbon-utilization>Carbon Removal or Carbon Utilization?<a hidden class=anchor aria-hidden=true href=#carbon-removal-or-carbon-utilization>#</a></h2><p>Let&rsquo;s be clear: this isn&rsquo;t a CDR technology. At 220mg per gram, you&rsquo;d need roughly 4.5 tonnes of this rubber to capture a single kilogram of CO₂. And the CO₂ releases again when heated.</p><p>But it&rsquo;s a fascinating CCU (carbon capture and utilization) concept. Imagine construction materials that harden by absorbing ambient CO₂. Or CO₂ sensors built from the material itself. The reversibility even suggests potential for CO₂ capture-release cycles in industrial settings.</p><p>The team at Gifu is now working on a version that retains CO₂ permanently and maintains its plastic state — which would push it closer to genuine carbon storage territory.</p><h2 id=the-japan-cdr-scene>The Japan CDR Scene<a hidden class=anchor aria-hidden=true href=#the-japan-cdr-scene>#</a></h2><p>This isn&rsquo;t an isolated data point. Japan&rsquo;s CDR ecosystem is growing fast. ENEOS (Japan&rsquo;s largest energy company) just invested in Berkeley DAC startup AirMyne. Sumitomo partnered with Global Thermostat. Mitsui backed Heirloom. Japanese industrial conglomerates are quietly building CDR portfolios across multiple pathways.</p><p>For a country with limited land area but massive industrial capacity, materials-based carbon capture could be a natural fit.</p><p><strong>Source:</strong> <a href=https://japan-forward.com/scientists-develop-new-rubber-material-that-absorbs-co2/>Japan Forward</a> · <a href=https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-025-65495-4>Nature Communications</a></p><hr><h3 id=-related-reading>🔗 Related Reading<a hidden class=anchor aria-hidden=true href=#-related-reading>#</a></h3><ul><li><a href=/posts/cof-electrochemical-carbon-capture/>New COF Material Captures CO₂ With Electricity — No Heat Required</a></li><li><a href=/posts/eu-ccus-financing-bottleneck/>EU Risks Losing Heavy Industry If CCUS Deployment Stalls</a></li><li><a href=/posts/pressure-based-co2-capture-26-per-ton/>Pressure-Based CO₂ Capture Claims $26/Ton — Here&rsquo;s What to Make of It</a></li><li><a href=/posts/green-carbon-india-biochar-partnership/>Japan-India Biochar Alliance: Industrial-Scale CDR Comes to Asia</a></li></ul>
