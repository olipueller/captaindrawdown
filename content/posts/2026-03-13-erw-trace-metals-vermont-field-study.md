<p>One of the persistent concerns about <a href=/posts/what-is-enhanced-weathering/>enhanced rock weathering</a> (<a href=/posts/what-is-enhanced-weathering/>ERW</a>) is trace metals. You&rsquo;re spreading crushed rock on farmland — what happens to the nickel, chromium, and other metals in that basalt?</p><p>A new preprint on CDRxiv offers some of the most detailed field data yet. <strong>Short answer: no detectable increase in harmful metals after two years.</strong></p><h2 id=the-study>The study<a hidden class=anchor aria-hidden=true href=#the-study>#</a></h2><p>Researchers from Yale and collaborators applied 20 tonnes/hectare of iron- and aluminum-rich basalt to hayfield and pasture soils on a working Vermont dairy farm. They sampled agricultural soils (0–15 cm) twice before application and four times after, spanning fall 2022 through spring 2025. They also tracked a riparian corridor hydrologically connected to the treated fields.</p><h2 id=results>Results<a hidden class=anchor aria-hidden=true href=#results>#</a></h2><ul><li><strong>No increase</strong> in extractable Ni, Cr, Pb, Al, Mn, Zn, Cu, or Cd in treated agricultural plots versus controls.</li><li><strong>Forage yields unchanged.</strong> Tissue metal concentrations in hay didn&rsquo;t differ between treated and untreated plots.</li><li><strong>Surprising downstream effect:</strong> Riparian soils showed 60–80% declines in extractable Ni, Al, and Pb, coinciding with a rise in Ca:Al ratios — consistent with basalt-derived solutes actually reducing metal mobility.</li></ul><p>In other words, the basalt weathering products that moved downslope appear to have <em>sequestered</em> trace metals rather than mobilized them.</p><h2 id=why-this-matters-for-erw-deployment>Why this matters for <a href=/posts/what-is-enhanced-weathering/>ERW</a> deployment<a hidden class=anchor aria-hidden=true href=#why-this-matters-for-erw-deployment>#</a></h2><p>Trace metal risk is a legitimate concern that critics raise — and rightly so. Iron-rich basalts contain nickel and chromium at concentrations that warrant monitoring. This study, conducted at realistic application rates on a real farm over multiple seasons, provides strong evidence that agronomic-rate applications pose minimal risk.</p><p>The paper also makes a practical MRV recommendation: track soil pH and Ca:Al ratios as indicators of metal availability, and consider hydrologic connectivity when designing monitoring programs.</p><p>It&rsquo;s one study, one soil type, one basalt source. But it adds to a growing body of evidence that <a href=/posts/what-is-enhanced-weathering/>ERW</a> can be deployed responsibly — provided you monitor properly and choose your feedstock carefully.</p><p><strong>Source:</strong> <a href=https://cdrxiv.org/preprint/463>Zacharias et al.
