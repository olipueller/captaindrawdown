Spreading crushed rock on U.S. farmland could pull 0.23 to 0.38 billion tonnes of CO₂ out of the atmosphere every year, enough to meet 36 to 60 percent of the country's technological CDR targets. That's the central finding from a state-by-state analysis led by David Beerling at the University of Sheffield, with a team of 20 researchers spanning geochemistry, economics, and climate science.

## Why it matters

The U.S. needs to remove roughly 1 billion tonnes of CO₂ per year by mid-century to hit net-zero, according to most federal modeling. Most of that burden is expected to fall on technological approaches like DAC and enhanced rock weathering (ERW), which accelerates the natural process by which silicate minerals react with CO₂ in soil and lock it away as dissolved bicarbonates. This paper argues that ERW alone, layered onto existing farming operations, could handle more than a third of that job. If the numbers hold, it would make ERW one of the largest single CDR levers available to the country.

## The details

The approach is straightforward in concept: crush basalt or similar silicate rocks, spread the powder on cropland, and let soil chemistry and rainfall do the work. CO₂ dissolves into soil water, reacts with the rock minerals, and gets carried as bicarbonate through rivers to the ocean, where it stays locked up for tens of thousands of years.

Beerling and colleagues modeled this across all 50 states, accounting for soil type, temperature, rainfall, crop coverage, and rock supply logistics. The headline range of 0.23 to 0.38 Gt CO₂ per year reflects different assumptions about deployment scale and weathering rates.

Three Midwestern states stand out: Iowa, Illinois, and Indiana. They contribute the most to total national CDR potential, thanks to vast cropland acreage, favorable soil conditions, and relatively warm, wet summers that speed up the chemical reactions. By 2050, costs in these states are projected to fall to roughly $100 to $150 per tonne of CO₂ removed.

That cost trajectory matters. Early deployment is more expensive because of rock sourcing, crushing infrastructure, and transport. But as supply chains mature and application scales up, the economics improve. The $100 to $150 range by mid-century would put ERW in the same ballpark as many DAC cost projections, but with a key advantage: it piggybacks on infrastructure farmers already use. Lime and fertilizer spreaders can handle crushed rock. No new machines required.

The paper also examines whether rivers and oceans can actually absorb the dissolved weathering products without environmental harm. Their assessment suggests the geochemical capacity is sufficient, and that the carbon stays locked away on "intergenerational time scales," meaning centuries to millennia.

An unexpected bonus: the researchers forecast that ERW could reduce ground-level ozone, a pollutant that damages crops and human health. As climate change worsens ozone levels in agricultural regions, the alkalinity changes from rock dust could partially offset those increases. That means avoided crop yield losses on top of the carbon removal, though the paper treats this as an indirect benefit rather than a primary selling point.

## The political angle

Here's where it gets interesting. The paper explicitly identifies multiple electoral swing states as essential for scaling ERW, and notes that those same states would be key beneficiaries. The authors argue this creates a natural case for bipartisan support. Whether you find that framing persuasive or a bit too neat, the underlying point is valid: ERW's value is concentrated in the agricultural heartland, not on the coasts. That geographic distribution could matter for policy.

## Implications

If these projections are even roughly correct, ERW deserves a much larger share of CDR policy attention and R&D funding than it currently gets. Most federal CDR dollars flow toward DAC hubs and geological storage. ERW has lower capital expenditure requirements (no giant facilities to build), integrates with existing land use, and could deliver co-benefits like improved soil pH and reduced fertilizer needs.

For CDR buyers, the cost trajectory is encouraging. At $100 to $150 per tonne by 2050, ERW credits could become competitive with other durable removal methods. Companies like UNDO, Lithos, and Mati Carbon are already working on commercial ERW deployment, and this kind of modeling gives them a stronger case for scaling.

## Caveats

A few important ones. First, this is a modeling study, not a field measurement campaign. The weathering rates used are based on geochemical models calibrated against lab and some field data, but real-world performance at scale could differ. Soil heterogeneity, rock mineralogy variations, and farmer adoption rates all introduce uncertainty.

Second, measurement, reporting, and verification (MRV) for ERW remains one of the hardest problems in CDR. Tracking dissolved bicarbonate through soil, groundwater, and river systems is genuinely difficult. The paper acknowledges this indirectly by assessing geochemical carrying capacity, but doesn't solve the MRV challenge.

Third, the authors themselves flag the need for a "stage-gating framework" to protect against environmental and biodiversity risks as deployment scales. Dumping millions of tonnes of crushed rock on farmland is not zero-risk. Heavy metal content in source rock, effects on soil microbiology, and downstream impacts on freshwater chemistry all need monitoring.

Finally, and this is worth stating plainly: ERW at scale does not reduce the urgency of cutting fossil fuel emissions. CDR addresses residual emissions that are genuinely hard to abate. It is not a substitute for decarbonization. The 0.23 to 0.38 Gt CO₂ per year figure is meaningful only in a world that is simultaneously and aggressively reducing emissions at source.
