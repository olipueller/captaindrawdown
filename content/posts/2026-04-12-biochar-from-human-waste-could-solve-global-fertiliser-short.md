Biochar made from human excrement, combined with nutrients recovered from urine, could supply 15% of the phosphorus, 17% of the nitrogen, and up to 25% of the potassium used globally in agriculture each year. That's the headline finding from a Cornell-led study published in PNAS, and it reframes one of civilization's least glamorous waste streams as a serious input for food production and emissions reduction.
## Why it matters
Agriculture accounts for roughly 25% of global greenhouse gas emissions, according to the IPCC. A big chunk of that comes from manufacturing and applying synthetic fertilizers. Nitrogen fertilizer production and use alone emits an estimated 2.6 billion tonnes of CO2 annually, more than global aviation and shipping combined. Any pathway that displaces even a fraction of synthetic fertilizer demand is worth paying attention to.
## What the study found
The research, led by Dr. Johannes Lehmann, a professor of soil biogeochemistry at Cornell University, modeled the global potential of converting solid human waste into biochar, a charcoal-like material produced by heating organic matter at high temperatures. Biochar from solids alone could provide about 7% of the world's annual phosphorus needs. But when you add nutrients extracted from urine to the biochar, the numbers jump considerably: 15% of phosphorus, 17% of nitrogen, and 25% of potassium.
Those three nutrients, nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium, are the core trio delivered by synthetic fertilizers. And each one comes with serious environmental baggage in its current production form.
Strip mining phosphate rock scars landscapes and generates radioactive phosphogypsum as a byproduct. Potash mining for potassium causes soil salinization and freshwater contamination from waste salt. And the Haber process for converting atmospheric nitrogen into ammonia is extraordinarily energy-intensive.
The biochar approach sidesteps much of this. It also shrinks the weight and volume of solid excrement by up to 90%, a huge logistics advantage over transporting wet sewage sludge.
## The contamination problem, solved at the source
Treated sewage sludge is already spread on farmland in many countries, but it's controversial for good reason. It often contains microplastics, heavy metals, PFAS forever chemicals, pathogens, and pharmaceutical residues. The researchers argue that biochar avoids this problem by separating waste at the source, before it enters mixed sewage systems where contamination accumulates.
The biochar production process also allows nutrient ratios to be adjusted for specific crops. That's a meaningful advantage over blanket fertilizer application, which can cause eutrophication, the process where excess nutrients leach into waterways and trigger algal blooms that choke aquatic life.
## Geopolitics and food security
Lehmann makes a point that extends well beyond soil science: "The implications go beyond just agriculture, and involve economics and geopolitics." Morocco holds 70% of the world's phosphate reserves. As finite mineral resources become scarcer, countries without significant deposits face growing dependence on those that have them.
A circular nutrient economy, where human waste is recovered and recycled into fertilizer, could reduce that dependence. Lehmann frames this as an environmental justice issue, particularly for the global south, where agricultural failure is already a driver of climate migration.
## What this changes
The study doesn't claim biochar from human waste will replace synthetic fertilizers entirely. Supplying 15-25% of key nutrients is meaningful but partial. Still, partial displacement at global scale is significant when you consider the emissions, mining damage, and geopolitical risk embedded in current fertilizer supply chains.
For the CDR community, the connection is worth noting carefully. Biochar is often discussed as a carbon removal tool because it locks carbon into a stable solid form. However, the Guardian article was actually amended after publication to remove the claim that this particular process removes carbon from the atmosphere. That correction matters. Not every biochar application qualifies as carbon dioxide removal, and the study's primary contribution is about nutrient recovery and fertilizer substitution, not carbon sequestration.
The emissions benefit here is real but indirect: displacing synthetic fertilizer production avoids the massive CO2 footprint of the Haber process and mining operations. That's an emissions reduction story, not necessarily a removal story. The distinction is important for anyone tracking CDR claims.
## Caveats
The study models global potential, not deployment reality. Getting from "this could work" to functioning collection, processing, and distribution systems in diverse countries is an enormous practical challenge. Source-separated waste collection barely exists at scale in most of the world. The economics of building that infrastructure aren't addressed here.
There's also the cultural dimension. Convincing farmers and consumers to embrace fertilizer derived from human excrement is not a trivial communications exercise, even if the science is sound.
And to be clear: reducing fertilizer emissions is important, but it's not a substitute for phasing out fossil fuels. Agriculture's 25% share of global emissions demands attention, but the other 75% still requires direct decarbonization. Nutrient recycling from human waste is a piece of the puzzle, not the whole picture.
What the study does establish is a credible, quantified case that we're flushing away resources we can't afford to waste. As Lehmann put it: "Talking about sewage is not as glamorous as renewable energy, but preventing resource wastage by creating a circular economy is also key to the green transition."
