> Take on a podcast episode from **Disruptors**, originally published Wed, 21 Oc.
> Listen: <https://thoughtleadership.rbc.com/rbc-disruptors/>

**TL;DR**

- Deep Sky positions itself as a "project developer" for direct air capture (DAC), hosting multiple vendor technologies on one Alberta site to compress the learning curve. Useful framing.
- CEO Alex Petre claims first DAC credits produced in North America as of June. Contestable — US sites have delivered earlier; depends on definition.
- Final investment decision on a first commercial facility targeted late 2027; three sites in development (Alberta, Manitoba, Quebec); three large plants operational within five years.
- The commercial thesis is export: Canada's storage geology plus the DAC investment tax credit sell to storage-poor buyers like Japan and Switzerland via Paris Article 6.2.
- No cost-per-tonne, capacity, or buyer figures disclosed. The episode is strategy and jurisdiction, not economics.

RBC's [Disruptors](https://thoughtleadership.rbc.com/rbc-disruptors/) sends host John Stackhouse to Deep Sky's test site in Innisfail, Alberta, for a conversation with CEO Alex Petre. The episode is a tour-plus-interview covering how DAC works, why Deep Sky runs competing capture units side by side, and why Petre thinks Canada — geology, gas-processing workforce, fiscal regime — is the jurisdiction to build in. It's pitched at a general Canadian business audience, so the basics get airtime.

The substantive idea is the project-developer model. Petre argues DAC until recently had only technology developers and no one playing the role utility-scale solar or wind had in independent power producers. Deep Sky's five-acre Innisfail site runs roughly half a dozen vendor units (Airbus and a British firm are name-checked) under commercial terms: whichever hardware delivers the cheapest verified tonnes gets more of the next build. The stated goal is to shortcut "the natural market forces" toward a standard DAC design by learning from several datasets at once. That's a reasonable hypothesis, and the fact that the site is producing credits rather than papers matters — though the "first in North America" claim will raise eyebrows given earlier US deliveries, and the transcript doesn't specify tonnage or registry.

The second thread is where the money comes from. Petre's investor pitch is jurisdictional rather than technological: the Canadian DAC/carbon capture investment tax credit, existing Class VI-equivalent storage expertise from oil and gas, and bilateral demand from countries with hard targets and no storage. Japan supplies capital and supply chain; Canada supplies land, pore space, and the credit. Stackhouse pushes on the Article 6.2 mechanics — corresponding adjustments, double-counting, and the fact that a tonne sold to Tokyo cannot also count toward Ottawa's inventory. Petre's answer is that Canada should treat removals as a "digital export" needing no new pipelines or ports, while keeping domestic-target tonnes accounted separately. The federal engagement with Environment, Natural Resources, and Finance is described as active but unfinished. Timeline: FID late 2027, three commercial facilities in five years.

What's absent is as telling as what's there. There is no cost curve, no $/t target, no named offtaker, and the durability and monitoring side of storage is asserted as a solved oil-and-gas competency rather than discussed. The "geotech race" framing and the China mention are rhetorical, not evidential.

For context, [Deep Sky](https://deepskyclimate.com) has previously announced offtake with large tech buyers and backing from institutional investors; this episode adds the Manitoba and Quebec site pipeline and the 2027 FID date to the public record. The multi-vendor test-bed model is the closest thing CDR has to a DAC "bake-off," and readers tracking Climeworks, Heirloom, and 1PointFive cost trajectories will want to see whether Deep Sky publishes per-vendor performance data rather than just aggregate credits.

Useful for policy and finance people working Article 6 deals or Canadian incentives; practitioners already following Deep Sky will find little new engineering detail.
