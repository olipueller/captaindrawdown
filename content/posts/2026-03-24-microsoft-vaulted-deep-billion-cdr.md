<p>Microsoft just signed what might be the largest carbon removal contract in history — and nobody in Washington had anything to do with it.</p><p><a href=https://www.eenews.net/articles/microsoft-spends-billions-on-nascent-carbon-dioxide-removal/>E&amp;E News reports</a> that Microsoft has agreed to store <strong>4.9 million tonnes of CO₂</strong> with Vaulted Deep, a Houston-based startup that uses geological storage. Neither party confirms the price, but industry experts estimate the deal could be worth approximately <strong>$1 billion</strong>. Vaulted Deep CEO Julia Reichelstein called the deal&rsquo;s impact &ldquo;Revolutionary.&rdquo;</p><p>She&rsquo;s not wrong.</p><h2 id=the-scale-is-staggering>The scale is staggering<a hidden class=anchor aria-hidden=true href=#the-scale-is-staggering>#</a></h2><p>To put 4.9 million tonnes in context: the entire global CDR industry (excluding conventional forestry) removed roughly 0.01 million tonnes in 2023. This single contract dwarfs the combined output of every DAC plant, <a href=/posts/what-is-enhanced-weathering/>enhanced weathering</a> project, and biochar operation on Earth. Obviously it&rsquo;s a multi-year commitment, not instant delivery. But the signal is enormous.</p><p>This is part of Microsoft&rsquo;s pledge to offset all carbon the company has emitted since its 1975 founding and reach carbon negative by 2030. That&rsquo;s an extraordinarily aggressive target, and Microsoft has been writing checks to match. At this point, Microsoft has likely spent more on CDR than most national governments.</p><h2 id=corporate-procurement-as-industrial-policy>Corporate procurement as industrial policy<a hidden class=anchor aria-hidden=true href=#corporate-procurement-as-industrial-policy>#</a></h2><p>Here&rsquo;s what&rsquo;s actually happening: the federal government was supposed to build the CDR market. The Bipartisan Infrastructure Law allocated $3.5 billion for DAC hubs. The IRA created 45Q tax credits. The whole policy architecture was designed to pull CDR from lab to deployment.</p><p>Then the funding froze. DAC hubs stalled. Political headwinds made every federal dollar uncertain.</p><p>Microsoft didn&rsquo;t wait. Neither did Frontier (the Stripe-led advance market commitment), nor a handful of other corporate buyers. But Microsoft is operating at a different magnitude. A billion-dollar deal with a single startup isn&rsquo;t a pilot program — it&rsquo;s market creation.</p><p>The uncomfortable question is whether one company&rsquo;s climate commitment can substitute for industrial policy. The answer is obviously no. Microsoft can create demand. It can derisk specific companies. It can set price signals. What it can&rsquo;t do is build the permitting frameworks, pipeline infrastructure, monitoring standards, and workforce training that a real industry needs.</p><p>But right now, with the federal government absent, corporate procurement is doing the heavy lifting. Microsoft is the de facto CDR industrial policy of the United States — and that&rsquo;s both impressive and deeply concerning.</p><p>The CDR sector needs buyers at this scale. It also needs a government that shows up. We&rsquo;ve got one of the two.</p>
