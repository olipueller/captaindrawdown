> Take on a YouTube video from **Musa Tule**, originally posted 2026-10-05.
> Watch the source: <https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=b34GVwxE66s>

**TL;DR**

- Explainer on Canada Nickel's Crawford project near Timmins, Ontario: a 41-year, ~1.6 Mt-nickel mine whose ultramafic tailings can mineralize CO2.
- Mechanism covered accurately: serpentinized ore rich in brucite reacts with concentrated CO2 in-circuit, forming magnesium carbonate in hours. Useful for a lay audience.
- Title is wrong on its face — this *is* carbon capture and storage, just mineralized. Overstated.
- Economic framing is the real signal: carbonation as the business case that justifies a sulfide mine in an Indonesia-flooded market.
- Transcript cuts off before any tonnage or cost numbers for the carbonation itself. Treat the scale claim as unverified here.

A general-interest Canadian channel ([video here](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=b34GVwxE66s)) walks through the Crawford nickel project and Canada Nickel Company's plan to inject concentrated CO2 into tailings while they're still moving through the processing circuit. The substantive argument is that a mine's waste stream — millions of tonnes of already-crushed, already-reactive rock — is a cheaper mineralization reactor than anything purpose-built.

**What's worth knowing**

The chemistry section is sound. Crawford's ore body has been serpentinized, so water has already converted the original olivine-family minerals into reactive magnesium compounds, notably brucite (Mg(OH)2). Dissolved CO2 forms carbonic acid, liberates magnesium ions, and precipitates magnesium carbonate — stable at surface conditions, no pressure maintenance, no well to monitor. The video's framing that this is "closer to rusting" than to storing gas in a tank is a decent one-liner for explaining mineralization to non-specialists. The process description — tailings slurry into stirred tanks, CO2 sparged through, several hours' residence time, then onward to the tailings management area — matches what Canada Nickel has described publicly as in-process tailings carbonation.

The second point is the one practitioners should actually weigh. The narrator argues that Crawford's strongest justification "might not be the nickel at all. It might be the garbage." That's a candid read of the economics: nickel is at four-year lows, the market is expected to stay in surplus through 2026, roughly two-thirds of supply comes from Indonesian laterite with integrated smelting, and a high-grade Ontario sulfide deposit is slower and costlier to build. If carbonation credits or a low-carbon-nickel premium tip the project across the line, then mine-tailings mineralization isn't a side benefit — it's load-bearing for the mine existing at all. That's a different dependency structure from most CDR, where the removal is the product. Here the removal's fate is coupled to nickel prices and permitting timelines that have nothing to do with carbon.

What's missing: the transcript I have ends mid-sentence before the video gives annual CO2 tonnage, the CO2 source (a mine needs a concentrated stream from somewhere — captured flue gas? purchased?), conversion efficiency on the brucite fraction, or cost per tonne. The teaser compares it to "hundreds of thousands of cars," which tells you nothing. If you want the numbers, go to the company's technical reports, not this video.

**Broader context**

Ultramafic tailings carbonation has been studied for two decades, and the "crushing is already paid for" argument is the standard pitch. [Canada Nickel](https://www.canadanickel.com) has been the most visible developer trying to turn it into a permitted, credited process at a greenfield mine rather than a retrofit. The video gestures at Iceland's basalt injection work as proof the chemistry scales, but basalt injection and tailings carbonation face different bottlenecks: the former is limited by suitable geology and water, the latter by the CO2 supply and by how much of the tailings mass is actually reactive. The video doesn't make that distinction.

Useful for people explaining mineralization to a mining or policy audience who've never heard of brucite. Practitioners already tracking tailings carbonation will learn nothing new, and the title will annoy you.
