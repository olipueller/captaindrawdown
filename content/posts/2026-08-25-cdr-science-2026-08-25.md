Three preprints landed on CDRxiv in quick succession that, taken together, redraw how I think about the aquatic leg of enhanced rock weathering (ERW). One asks what rivers *do* to ERW-derived dissolved inorganic carbon (DIC) and alkalinity before it ever reaches the sea. One models what happens once that alkalinity hits a real coastal ocean. And one proposes skipping the field entirely and dosing rivers directly. The lede for me: rivers are no longer a black box between the field and the ocean — they're becoming the modeled middle.

### What we thought we knew

The ERW story has always leaned on a clean pipeline: crush silicate rock, spread it on cropland, let it react with soil CO₂, and route the resulting bicarbonate through rivers to a >10,000-year ocean store. Recent reviews in [Nature Reviews Earth & Environment](https://www.nature.com/articles/s43017-025-00713-7) and estimates of [0.76–1.1 Gt CO₂/yr by 2050–2100](https://www.nature.com/articles/s44458-026-00034-w) have made ERW one of the fastest-growing CDR categories commercially — Lithos, Alt Carbon, InPlanet, Terradot, and CREW Carbon all sit somewhere on that pipeline. But field verification has been humbling: a [three-year ACS ES&T trial](https://pubs.acs.org/esthag/article/59/48/25751/3672562/Three-Years-of-Field-Trials-Indicate-a-Sustained) found removal rates under 10% of theoretical, and Project Drawdown has flagged monitoring as [a core open problem](https://drawdown.org/explorer/deploy-enhanced-rock-weathering). Most MRV attention has focused on the soil column and the export flux at the catchment outlet. What happens *inside the river* has been treated largely as conservative transport.

### What's new

Rebecca Neumann and colleagues push directly on that assumption in ["Photosynthesis in Rivers as a Loss Pathway for ERW-Derived DIC and Alkalinity"](https://cdrxiv.org/preprint/329). Their headline number: within a single stream-order segment, aquatic photosynthesis takes up **1–30% of the DIC delivered by flow** at most locations. The twist is that the *consequence* for ERW accounting depends on which membrane transport pathway aquatic autotrophs use and where the fixed carbon ends up — different combinations can knock down DIC alone, DIC and alkalinity together, or alkalinity alone. And because ERW pushes river pH and alkalinity up, it may itself stimulate the photosynthesis that erodes its ledger. That's a feedback loop nobody has been quantifying.

Xing Zhou and colleagues then take the baton at the coast. Their [high-resolution CROCO-PISCES simulations of the Mississippi–Atchafalaya plume](https://cdrxiv.org/preprint/408) test eight riverine alkalinity/DIC injection scenarios in the northern Gulf of Mexico. For pure alkalinity enhancement, oceanic CO₂ uptake efficiency lands at **58–85%**, with the higher end achieved under modest, summer additions when a shallow mixed layer keeps alkalinity near the surface. For the ERW-style case (alkalinity + DIC together), **12–15% of land-based sequestration re-vents** from the coastal ocean — a loss that's remarkably stable across scenarios. That stability is genuinely useful: it suggests coastal outgassing is a tractable, near-constant haircut rather than a wild card.

Arthur Vienne and colleagues, in ["River alkalinity enhancement for scalable and energy-efficient geochemical CDR"](https://cdrxiv.org/preprint/539), take a different swing: simulate dosing 149 major rivers with limestone or slaked lime via controlled reactor ponds. Their upper-bound estimates are **0.2–0.4 Gt CO₂/yr with CaCO₃** and **>1 Gt CO₂/yr with Ca(OH)₂**, at potentially **<$200/t CO₂**, net of life-cycle emissions. Because dissolution happens in controlled ponds before release, MRV becomes a metering problem rather than an inference problem — a direct answer to the verification headache that trials keep surfacing.

### Who this touches

The Neumann paper bears on essentially every terrestrial ERW deployer whose crediting model assumes near-conservative river transport — Lithos, Alt Carbon, InPlanet, Terradot, CREW Carbon. It's not a verdict against them; it's an argument that the aquatic-phase term in the MRV stack deserves its own model. The Vienne paper's reactor-pond RAE concept sits closest to CarbonRun, which already operates in the river-alkalinity space. The Zhou modeling is a gift to anyone — ERW or ocean alkalinity enhancement — who has to defend a coastal efficiency number.

### What I'm watching

Whether ERW registries begin to require an aquatic-loss term; whether Neumann's team or others can pin down the dominant transport pathway empirically in ERW-affected streams; and whether reactor-pond RAE finds a regulatory home now that direct ocean addition remains constrained by the London Protocol. The river just became interesting.
