<p>DAC&rsquo;s biggest cost isn&rsquo;t the fan. It isn&rsquo;t the building or the sorbent material. It&rsquo;s the heat — specifically, the heat required to <em>release</em> the CO₂ you just captured so you can regenerate your sorbent and do it again.</p><p>Standard amine-based systems need north of 100°C for that regeneration step. Which means you&rsquo;re burning energy — often natural gas — to run a machine designed to remove carbon. The irony writes itself.</p><p>That&rsquo;s why a paper out of Chiba University caught my attention. <a href=https://doi.org/10.1016/j.carbon.2026.121405>Kondo et al. in <em>Carbon</em> (254:121405, 2026)</a> describes a new class of CO₂ capture materials they call <strong>viciazites</strong>. The trick is in the molecular architecture: adjacent nitrogen groups in an N-N configuration that weaken how tightly the material binds CO₂. Weaker binding means you don&rsquo;t need as much heat to break it loose. The desorption threshold drops below 60°C.</p><p>That number matters because industrial waste heat — the thermal energy that factories, data centres, and other facilities currently dump into the atmosphere unused — sits right in that range. Sub-60°C desorption potentially means you&rsquo;re not burning extra fuel to run your DAC plant. You&rsquo;re plugging into heat that was going to waste anyway.</p><p>Energy costs represent somewhere between 40–60% of DAC operating expenses depending on the design. If you can cut that component significantly by switching from combustion heat to recovered waste heat, you&rsquo;ve addressed the biggest single lever in the cost structure.</p><p>A few caveats worth naming. This is lab-scale materials science, not an operating plant. The Carbon journal paper covers synthesis and characterization; it doesn&rsquo;t give you a levelized cost of capture. There&rsquo;s a long road between &ldquo;interesting sorbent&rdquo; and &ldquo;cost-competitive DAC facility.&rdquo; Materials that work beautifully at bench scale frequently run into durability, cycling, and scalability issues when you try to engineer around them.</p><p>But the directionality is right. The field has known for years that reducing regeneration temperature is one of the highest-leverage moves available for DAC economics. Most incumbent sorbents were designed for post-combustion capture at power plants — a different problem with different constraints. Building materials specifically optimized for low-temperature swing is the kind of first-principles work that eventually produces step changes.</p><p>Viciazites are early. Worth watching.</p>
