The rate of global warming has nearly doubled. A new study from the Potsdam Institute for Climate Impact Research (PIK) provides the first statistically significant proof that Earth warmed at roughly 0.35°C per decade between 2015 and 2025, up from about 0.2°C per decade during the 1970–2015 period. That's not a subtle shift. It's an acceleration that, if sustained, would push the planet past the Paris Agreement's 1.5°C threshold before 2030.
## Why it matters
Suspicions about accelerating warming have circulated for a while. But suspicion is not proof. What statistician Grant Foster and climate scientist Stefan Rahmstorf have done is strip away natural noise, including El Niño cycles, volcanic eruptions, and solar variability, to isolate the underlying human-driven warming signal. The result is unambiguous: the planet is heating up faster than it was even a decade ago. For anyone working on carbon dioxide removal, this finding sharpens the timeline. The window for deploying CDR at meaningful scale just got narrower.
## The details
Foster and Rahmstorf published their analysis in the journal *Geophysical Research Letters*. They used five separate global temperature datasets and adjusted them to remove the effects of known natural climate drivers. After that correction, the long-term warming trend stands out more clearly.
Even after stripping out El Niño's contribution, 2024 remains the warmest year since weather records began in 1850. That's notable because skeptics often attribute recent heat records to the strong El Niño event of 2023–2024. The data say otherwise: the underlying trend itself has shifted upward.
The researchers are blunt about the implications. "In the current political climate, it is entirely possible that warming continues at its rapid pace or even accelerates further," they write. Every tenth of a degree, they note, worsens the impacts of the climate crisis, from severe storms and flooding to heatwaves and droughts. They also warn that critical tipping elements in the climate system could be pushed past the point of no return.
## What's driving the acceleration?
The study deliberately does not answer this question. Foster and Rahmstorf focused purely on data analysis, not attribution. But they do mention one hypothesis being actively discussed in the scientific community: improved air quality.
This sounds counterintuitive. Cleaner air is good for human health, but tiny pollution particles (aerosols) in the atmosphere have a cooling effect. As countries reduce air pollution, particularly sulfur dioxide from shipping and coal plants, that cooling mask is removed, and temperatures rise faster. Researchers at the University of Leipzig have recently provided more concrete data on this effect. However, the PIK authors caution that large uncertainties remain about how much this aerosol reduction contributes to the observed acceleration.
The dominant driver, of course, remains the burning of fossil fuels: oil, gas, and coal. The researchers call for an accelerated exit from fossil energy as the most direct way to halt the rapid warming.
## What this means for CDR
Let me be direct: faster warming does not make CDR a substitute for emissions cuts. The PIK researchers themselves call for phasing out fossil fuels as quickly as possible. CDR exists to handle residual emissions that are genuinely hard to abate, not to give anyone a reason to delay decarbonization.
That said, an accelerating warming rate has real consequences for CDR planning. If the 1.5°C threshold is crossed before 2030 on a sustained basis, the conversation shifts from "staying below 1.5°C" to "returning to 1.5°C." That return trip requires net-negative emissions, which means CDR at scale. The faster warming proceeds, the larger the overshoot, and the more removal tonnage will eventually be needed.
This also raises the stakes for measurement, reporting, and verification (MRV) of CDR. If we're going to rely on removal to compensate for overshoot, the accounting has to be airtight. Sloppy credits won't cut it when the physics are moving this fast.
## Caveats
A few things this study does not tell us. It doesn't identify the precise mix of causes behind the acceleration. It doesn't project future warming rates with certainty, since the trend could plateau, continue, or worsen depending on emissions trajectories and feedback loops. And a decade of data, while statistically significant in this analysis, is still a relatively short window in climate science.
The aerosol hypothesis is plausible but unresolved. If reduced air pollution is a major contributor, that creates an awkward policy tension: cleaning up the air we breathe may temporarily accelerate the warming we're trying to stop. That's not an argument against clean air. It's an argument for cutting greenhouse gas emissions even faster to compensate.
What's clear is that the trend line has bent upward, and the researchers who found it are not optimistic about a reversal under current political conditions. The numbers are what they are. The question is what we do with them.
