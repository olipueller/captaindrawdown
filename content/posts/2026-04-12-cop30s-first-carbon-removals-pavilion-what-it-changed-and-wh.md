COP30 in Belém, Brazil hosted the first-ever dedicated Carbon Removals Pavilion inside UNFCCC climate negotiations, marking a turning point in how CDR is treated at the world's most important annual climate summit. The CDR30 Pavilion, initiated and led by the Negative Emissions Platform (NEP), delivered roughly 46 sessions and served as the launch site for the CDR Mutirão, a new multi-year platform designed to push carbon removal from concept to real-world delivery.
## Why it matters
For years, CDR has been a side conversation at COP events, scattered across panels and side rooms without a clear institutional home. Having a physical, dedicated pavilion inside the negotiation zone changes the dynamic. It makes CDR visible to delegates who might otherwise never engage with it, and it gives the community, from governments to investors to project developers, a single place to coordinate. That shift from peripheral topic to Action Agenda fixture is significant.
## What the Pavilion actually did
The CDR30 Pavilion ran about 36 in-person sessions plus 10 virtual sessions, a substantial increase in COP programming focused on carbon removal compared to previous years. NEP designed it as a shared meeting point for governments, scientists, civil society, investors, buyers, and project developers. According to NEP, the pavilion also drove cross-promotion with other thematic pavilions focused on science and policy, extending CDR's reach beyond its own walls.
One of the most concrete outcomes was the launch of the CDR Mutirão. This is a multi-year platform created by the Climate High-Level Champions, the World Business Council for Sustainable Development (WBCSD), and the Group of Negative Emitters (GONE). The Mutirão is structured around dedicated "launchpads" for coordinated action across governments, business, and hard-to-abate industries. The name itself, "mutirão," is a Brazilian Portuguese term for collective community effort, fitting for a COP held in the Amazon.
## Six takeaways from the sessions
NEP distilled the pavilion's programming into six key conclusions:
**1. CDR is now part of the COP Action Agenda.** The conversation has shifted from "should we do this?" to "how do we scale it?" More delegates and institutions engaged seriously with implementation questions than at any previous COP.
**2. The 2030 deadline is real.** A recurring message across sessions: without rapid progress in the next five years, CDR will remain too expensive and underdeveloped to scale along the pathways climate models require. The 2050 and 2100 targets everyone likes to cite depend on what happens now.
**3. GONE is gaining traction.** The Group of Negative Emitters, initiated by Denmark, is becoming a focal point for national and subnational coordination on negative emissions. Its membership is growing, it's moving toward a more structured secretariat, and it's carving out a clearer role connecting public actors with expertise and industry.
**4. Finance and carbon markets dominated the conversation.** Article 6 of the Paris Agreement, which governs international carbon trading, was on everyone's radar. Discussions focused on credible routes to mobilize finance for both engineered and nature-based removals while safeguarding environmental and social integrity. How voluntary markets and Article 6 mechanisms evolve will shape CDR investment for years.
**5. Technology sessions were practical.** Biochar drew notably high attendance. Enhanced rock weathering (ERW, the process of spreading crushed minerals to accelerate natural CO2 absorption) showed growing momentum. Bioenergy with carbon capture and storage (BECCS) and DAC continued to receive attention. Ocean-based CDR was flagged as needing more programming at future COPs.
**6. Transparency and co-benefits were consistent themes.** Across sessions, participants kept returning to the need for honest accounting and the importance of CDR projects delivering benefits beyond just carbon removal.
## Implications
The CDR Mutirão could matter more than the pavilion itself if it delivers on its promise. A multi-year platform with institutional backing from the Climate Champions and WBCSD gives CDR a coordination mechanism that persists between COPs. That's the perennial problem with COP outcomes: energy peaks during the two-week event and dissipates afterward. The Mutirão's launchpad structure is explicitly designed to counter that pattern.
GONE's growth is worth watching closely. Government-led coordination on negative emissions has been fragmented. If GONE can consolidate national commitments and connect them to private-sector capacity, it fills a gap that no other body currently occupies.
NEP has already signaled its intention to bring a larger Carbon Removals Pavilion to COP31 in Turkey, with expanded programming and wider participation.
## Caveats
A pavilion is infrastructure for conversation, not a guarantee of action. The real test is whether the Mutirão's launchpads produce measurable commitments, funded projects, and policy changes between now and COP31. The Article 6 discussions remain unresolved, and without clear market rules, private finance will stay cautious.
It's also worth stating plainly: CDR exists to address residual emissions that cannot be eliminated through decarbonization. Nothing about a dedicated pavilion or a new coordination platform changes the fundamental priority of phasing out fossil fuels. CDR is a complement to emissions reduction, not a substitute. Any framing that suggests otherwise, whether at COP or elsewhere, deserves scrutiny.
The pavilion proved CDR has a seat at the table. Now the question is whether the community can turn that visibility into gigatons.
