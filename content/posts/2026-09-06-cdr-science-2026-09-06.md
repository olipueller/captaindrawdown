Two preprints landed on my desk this week that I want to read side by side, because together they push on the same uncomfortable question: when we count carbon removal from biochar and enhanced rock weathering, are we drawing the system boundaries in the right place? Ayesha Ahmed and colleagues at Yale/Georgia Tech argue that biochar's habit of [trapping cations in its matrix](https://doi.org/10.70212/cdrxiv.2026533.v2) shaves — on average — 3.2% off effective CDR, with a tail running up to ~40% in their [companion framing](https://cdrxiv.org/preprint/533). And Tyler Kukla and coauthors at CarbonPlan, Georgia Tech, and Yale [model the carbonate-vs-silicate swap](https://cdrxiv.org/preprint/304) in agricultural ERW and find that plain old aglime can, under a range of realistic conditions, beat basalt on end-to-end carbon removed. Neither result overturns the pathways. Both sharpen the accounting.

### What was already on the table

The status quo for ERW is a field visibly maturing from optimism into hard evidence. The [Nature Reviews Earth & Environment review](https://www.nature.com/articles/s43017-025-00713-7) frames commercial scale-up as racing ahead of measurement, and a 3-year Illinois trial [reported in C&EN](https://cen.acs.org/environment/climate-change/enhanced-rock-weathering-co2-sequestration-climate-change/103/web/2025/12) found basalt removing less than 10% of the expected CO₂, while steel slag performed better in a [parallel Frontiers study](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/climate/articles/10.3389/fclim.2025.1657058/full). A recent [Nature paper on basaltic critical zones](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10936-3) showed that secondary minerals can incorporate base cations and attenuate alkalinity export — the same "where do the cations actually go" problem, one level up the watershed.

For biochar, most MRV frameworks focus on persistent organic carbon, treating the ash-borne elements as a nutrient story rather than a carbon-cycle story. Ahmed and colleagues' contribution is to say: those cations *are* part of the carbon story, because plants generated soil acidity to acquire them, and whether that acidity gets neutralized later depends on whether the cations come back.

### What's genuinely new

Ahmed et al.'s move is elegant. Plant uptake of Ca, Mg, K, Na (net of N and S anion uptake) leaves behind soil acidity that consumes bicarbonate alkalinity and drives CO₂ back to the atmosphere on re-equilibration. In the decay counterfactual, cations return, alkalinity is restored, and the perturbation reverses. Biochar delays or prevents that return by locking cations into its matrix — making permanent what would have been a transient CO₂ source. Across 85 observations with complete elemental data, the mean penalty is 3.2%; the upper tail (~40%) shows up when cations are treated as fully retained rather than released proportional to biochar degradation. That's a first-order correction, not a project-killer, but it's a real number that belongs in registries.

Kukla et al. push the ERW conversation in a direction the field has been circling but rarely models cleanly: carbonates weather fast, and in less acidic soils at lower application rates, liming can out-remove basalt. The catches are the ones you'd expect — fossil-carbon mobilization from carbonate dissolution, downstream losses, and where you draw system boundaries in space and time. Their punchline is that basalt responds more strongly to the standard levers (apply more, grind finer), so silicates win as you scale intensity, but carbonates win in the "gentle" regime that overlaps a lot of actual farmland.

### Who this touches

For ERW deployers — [Lithos](https://www.lithoscarbon.com/), [InPlanet](https://inplanet.earth/), [Terradot](https://terradot.earth/), [Alt Carbon](https://altcarbon.com/) on basalt; [CREW Carbon](https://crewcarbon.com/) and [CarbonRun](https://www.carbonrun.io/) on alkalinity in freshwater systems — the Kukla paper is a nudge to be explicit about the counterfactual against liming, especially on lower-acidity fields. For biochar sellers and registries (Puro.earth, Isometric, Riverse and the rest), the Ahmed work is an argument for adding cation/nitrogen bookkeeping to MRV. The secondary literature on biochar's [liming effect](https://doi.org/10.3390/agriculture16161767) and its [potassium exchange behavior](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/agronomy/articles/10.3389/fagro.2026.1880378/full) already treats these fluxes as agronomically important — Ahmed et al. are asking that they also be treated as carbon-relevant.

### What I'll watch

Three things. First, whether biochar registries actually incorporate a cation-adjustment term, and where they set the default between the 3.2% mean and the 40% ceiling. Second, whether ERW protocols start requiring an explicit carbonate counterfactual rather than a "no-treatment" baseline. Third, field data — the Illinois basalt trial and the Nature critical-zone paper both suggest that what leaves the plot and what reaches the ocean are two very different numbers, and the accounting frameworks are still catching up to that gap.
