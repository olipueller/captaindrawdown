The overshoot conversation keeps circling back to a hard question: if net-negative emissions arrive late or slow, is stratospheric aerosol injection (SAI) a reasonable bridge, and does *where* you spray matter? A new preprint from Jerry Tjiputra, Dirk Olivié, Jörg Schwinger and colleagues, [posted to EGUsphere on 16 September 2026](https://doi.org/10.5194/egusphere-2026-5188), takes that question into a fully coupled Earth system model and finds that deployment latitude reshapes the ocean response — particularly the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC) — even when the global thermostat lands in roughly the same place. That's the bit that caught my eye.

### What was already on the table

The framing around overshoot has hardened over the past year. Carbon Brief's [guest post on minimising overshoot risks](https://www.carbonbrief.org/guest-post-how-to-minimise-the-risks-from-overshooting-the-1-5c-limit) notes that nearly all remaining 1.5 °C pathways now imply a temporary exceedance, and UNEP's latest emissions gap messaging — [summarised here](https://carboncredits.com/unep-warns-global-warming-may-overshoot-1-5c-calls-for-faster-emissions-cuts-and-carbon-removal/) — argues the central task is keeping any overshoot *small and short*, with CDR doing the pull-down. SAI enters as a controversial shock absorber. [The Conversation recently called it the "Voldemort" of climate policy](https://theconversation.com/as-global-heating-heads-to-1-8c-is-it-time-to-consider-the-voldemort-of-climate-policy-290427); a [Frontiers in Climate risk-based piece](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/climate/articles/10.3389/fclim.2026.1872846/full) explores partial, seasonal, high-latitude deployments as a way to slow ice-sheet and permafrost loss with lower global footprint. And a [Boston Globe opinion piece from January 2026](https://www.bostonglobe.com/2026/01/10/opinion/climate-change-carbon-dioxide-removal/) pushes back that SAI risks becoming an excuse to defer the phase-out. That's the terrain.

Two things the field has generally agreed on: tropical injection is aerosol-efficient (longer stratospheric lifetime, stronger radiative leverage per Tg), and SAI does nothing about ocean acidification or the underlying CO₂ stock — CDR still has to do the cleanup.

### What Tjiputra and colleagues add

The [new simulations](https://doi.org/10.5194/egusphere-2026-5188) run an idealized overshoot with prescribed emissions and CDR, then layer SAI in three separate regions: the tropics, Northern Hemisphere mid-latitudes, and Southern Hemisphere mid-latitudes, each with three ensemble members. The underlying [dataset is archived on NIRD](https://doi.org/10.11582/2026.kymtop3b) at ~70 TB, which is a nice signal for anyone wanting to reanalyze.

The headline results, as I read the abstract: all three deployments hold mean warming below 2 °C, and — consistent with prior work — tropical injection is the most radiatively efficient. But the *climatic consequences* diverge by latitude, routed largely through AMOC evolution. That's the piece worth sitting with. It means "does SAI work?" is the wrong question; "what does each deployment geometry do to the ocean overturning, and how does that interact with CDR pulling CO₂ down on a different timescale?" is closer to right. The paper frames these as *limitations* of overshoot avoidance via SAI, not as an endorsement.

Alongside this, Niki Lohmann and Nico Wunderling (Goethe Frankfurt / PIK) released the [code for "Carbon dioxide removal decisive to limit tipping risks in global warming overshoots" on 19 August 2026](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.22011481) — a tipping-point emulator with overshoot trajectories. Same theme from a different angle: CDR carries the decisive load for tipping risk, and the emulator lets you probe how much and how fast.

### How the field is reacting

The secondary chatter I can see hasn't yet metabolized these specific papers, but the surrounding drumbeat — [Zero Carbon Analytics on overshoot costs](https://zerocarbon-analytics.org/science/beyond-1-5c-what-the-science-says-about-overshoot-and-what-comes-next/), the [UNEP coverage](https://carboncredits.com/unep-warns-global-warming-may-overshoot-1-5c-calls-for-faster-emissions-cuts-and-carbon-removal/), and the [open letter supporting applied-science testing of near-term cooling](https://www.cpegonline.org/post/open-letter-in-support-of-applied-science-testing-and-piloting-of-near-term-global-climate-cooling-a) — is converging on a "CDR is necessary but insufficient on the tipping timescale; SAI is under active study but not a substitute" position. Tjiputra et al. fit that mood: SAI can hold the temperature line, but the ocean doesn't care about global means.

### What I'll watch next

Three things. First, the AMOC signal across ensemble members in the full paper — how robust the latitude dependence is versus internal variability. Second, whether the Lohmann–Wunderling emulator gets picked up to stress-test SAI+CDR combinations, not just CDR alone. Third, whether the "high-latitude, seasonal" SAI framing from the Frontiers piece survives contact with fully coupled ocean responses like these. If deployment geometry really is the dominant knob for ocean side-effects, that reframes the policy debate away from "SAI yes/no" toward a much more specific engineering conversation — one CDR practitioners should be inside, not adjacent to.
