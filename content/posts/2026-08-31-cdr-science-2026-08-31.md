When three field studies land in the same window all pointing the same direction — that enhanced rock weathering (ERW) meaningfully raises yields for smallholder farmers in the Global South — I pay attention. Jordan and colleagues at Mati Carbon report a [median 24.7% yield increase and >20% household income gain across 216 rice farmers in 42 Indian villages](https://cdrxiv.org/preprint/502), backed by a [commercial-scale 2024 deployment across ~810 ha and 600+ farmers in Chhattisgarh showing a 22.9% median yield lift and ~$303/farmer revenue gain](https://cdrxiv.org/preprint/483). Meanwhile, Haque and colleagues at Flux report a [71% first-year and 79% second-year maize yield increase from a single 20 t/ha nephelinite application across 56 women smallholders in Kisumu County, Kenya](https://cdrxiv.org/preprint/410). Different rocks, different crops, different continents, same direction of travel.

## Where the field was

Until recently, most of the ERW evidence base sat in temperate row-crop systems or greenhouse pots. The [2025 Nature Reviews Earth & Environment review](https://www.nature.com/articles/s43017-025-00713-7) frames ERW as commercially fast-moving but still short on real-world data across pedoclimates. [Project Drawdown](https://drawdown.org/explorer/deploy-enhanced-rock-weathering) has long flagged co-benefits alongside the honest caveats: variable CDR effectiveness and hard measurement. That caution got sharper this fall when [C&EN covered a three-year U.S. trial showing CO₂ removal at less than 10% of expected rates](https://cen.acs.org/environment/climate-change/enhanced-rock-weathering-co2-sequestration-climate-change/103/web/2025/12), and a [Frontiers in Climate midwestern trial found detectable removal from steel slag but not basalt](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/climate/articles/10.3389/fclim.2025.1657058/full). The temperate CDR signal, in other words, has been wobbly.

What these new papers do is shift the conversation onto ground where the agronomic case may actually be stronger than the temperate one: warm, wet, weathering-favorable tropical soils that are also nutrient-depleted and farmed by people for whom a 20% income bump is life-changing.

## What's genuinely new

Three things stand out to me.

First, **scale and study design in real farms, not plots.** Mati's 600-farmer commercial deployment and 216-farmer paired-measurement study aren't demonstrations — they're production. Flux's Kenya trial uses paired control/treatment plots on each of 56 farms, which controls for the enormous farm-to-farm heterogeneity that has haunted smallholder ag research.

Second, **the persistence signal in Kenya.** A single 20 t/ha nephelinite dose that produces a *larger* yield boost in year two than year one is striking, and it's consistent with the slow-release nutrient story ERW proponents have told. Whether that's sustained cation supply, pH trajectory, silicon effects, or something microbial isn't nailed down — Haque and colleagues are candid that the soil chemistry data is "inconclusive regarding possible modes of action" — but the yield curve itself is the headline.

Third, **the finance architecture.** Mati's Chhattisgarh paper puts numbers on first-year weathering extent (τ = 0.27, ~1.6 t CO₂/acre initial CDR potential) and — crucially — argues that carbon revenue and farmer productivity gains are *economically separable*. That means climate finance can underwrite soil restoration without asking cash-poor farmers to co-invest. For a pathway whose scaling constraint has always been who pays, that framing matters.

## Who this bears on

Most directly, **Mati Carbon** and **Flux** are the authors here, so this is their evidence base. But the broader implication reaches every ERW company working tropical or subtropical smallholder geographies — **Alt Carbon** in India, **InPlanet** in Brazil — where the co-benefit story is central to the operating model. It's a different footing from **Lithos**, **Terradot**, or **CREW Carbon**, which are largely oriented to industrial-scale row-crop or waterway systems in wealthier geographies. And it sits alongside modeling work like the [Communications Sustainability piece estimating 0.76–1.1 Gt CO₂/yr potential by 2050–2100 with low- and middle-income regions doing much of the lifting](https://www.nature.com/articles/s44458-026-00034-w) — a projection that requires exactly this kind of ground-truth to be credible.

## What I'm watching

Multi-year CDR quantification on these same farms is the obvious next shoe. The yield data is compelling; the paired CDR measurement is what turns this into a stackable, financeable pathway rather than just a good agronomic story. I'd also love to see the Kenya persistence result reproduced across a third season and in a second agroecology. If both hold, the Global South smallholder case for ERW stops being a hypothesis and starts being a template.
