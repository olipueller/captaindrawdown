> Take on a podcast episode from **Climate Chat**, originally published Tue, 29 Se.
> Listen: <https://youtube.com/watch?v=bnI-2ckIDMQ>

**TL;DR**
- Thermodynamic minimum for direct air capture is ~20 kJ/mol CO2 vs ~700 kJ/mol released burning gasoline; current DAC runs 10–20x above that floor. Useful framing.
- Lackner's practical cost floor: $10–30/t (materials, water, electricity only); $100/t is the "economy survives" threshold. Cost-floor argument is the episode's strongest section.
- Learning rate is the make-or-break variable: 20% per doubling scales to gigatons, 10% fails. Rarely stated this bluntly.
- Policy ask: certificate of sequestration at the wellhead plus a reverse auction for government buying of historical emissions. Coherent, not new.
- Arcusa (ASU) finding cited: roughly half of Microsoft's carbon certificates are "orphaned" after 30 years. First time I've seen this number on a podcast.

Dan Miller hosts Klaus Lackner — the physicist who published the first technical direct air capture paper in 1999 and co-founded Global Research Technologies — for a structured walk through what he calls the myths about carbon dioxide removal. Episode: https://youtube.com/watch?v=bnI-2ckIDMQ. Topics are energy balance, cost floors, storage capacity, fossil-company moral hazard, scaling precedents, and the policy design Lackner wants. It's a long interview with a live Q&A; the substance is front-loaded.

The cost argument is the part worth your hour. Lackner reasons from bottom-up inputs rather than today's quotes: entropy of mixing is ~20 kJ/mol, air contact at windmill-equivalent capex works out to cents per ton, and sorbent regeneration is comparable to flue-gas capture. Summing only steel, plastic, water and electricity gives $10–20/t, which he calls the floor — "not in the $100 range." He then anchors $100/t as tolerable: a barrel of oil makes ~0.4 t CO2, so full cleanup adds ~$40/barrel, inside the historical price swing; for power it's 2–5 ¢/kWh, less than the spread across US states. His read on today's vendors is less charitable — "in the few hundreds to 1,000," with Climeworks "now 1,000 after they thought they were 700" — and he attributes it to copying flue-gas scrubber designs into a 0.04% regime.

The learning-rate point is the practitioner's takeaway. At 20% per doubling, a thousand-fold growth cuts cost 10x, so kilotons-to-megatons and megatons-to-gigatons each buy a factor of ten. At 10%, you need 100,000x growth for the same gain and "you don't have two factors 10 in that game." He notes coal plants learned ~10%, PV ~24%, and small mass-produced units outlearn monoliths — his 1 t/day unit sized "like a large car," 100 million of them for 36 Gt/yr, 10 million built annually against 100 million cars. On demand, he's blunt: the US merchant CO2 market is 7–8 Mt/yr and saturating it could get DAC near $100/t, but only regulation creates real pull. His mechanism is a certificate of sequestration tied to extraction, with a 50-year staggered-futures scheme to let today's capacity underwrite tomorrow's obligation, and a reverse auction with guaranteed offtake for government purchases of historical emissions. He admits every DAC company he's pitched disliked competing on price.

Durability gets a sharp pass. Lackner wants "tens of thousands of years," calls 100-year storage "kicking the can," and puts biochar in an awkward 500-year window. The Arcusa paper on orphaned Microsoft certificates is cited without a link; worth tracking down. On geologic capacity he's relaxed — even 1% of the resource is "staggeringly large" — while favoring mineralization long-term, citing [Carbfix](https://carbfix.com) as the culturally right model: a seller of sequestration not tied to oil. He discloses work with Heirloom on CO2-to-fuel for seasonal storage.

Context: Miller's earlier audio interview with Lackner (https://youtu.be/IRO9q8MuQms) covers the moisture-swing sorbent engineering this episode skips, and the takeback framing maps onto Oxford's carbon takeback obligation, which Lackner says mirrors his own 2000 paper. Compare his $30 floor against [Climeworks](https://climeworks.com) public pricing to see how far the gap is.

Useful for anyone building a DAC cost model or a buyer-side durability policy; skip if you already know Lackner's thermodynamics talk.
