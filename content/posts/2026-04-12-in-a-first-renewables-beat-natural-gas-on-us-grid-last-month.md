In March 2026, renewable energy sources generated more electricity than natural gas on the U.S. grid for the first time ever in a single month. It's a milestone worth pausing on, even if it doesn't mean renewables have permanently overtaken gas. The primary driver? A surge in solar capacity that has been building for years and is now large enough to reshape monthly generation totals.
## Why it matters
Natural gas has been the dominant source of U.S. electricity for roughly a decade, steadily displacing coal while fending off competition from wind and solar. Renewables overtaking gas for even one month signals that the installed base of clean generation, particularly solar, has reached a scale where seasonal conditions can tip the balance. For CDR, this matters because the cost and carbon intensity of the electricity powering direct air capture and other energy-hungry removal technologies is directly tied to how fast the grid cleans up.
## The details
March is a favorable month for this kind of crossover. Electricity demand tends to be lower in spring, which means gas plants cycle down. Meanwhile, solar output is climbing as days get longer, and wind resources in many parts of the country remain strong. Put those factors together with the massive buildout of utility-scale solar over the past several years, and you get a month where renewables, taken as a group, edge past gas.
Solar is doing the heavy lifting. The U.S. has added enormous amounts of solar capacity in recent years, and each new gigawatt compounds the effect. Wind, hydropower, and other renewables contribute too, but the trajectory of solar installations is what's bending the curve most visibly.
It's worth being precise about what "renewables" includes here. This category typically covers solar, wind, hydropower, geothermal, and biomass. Hydro output can vary significantly year to year based on precipitation and snowpack, so the exact margin in any given March will depend partly on water conditions. But the structural story is about solar panels, millions of them, feeding electrons into the grid at a pace that was hard to imagine a decade ago.
## What this changes
For the CDR field specifically, a cleaner grid is not a nice-to-have. It's a prerequisite. Running a DAC plant on electricity generated mostly from natural gas undermines the net carbon math. Every percentage point that renewables gain in the generation mix improves the lifecycle emissions profile of electrically powered removal technologies. March 2026 is one data point, but it sits on a trend line that points in the right direction.
This milestone also strengthens the economic case for co-locating CDR facilities with renewable generation. If you're building a DAC plant that needs cheap, clean power around the clock, the growing share of renewables on the grid, and the increasingly low cost of solar in particular, makes project economics more attractive. Developers can contract for renewable power at prices that would have seemed unrealistic a few years ago.
For policymakers, the crossover is a useful proof point. It demonstrates that the energy transition is not theoretical. Renewables can and do outproduce fossil fuels under the right conditions. The question now is how quickly those conditions expand from one favorable month to a sustained annual reality.
## The caveats
One month is not a trend. Gas will almost certainly reclaim the top spot in summer, when air conditioning drives peak demand and gas peaker plants run hard. Annual generation totals still favor natural gas by a wide margin. The structural shift to a renewables-dominated grid is underway, but it will take years of continued buildout, plus storage deployment, to make this kind of monthly crossover routine rather than exceptional.
There's also the intermittency question. Renewables generated more total electricity in March, but that doesn't mean they were generating more at every hour of every day. Gas plants still provide critical dispatchable power when the sun isn't shining and the wind isn't blowing. Until battery storage and other flexibility resources catch up, gas will remain essential for grid reliability even as its share of total generation shrinks.
And a necessary note on moral hazard: a cleaner grid does not give anyone a pass to slow down fossil fuel phase-out. CDR exists to handle residual emissions that are genuinely hard to abate, not to provide cover for continued reliance on unabated fossil generation. The fact that renewables beat gas in one month is encouraging, but the U.S. still burns enormous quantities of natural gas for electricity, heating, and industrial processes. That has to change on its own terms, independent of how much carbon removal capacity gets built.
## What to watch next
The key question is how soon this monthly crossover becomes a regular occurrence. If solar installations continue at their current pace and battery storage deployment accelerates, we could see renewables beating gas in multiple months per year within the next few years. The month to watch is the first time it happens in summer, when demand peaks. That would be a far more definitive signal than a mild March.
For CDR operators and investors, the practical takeaway is straightforward: the grid is getting cleaner, and the economics of powering removal with renewables keep improving. That's good news for a field that needs abundant clean electricity to work at scale.
