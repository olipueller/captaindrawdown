> Take on a podcast episode from **The CDR Policy Scoop**, originally published Wed, 30 Se.
> Listen: <https://shows.acast.com/the-cdr-policy-scoop/episodes/the-minister-betting-big-on-biochar-with-minister-peng-chi-m>

**TL;DR**

- Taiwan has set a 1 Mt biochar carbon removal target for 2030 — but no biochar crediting methodology exists yet and one plant is operating. Aspirational, not a roadmap.
- Minister Peng's math: ~1.3 Mt of Taiwan's ~5 Mt annual agricultural waste, ~500 pyrolysis units. Feedstock imports are politically and legally (Basel) blocked.
- Demand driver is the semiconductor sector; TSMC alone may need ~10 Mt/yr of credits. CCS at $300–500/t is priced out; biochar is the "now" option.
- Taiwan's ETS runs on the EEX platform and is being designed to accept biochar credits. Article 6.2 deals (Paraguay MOU) are the workaround for non-UNFCCC status.
- Useful as a case study in top-down CDR target setting before the value chain exists.

Eve Tamme and Sebastian Manhart host Taiwan's Environment Minister Chi-ming Peng on [The CDR Policy Scoop](https://shows.acast.com/the-cdr-policy-scoop/episodes/the-minister-betting-big-on-biochar-with-minister-peng-chi-m), recorded 28 September, two weeks after he announced a 1 Mt biochar removal target for 2030. The conversation covers the origin of the target, feedstock and pyrolysis capacity numbers, how the target plugs into Taiwan's carbon fee and forthcoming emissions trading system, and how a non-Party to the Paris Agreement intends to trade credits.

The most revealing part is how little sits underneath the number. Peng — a former meteorologist and entrepreneur — says he asked his ministry who owned biochar and was told it was Agriculture's problem; when he asked the Climate Change Administration about crediting, the answer was "nobody submit the methodology to us." Four months later he announced the target anyway, explicitly as a forcing device: "I use this number to push our government and also our industry." Today Taiwan has research demo sites plus one European Biochar Certificate-certified facility in central Taiwan pyrolysing storm-felled trees from semiconductor parks. Getting from there to roughly 500 units and a national methodology in four years is a policy bet, and he more or less says so.

The second substantive thread is the demand logic. Peng frames biochar as the removal pathway Taiwan can afford: he puts new CCS at $300–500/t and cites a survey figure of ~$150/t for CDR, maybe $100/t at scale. TSMC has signed an MOU with Formosa to capture LNG power station CO2 for Taiwan Strait storage around 2030, but the minister treats that as too expensive to carry the load. The intended business model is a carbon fee already in force, an ETS built on the European Energy Exchange ([EEX](https://www.eex.com)) platform that will "welcome the biochar" credits, and a government-convened "Biochar Taiwan Team" spanning ministries, academia and industry, with a national plan due within 12 months plus concessional bank finance. Feedstock is the constraint he's most candid about: 1.3 Mt of agricultural residue would have to be diverted from incinerators that are already at capacity, imports face public resistance and Basel Convention restrictions, and his hedge — export biochar-bearing construction products to justify importing waste — is speculative.

For context, Peng visited Ottmar Edenhofer at the Potsdam Institute ([PIK](https://www.pik-potsdam.de)) three weeks before recording and is openly modelling on Europe: the Carbon Removals and Carbon Farming Regulation (CRCF) as a methodology template, the European Buyers' Club as a demand model, and the EU ETS as an eventual linkage. He also flags that Article 6.2 implementation agreements with Paris Parties are the route to bring credits home despite Taiwan's exclusion from COP, with Paraguay as the first partner. Anyone tracking how CRCF methodologies travel outside the EU, or how sub-national and non-Party jurisdictions use Article 6.2, will want to watch whether the Taiwanese methodology ends up mirroring EBC or CRCF rules.

Worth an hour for biochar developers eyeing East Asian offtake, methodology bodies, and policy people interested in what happens when a target precedes the value chain.
