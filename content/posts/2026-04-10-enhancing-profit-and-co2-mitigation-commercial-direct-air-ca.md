DAC plants don't need to run 24/7 to be profitable. They might actually do better if they don't.

A new study from Fan, Dentzer, Glynn, Goldberg, and Friedmann models four commercial DAC configurations buying electricity from wholesale power markets while earning revenue from carbon credits (think 45Q or voluntary markets). The key finding: flexible operation that responds to power price volatility can significantly improve both profit margins and net CO2 removed.

This matters because DAC's electricity demand is enormous. A solid sorbent system might need 1,500+ kWh per tonne of CO2. If you're buying that power at flat industrial rates, your economics look one way. If you're strategically curtailing during price spikes and ramping up when wind and solar are flooding the grid with cheap electrons, the picture changes considerably.

The study also highlights something that should be obvious but often gets ignored in DAC discourse: the carbon intensity of your electricity source directly determines your net removal. Run a DAC plant on a coal-heavy grid at peak hours and you might be removing less CO2 than you think, or even going net-positive on emissions.

For the 159+ DAC companies we track in our directory, this is a practical design question, not an academic one. Do you build for maximum uptime or maximum flexibility? The answer increasingly looks like flexibility, which has implications for equipment sizing, capital costs, and where you site these plants.
