Three preprints landed on my desk in quick succession, and together they map the entire MRV terrain for enhanced rock weathering better than anything I've read this year. Two of them ([Suhrhoff et al.](https://cdrxiv.org/preprint/390) and [Rogers & Maher](https://cdrxiv.org/preprint/334)) stress-test the solid-phase soil mass-balance approach that most ERW protocols currently lean on. The third ([Muth, Boysen & Michel](https://cdrxiv.org/preprint/456), from Everest Carbon) validates a physical sensor that skips the soil entirely and measures alkalinity export directly. Read side by side, they crystallize a debate that has been simmering in the ERW community: what should we actually be measuring, and how confident can we be in the answer?

## What we already knew

ERW's climate case is real but contested. The [Nature Reviews Earth & Environment overview](https://www.nature.com/articles/s43017-025-00713-7) frames it as a promising gigaton-scale pathway, and a recent [Communications Sustainability paper](https://www.nature.com/articles/s44458-026-00034-w) puts the ceiling at 0.76–1.1 Gt CO₂/yr by mid-century. But field evidence has been thin and, in some cases, disappointing: a [three-year trial reported in C&EN](https://cen.acs.org/environment/climate-change/enhanced-rock-weathering-co2-sequestration-climate-change/103/web/2025/12) found removal rates less than 10% of expectations, and a [Frontiers in Climate field study](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/climate/articles/10.3389/fclim.2025.1657058/full) found detectable signal for steel slag but not basalt. Project Drawdown's own [ERW explainer](https://drawdown.org/explorer/deploy-enhanced-rock-weathering) flags "complex monitoring and measurement" as a headline barrier. The dominant MRV workhorse — element-element mixing models applied to topsoil samples, comparing an immobile tracer (like Ti or Zr) against a mobile cation — is elegant on paper but has always had a signal-to-noise problem in heterogeneous fields.

## What's genuinely new here

**Suhrhoff and colleagues** update the mass-balance framework with a correction I hadn't seen made explicit before: when feedstock dissolves, the immobile tracer in the remaining topsoil gets *enriched* because mass is leaving. Ignoring that enrichment systematically overestimates dissolution. They also argue that dissolution should be quantified from population means, not individual samples, and they provide a signal-to-noise analysis grounded in high-density sampling across five fields (0.6–19.2 samples/ha). The takeaway: mass balance can work, but only above threshold combinations of application rate, dissolution fraction, and sampling density.

**Rogers and Maher** arrive at the same terrain from a probabilistic direction and land somewhere more sobering. Using distance-based sensitivity analysis and simulations of composite sampling, they conclude that geochemical variance in real fields is likely too high for element-element mixing models to serve as effective near-term constraints on dissolution. That's not a dunk on the method — it's a call to size sampling plans to actual baseline variance, which is rarely characterized before deployment begins.

**Muth, Boysen & Michel** offer a different response to the same problem: don't fight the soil, measure what leaves it. Their Everest Pulsar sensor uses a weak-acid ion-exchange resin to accumulate total alkalinity in situ, with capture efficiencies of 98.9% (no soil) and >97.7% (with soil) over a 7-day continuous-flow column experiment, and digital readouts within -0.3% (SD 6.0%) of chemically recovered TA. This is a bench-to-column validation, not a field deployment, but it's the first quantitative validation I've seen of an in situ cumulative alkalinity sensor.

## Where this touches the companies

Mati Carbon is a co-author on the Suhrhoff paper, which tracks with their soil-based quantification approach. The broader mass-balance camp — where [Lithos](https://www.lithoscarbon.com/), [InPlanet](https://inplanet.earth/), [Alt Carbon](https://altcarbon.com/), and [Terradot](https://terradot.earth/) have all published or gestured toward solid-phase methods — inherits both the Suhrhoff refinements and the Rogers/Maher cautions. Meanwhile, [CREW Carbon](https://crewcarbon.com/) and [CarbonRun](https://www.carbonrun.io/) already work downstream in the alkalinity-export paradigm (river and wastewater systems), which is philosophically adjacent to what Everest is proposing for edge-of-field.

## What I'll watch next

Three things. First, whether registries update protocols to require baseline variance characterization *before* application, as Rogers & Maher effectively demand. Second, whether the immobile-element enrichment correction from Suhrhoff et al. gets adopted in already-issued credits or only prospective ones — that's a retroactive accounting question with real dollar consequences. Third, field deployments of the Pulsar or comparable sensors: column validation is a strong start, but the near-field zone in a working farm is a messier place than a 7-day flow-through rig. If direct alkalinity export can be made cheap and reliable, the whole MRV conversation shifts.
