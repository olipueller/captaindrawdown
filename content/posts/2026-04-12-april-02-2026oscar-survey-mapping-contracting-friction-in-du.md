CDR.fyi and OSCAR have launched what they say is the first survey focused entirely on contracting friction in durable carbon removal. The target: understanding why negotiating offtake agreements from scratch remains so costly, complex, and inconsistent, and figuring out what shared tools can actually fix.
## Why it matters
The CDR market has spent enormous energy on measurement, reporting, and verification (MRV), pricing transparency, and buyer demand signals. Contracting, the actual legal process of getting a deal signed, has been largely ignored. That's a problem. If every new CDR transaction requires months of bespoke legal negotiation, the market can't grow fast enough to matter. This survey is an attempt to finally put data behind what many suppliers already know from experience: closing deals is painfully slow.
## What the survey covers
The survey is a joint effort between CDR.fyi, the leading public tracker of CDR purchases, and OSCAR, the Open Standard Carbon Removal Agreement. OSCAR is essentially a template contract designed to reduce the overhead of negotiating CDR offtake deals from zero each time.
The survey itself is short, roughly five minutes, and targets carbon removal suppliers and project developers. It asks about:
- How long respondents have been active in the market
- How many transactions and counterparties they've worked with
- How long the contracting process typically takes
- What legal resources suppliers rely on
- Where the biggest negotiating challenges come up
- How the lack of standardized contracts affects their business
That last point is the most interesting. The survey isn't just asking "is contracting hard?" It's trying to quantify the cost of not having standards. How many deals fall apart because of legal complexity? How much do small suppliers spend on lawyers relative to their revenue? How often do buyers and suppliers get stuck on the same terms over and over?
## The problem OSCAR is trying to solve
Think about what happens when a CDR supplier signs a deal today. Unless both parties agree to use a standard template, every contract starts as a blank page. Each buyer has their own legal team, their own risk preferences, their own ideas about delivery terms, credit ownership, liability, and what happens if the supplier can't deliver the promised tonnes.
For large suppliers with dedicated legal teams, this is annoying but manageable. For smaller developers, it can be a dealbreaker. Legal costs eat into already thin margins. Negotiations drag on for months. And because every contract is different, there's no way to compare terms across the market or build institutional knowledge about what "normal" looks like.
OSCAR was created to address exactly this. By offering a standardized agreement that covers common terms, it reduces the surface area of negotiation. Parties can still customize where needed, but they start from a shared baseline instead of a blank page.
Since its launch, OSCAR has been adopted by a number of market participants, but the team behind it acknowledges there's a lot they still don't know about how suppliers experience contracting day to day. This survey is designed to fill that gap.
## What this could change
If the survey gets enough responses, the results could do a few useful things.
First, it would give the CDR market its first real dataset on contracting timelines and costs. Right now, most of what we know is anecdotal. Having actual numbers, like "the median CDR contract takes X months to negotiate" or "suppliers spend Y% of deal value on legal fees," would be genuinely new information.
Second, it could help OSCAR prioritize which parts of the standard agreement need the most work. If 80% of respondents say delivery risk allocation is the biggest sticking point, that's where the template should focus.
Third, and this is the bigger picture, it could help make the case to buyers that standardization isn't just good for suppliers. Buyers benefit too. Faster contracting means faster deployment. Lower transaction costs mean more capital goes toward actual carbon removal instead of legal overhead.
## Caveats
A few things to keep in mind. This is a self-selected survey of market participants, not a randomized study. The respondents will skew toward people who already care about contracting issues, which likely means the results will overrepresent the pain points. That's not necessarily bad for the purpose of identifying friction, but it means the findings shouldn't be read as a perfectly representative snapshot of the entire market.
Also, the survey targets suppliers and project developers. Buyer perspectives on contracting friction are equally important but aren't the focus here. A follow-up survey covering the buy side would be a natural next step.
Finally, standardized contracts are a tool, not a silver bullet. Even with OSCAR, complex deals involving novel technologies, multi-year delivery schedules, or unusual risk profiles will still require custom legal work. The goal isn't to eliminate negotiation. It's to make sure parties aren't reinventing the wheel on terms that should be routine.
The CDR market is still small enough that infrastructure decisions made now will shape how it operates for years. Getting contracting right, making it faster, cheaper, and more predictable, is one of those unglamorous but critical steps that determines whether the market can actually scale to the gigatonne level.
