ExxonMobil is pushing deeper into Gulf Coast carbon storage, advancing a large-scale CCS project in Southeast Texas with construction reportedly set to begin soon. The move extends what is already the most aggressive CCS build-out by any oil major in the United States.
This is a storage and transport play, not a removal play. It matters for the CDR field because the pipelines, wells, and pore-space leases Exxon is locking down along the Gulf Coast will shape the economics of every carbon removal project that eventually needs to inject CO2 underground in that region, including future DAC and BECCS (bioenergy with carbon capture and storage) developers.
## Why Southeast Texas
The Gulf Coast is the single most attractive CCS corridor in North America for three reasons: thick, well-characterized saline formations with high injection capacity, a dense cluster of industrial emitters (refineries, petrochemicals, ammonia, LNG), and a state regulator (the Texas Railroad Commission) that is seeking primacy over Class VI injection wells from the EPA. Exxon already controls a large pore-space position in the region after its 2023 acquisition of Denbury, which brought with it the largest CO2 pipeline network in the country.
A new Southeast Texas project fits that pattern. It gives Exxon another anchor point to aggregate industrial CO2, move it through existing and new pipeline segments, and inject it into dedicated storage.
## What we know, and what we don't
Based on the summary available, the project is described as large-scale, located in Southeast Texas, and nearing construction. Specific tonnage, customer identities, well count, and financial structure are not disclosed in the material we have. Readers should treat the scale claim as directional until Exxon publishes engineering details or files Class VI permit applications that become public through the EPA or Texas regulators.
What is clear is the commercial model. Exxon's CCS business sells carbon management as a service to third-party emitters. It has already signed offtake-style contracts with industrial customers including steel, ammonia, and industrial gas producers along the Gulf. Each new storage hub expands the addressable customer base within pipeline reach.
## Implications for CDR
Three things worth tracking.
First, shared infrastructure. If Exxon builds out injection capacity well beyond what its point-source capture customers need in the near term, that spare capacity becomes available to CDR developers. A DAC plant or a biomass carbon removal project sited near the Gulf could in principle pay a tolling fee to use Exxon pipelines and wells rather than permit its own Class VI well, which typically takes 4 to 6 years. That compresses project timelines meaningfully.
Second, pricing power. Whoever controls the pore space and the pipelines sets the floor price for sequestration services. If Exxon becomes the dominant storage operator on the Gulf, CDR project economics in that region will partly depend on what Exxon chooses to charge. Today, rough storage tolls are in the $15 to $30 per tonne range, but that is a function of early-market pricing and 45Q tax credit economics, not long-run competitive pricing.
Third, the 45Q question. The US 45Q tax credit pays $85 per tonne for industrial CCS and $180 per tonne for DAC with saline storage. Exxon's project, as a point-source CCS hub, sits firmly in the $85 bucket. That is a subsidy flow, not a carbon removal flow. It avoids emissions, it does not remove legacy CO2 from the atmosphere.
## The residual-only line
This is the part that has to be said plainly. Point-source CCS on a refinery or chemical plant reduces emissions from that facility. It does not remove CO2 already in the atmosphere. And it certainly does not justify expanding fossil fuel production.
CDR, meaning actual removal, exists to handle the small slice of emissions that cannot be eliminated any other way. It is not a permission slip for oil majors to keep pumping. An Exxon storage hub that captures CO2 from an ammonia plant is useful if the alternative is venting. It becomes a problem if it is used in corporate communications to suggest that continued oil and gas expansion is climate-compatible. It is not.
## What to watch
Concrete markers that will tell us whether this project is real and whether it matters for CDR:
- A filed Class VI permit application with specific injection volumes and formation targets.
- Named anchor customers and contracted tonnage.
- Whether Exxon opens its pipeline and storage network to third-party CDR developers on non-discriminatory terms, or keeps it as a captive asset for its own customers.
- Final investment decision timing and any federal loan or grant support.
Until those details surface, the useful read is this: the Gulf Coast CCS corridor is consolidating faster than most CDR developers have planned for, and the companies building the pipes and the wells today will be the landlords the removal industry negotiates with tomorrow.
