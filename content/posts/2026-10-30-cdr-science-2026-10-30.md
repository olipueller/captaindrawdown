Three papers landed within a week of each other this October, and together they sharpen a point I've been circling for a while: the enhanced weathering carbon ledger has two columns, and the inorganic one fills slowly while the organic one can drain quickly.

The headline result for me comes from [Jaeeun Sohng and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1038/s43247-026-04143-1) at UC Davis and Lawrence Livermore, published 2026-10-23 in *Communications Earth & Environment*. Over a 55-week microcosm, crushed olivine applied alone reduced total soil organic carbon, with losses concentrated in the particulate fraction. Olivine co-applied with glucose did something more interesting: at week 15 it suppressed microbial uptake of a ¹³C pulse and pushed respiration per unit biomass to the highest of any treatment; by week 55 the pattern flipped, and the Olivine10%+Glucose soils had the highest microbial biomass (1.2 mg C g⁻¹), the most new ¹³C in mineral-associated organic matter, and the lowest respired fraction. The surprise is where that new carbon ended up. NanoSIMS imaging showed ¹³C hotspots sitting on pre-existing organic and mineral microsites, with no co-location on Mg-rich olivine particles. Sohng et al. argue EW builds MAOM indirectly, through microbial processing under buffered pH, not by supplying fresh sorptive surfaces.

## What was already known

The MRV community has known for a while that weathering signals and carbon removal signals are not the same thing. Clarkson et al.'s 2024 review laid out the measurement challenge; Kanzaki et al. (2025) modeled soil cation storage as a primary control on removal timing, with lags of years to decades; and Vienne et al. (2026) reported substantial silicate dissolution with limited short-term DIC export. On the organic side, Vicca et al. (2022), Buss et al. (2024), and Steinwidder et al. (2025) had all flagged that SOC responses to rock dust could rival or exceed the inorganic signal. A 2025 Midwestern U.S. field trial in *Frontiers in Climate* found measurable CDR from steel slag but not basalt, consistent with slag's fast kinetics.

## What's new here

The other two papers extend both threads with concrete numbers.

[Malgorzata Rizzi, Elliot Chang, and Céline Pallud](https://doi.org/10.3389/fclim.2026.1964775), published 2026-10-26 in *Frontiers in Climate*, ran unsaturated columns of acidic North Carolina sandy soil amended with 16 t ha⁻¹ olivine under flow equivalent to 22 mm day⁻¹. About 2.5% of the olivine weathered. Potential CDR inferred from Mg release came to 115.2 kg CO₂ ha⁻¹; realized CDR from bicarbonate or DIC export was 11.5 kg ha⁻¹. A tenfold gap, in a soil with CEC of only 3.5 meq/100 g. Most weathered Mg sat in the upper half of the column on exchange sites. Bulk soil pH rose from 6.5 to 7.3 in the amended columns. DOC export increased roughly ten times more than DIC export did, and the top centimeter lost about 225 ppm TOC (not statistically significant, but directionally consistent with Sohng's olivine-alone result).

[Jasper Roussard and colleagues](https://cdrxiv.org/preprint/574) at Antwerp and ILVO, posted to CDRXIV on 2026-10-29, swapped in basic oxygen furnace slag at 16.8 t ha⁻¹ with and without two biochars in a seven-week leaching experiment. Slag weathered fast: soil pH hit 8.22, and inorganic C sequestration reached 0.050 t CO₂ per t slag, with roughly six times more carbon in pedogenic carbonate than in leached DIC. Biochar changed none of that. But SOC in slag+biochar columns was 10.2% (poplar) and 6.3% (MDF) lower than biochar alone, which the authors read as priming of native SOC — the biochars were 99.7% inertinite, so the lost carbon wasn't biochar. Once organic losses are included, the estimated net CDR is 0.06 t CO₂/t slag alone but −0.06 and −0.17 with biochar. Only 43% of leached Ca charge was balanced by DIC; DOC took much of the rest.

The common pattern across all three: cations mobilize before carbon does, retention delays the inorganic credit, and the organic pool responds immediately and sometimes negatively. Sohng's microcosms add the hopeful corollary that with sustained labile carbon the organic column can turn positive, but only after a year, and that short monitoring windows would have missed it.

## Who this touches

Rizzi et al. was funded by a donation from Eion, and Chang is now at Lithos Carbon; it's good to see a practitioner-adjacent group publishing a result that complicates cation-based crediting. For any program quantifying removal from soil cation mass balance, the Mg-versus-bicarbonate gap here is a reminder that those numbers are an upper bound on *eventual* removal. Roussard's slag work also speaks to the slag-based deployments the Midwestern field trial pointed toward: fast weathering and real carbonate formation, but an organic-side priming risk when stacked with biochar.

## What I'm watching

Whether Sohng's week-55 reversal shows up with compost or residues instead of glucose, and in the field. Whether the slag–biochar priming persists beyond seven weeks or is a transient. And whether crediting protocols start carrying a soil organic carbon line item alongside the inorganic one. On this evidence, they should.
