The recent MoU between India's Varhad Capital and UK-based Velocys to develop "Make-In-India, cost-competitive biomass-to-SAF projects" might, at first glance, seem primarily an aviation decarbonization play. However, for those of us deeply entrenched in the CDR space, this announcement holds significant latent potential and underscores critical trends we need to watch. Velocys, known for its proprietary Fischer-Tropsch (FT) technology, enables the conversion of syngas derived from biomass into sustainable aviation fuel (SAF). This is a well-established pathway for producing drop-in fuels with significantly lower lifecycle emissions than conventional jet fuel.

What makes this particularly interesting for CDR is the underlying technology and feedstock. Biomass gasification, a precursor to the FT process, inherently produces a concentrated stream of biogenic CO2. While the primary goal here is SAF production, the existence of this CO2 stream presents a clear opportunity for integration with carbon capture and storage (CCS) or utilization (CCU). If these "Make-In-India" projects are designed with CO2 capture in mind, they could evolve into significant biomass carbon removal and storage (BiCRS) facilities. The "cost-competitive" aspect is crucial here; if the economics of SAF production are strong, it could help subsidize the additional capital and operational expenditure of integrating CCS, moving BiCRS closer to commercial viability in a region like India.

The "Make-In-India" emphasis is also noteworthy. It suggests a focus on developing local supply chains for biomass, technology, and potentially even carbon infrastructure. India has vast agricultural resources, which, if managed sustainably, could provide a substantial feedstock base without competing with food security. However, scaling biomass supply chains reliably and sustainably is a perennial challenge for BiCRS and bioenergy projects globally. Careful attention to land use, water, and biodiversity will be paramount. From a policy perspective, while India is heavily investing in renewables, explicit CDR policies are still nascent. The development of these SAF projects could lay the groundwork, both technologically and infrastructurally, for future BiCRS deployments, perhaps incentivized by international carbon markets or future domestic policies that recognize negative emissions.

This partnership highlights a broader trend: many "decarbonization" projects, especially those involving biogenic carbon, are dual-use opportunities for CDR. We saw similar discussions around facilities like Drax in the UK exploring BECCS (Bioenergy with Carbon Capture and Storage). While the immediate focus is SAF, the CDR community should be engaging with projects like this early on. Encouraging the integration of capture technology from the design phase, even if not immediately deployed, can drastically reduce future retrofit costs and accelerate the deployment of large-scale, durable carbon removal. This isn't just about reducing aviation emissions; it's potentially about building the physical infrastructure for India's future carbon removal capacity.
