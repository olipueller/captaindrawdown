The UK Department for Energy Security and Net Zero (DESNZ) has launched a pilot program focused on developing non-pipeline methods for transporting captured CO2. The goal: connect industrial emitters that sit outside planned pipeline networks to permanent storage sites using rail, road, and shipping. It's a practical acknowledgment that pipelines alone won't reach every facility that needs to move CO2.
## Why it matters
Most CO2 transport planning in the UK and elsewhere has centered on pipelines. That makes sense for large clusters of emitters near coastlines or storage hubs. But plenty of industrial sites, and potentially future CDR facilities, are nowhere near those pipeline corridors. If captured CO2 can't get to a storage site, it doesn't matter how good your capture technology is. The molecule has to move.
This pilot signals that the UK government recognizes a bottleneck that the CDR and carbon capture community has been talking about for years. Transport infrastructure is the unsexy middle link in the chain, and it's often the one that gets the least attention.
## The details
The program is being run by DESNZ, the UK department responsible for energy and climate policy. The focus is on three non-pipeline transport modes: rail, road, and shipping. Each of these has different cost profiles, capacity limits, and logistical challenges.
Road transport of CO2, typically in pressurized tanker trucks, is the most flexible option. It can reach almost any site. But it's also the most expensive per tonne and the least efficient at scale. It works best for small volumes or as a bridging solution while other infrastructure is built.
Rail transport offers a middle ground. It can handle larger volumes than trucks at lower per-tonne costs, but it requires emitters or capture facilities to be near rail lines. The UK has a dense rail network, which could be an advantage here.
Shipping is the most interesting option for longer distances, especially for moving CO2 from inland or coastal capture sites to offshore storage locations in the North Sea. Norway's Northern Lights project has already demonstrated ship-based CO2 transport at commercial scale, so there is a proof of concept to build on.
The pilot is designed to explore how these modes can work in practice within the UK context, connecting emitters that fall outside the geographic reach of the planned pipeline clusters like those in the East Coast Cluster and HyNet in the northwest.
## Implications for CDR
This matters directly for carbon dioxide removal. DAC plants, bioenergy with carbon capture and storage (BECCS) facilities, and other CDR operations don't get to choose their location based solely on proximity to a pipeline. A DAC plant might be sited where energy is cheap and land is available. A BECCS facility goes where the biomass feedstock is. Neither of those factors necessarily correlates with being next to a CO2 pipeline.
Non-pipeline transport could unlock CDR deployment in locations that would otherwise be stranded. If you can load captured CO2 onto a train or a ship, the geographic constraints on where you build a removal facility loosen considerably.
It also has implications for the economics of smaller-scale capture operations. Building a pipeline to serve a single facility producing tens of thousands of tonnes of CO2 per year rarely makes financial sense. But a truck or rail connection might pencil out, especially if the alternative is not capturing that CO2 at all.
For the UK's broader net zero strategy, this pilot could help fill gaps in the transport network that pipelines can't practically or economically cover. It's the kind of infrastructure thinking that tends to get overlooked in favor of flashier announcements about capture technology or storage permits.
## Caveats
The summary available on this pilot is thin on specifics. We don't yet know the budget, timeline, which companies or research institutions are involved, or what specific routes or emitter sites are being considered. Those details will matter a lot in determining whether this moves from pilot to real deployment.
Non-pipeline CO2 transport also comes with its own challenges. Road transport adds truck emissions (unless using zero-emission vehicles). Rail requires scheduling and capacity on already-busy networks. Shipping needs port infrastructure for CO2 loading and unloading that largely doesn't exist yet in the UK.
Cost is the big question. Pipeline transport, once built, is cheap per tonne. Non-pipeline methods are more expensive on a per-tonne basis, though they avoid the massive upfront capital expenditure of laying pipe. The economics will depend heavily on volumes, distances, and how quickly the UK can standardize equipment and logistics for CO2 movement.
There's also the question of how this fits with the UK's existing carbon capture cluster model, which has been built around pipeline infrastructure. Non-pipeline transport could complement that model or could signal that the cluster approach has geographic limits the government is now trying to work around.
This is early-stage work. But the fact that a government department is formally piloting alternatives to pipelines suggests the UK is thinking seriously about the practical logistics of moving CO2 at scale, not just capturing it. For CDR, that's a necessary piece of the puzzle.
