Vancouver's Carbonyx just closed a $1.2 million CAD pre-seed round to commercialize an electrochemical device that mineralizes CO2 inside mining waste while pulling out saleable carbonates and silica. The pitch from co-founder and CEO Doug Pimlott is blunt: this is "a carbon removal company disguised as a materials company," and the strategy is to make CDR pencil out without leaning on carbon credit revenue.
## Why this matters
The carbon removal market has a revenue problem. Most early CDR companies depend on voluntary credit buyers, and that demand is concentrated in a handful of corporate purchasers. When one of them slows down, the whole sector feels it. Pimlott pointed directly to Microsoft pausing credit purchases as evidence that credit-only business models are fragile.
Carbonyx is part of a small but growing group of CDR startups trying to stack a saleable physical product on top of the carbon removal. If it works, the company can sell tonnes of carbonate and silica into existing industrial markets, with the CO2 storage as a co-benefit that gets credited or sold separately. That changes the financing math.
## How the technology works
The device came out of the Curtis P. Berlinguette Research Group at the University of British Columbia. It takes waste rock from mining, construction, or manufacturing and accelerates the rock's natural mineralization reaction using electricity and water. Mineralization is the process where certain rocks bind atmospheric CO2 into stable carbonate minerals. In nature it takes thousands of years. Electrochemistry speeds it up.
The output is twofold: solid carbonates that lock up CO2 for geological timescales, and silica, which Pimlott notes is used in "everything from tires to toothpaste." Both have established industrial buyers and reference prices, which is the part that makes the model less dependent on credit demand.
## The team and the money
Pimlott is a UBC post-doctoral fellow. His co-founder and advisor is chemist Curtis Berlinguette, who has previously founded cleantech companies Cura, Miru, and Sora Fuel. The technical bench includes Adrien Noble, formerly hardware director at BarrelWise, as head of engineering, and Mia Stankovic, a PhD electrochemist.
The $1.2 million CAD round was led by WUTIF Capital, a Vancouver angel group, with Spring Impact Capital, UBC Venture Funds, and undisclosed angels participating. WUTIF COO Aaron Stuart framed the appeal directly: a profitable byproduct is what you want when the credit market is shaky.
The capital will fund three technical hires and a demonstration unit roughly the size of a shipping container. Pimlott says that machine will move the company from kilogram-scale output to multi-tonne output. He's targeting something "resembling commercial scale" deployment within two to three years and megatonne extraction capacity within ten.
## What this changes
The interesting argument here is the one Pimlott is making about market structure, not just the chemistry. CDR pricing has been wobbly because demand is voluntary and concentrated. If a removal pathway can sell its outputs into industrial commodity markets that already exist, it gets two revenue lines instead of one, and the credit becomes upside rather than the entire business case.
Mining and construction waste is also abundant and often a liability for the companies producing it. A device that turns tailings or slag into a saleable product while sequestering CO2 has a built-in customer for the input side too. If the unit economics hold up at demonstration scale, this is exactly the kind of model that more CDR companies should be studying.
The team also says it wants to expand into critical minerals and rare earth elements over the same ten-year horizon. That's a logical adjacency given the chemistry, though it's a much harder market to enter than industrial silica.
## What to watch for
A pre-seed round and a shipping-container demo are early signals, not proof. The company has not yet shown public data on capture cost per tonne, energy intensity per tonne of CO2 stored, or the purity and pricing of the carbonate and silica it produces compared to incumbent suppliers. Those numbers will determine whether the materials-first model actually subsidizes the removal, or whether the removal still needs credit revenue to close the gap.
The leap from kilograms today to megatonnes in ten years is also enormous. Most mineralization startups have found that scaling electrochemical reactors, sourcing consistent feedstock, and qualifying byproducts for industrial buyers each take longer than projected. Independent measurement, reporting, and verification (the protocols that confirm how much CO2 is actually stored and for how long) will need to catch up to whatever Carbonyx claims for its carbonates.
One last point worth naming. CDR is a tool for residual emissions that cannot be eliminated through decarbonization. A more durable revenue model for mineralization is genuinely useful, but it does not change the basic rule: removal complements emissions cuts, it does not replace them. Carbonyx's framing of "fireproofing" CDR against market and policy volatility is the right instinct, as long as the volume it eventually delivers is added on top of aggressive mitigation, not used to justify slower mitigation elsewhere.
