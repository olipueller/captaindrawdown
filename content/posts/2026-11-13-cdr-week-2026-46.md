A model of enhanced rock weathering (ERW) in Ghanaian soils found that a median 84.1% of the applied charge weathered within 20 years while a median 0.0023% crossed the 1 m accounting boundary. Dissolved rock is not yet carbon dioxide removal (CDR), and three more ERW papers this week show how far the answer depends on what gets measured.

## Tropical soils: 84.1% weathered, 0.0023% exported

The [Frontiers in Climate paper](https://doi.org/10.3389/fclim.2026.1979636) ran a daily charge-conserving model across four Ghanaian climates, four tropical soil archetypes, five feedstocks and three hydrologic structures, 2,400 simulations in all. After 20 years, 48.8% of simulations exported no charge and 91.6% failed to reach 10% breakthrough. The authors condense this into a barrier ratio: initial proton demand plus exchange headroom, relative to the applied charge. The median ratio was 2.27, and no simulation above 0.865 reached 10% breakthrough. In 75% of matched contexts at least one feedstock pair showed zero export, so the feedstocks could not be ranked. Two caveats: this is a model, not a field trial, and the boundary shifted over longer horizons as exchange-stored charge was released. My verdict: in high-barrier tropical soils, characterize the soil before choosing the rock.

## Denmark: five years of field data

A [five-year field experiment in Biogeosciences](https://doi.org/10.5194/bg-23-7727-2026) applied Greenlandic glacial rock flour at 50 t per hectare to a sandy agricultural soil in Denmark. A soil mass balance based on magnesium depletion puts dissolution at 56% or more of the applied material. Initial CO2 uptake comes to 3.2 to 4.9 t CO2 per hectare once carbonate speciation in the acidic soil solution is accounted for. Treated plots also held significantly higher soil organic carbon stocks, equivalent to an additional 18.3 t CO2 per hectare, roughly four to six times the inorganic figure. Read next to the Ghana model, the 56% is a weathering number and the 3.2 to 4.9 t is the removal number.

## China's rice fields: yield is not removal

A [CDRXIV preprint](https://cdrxiv.org/preprint/583) modeling basalt-based ERW on China's acidic rice fields estimates rice yield gains of 4.61 ± 2.45% to 6.34 ± 3.98% from a mean soil pH increase of 0.68 to 1.02 units, worth a preliminary USD 3.56 billion a year. It also estimates 204.39 Mt CO2 over 35 years from 50 t of basalt per hectare every ten years where pH stays below 6.5. Yield benefits alone give USD 609 of gross agricultural revenue per tonne of gross CO2 sequestered. A [review led from Oak Ridge National Laboratory](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11157-026-09793-3) is the counterweight: it reports field-scale rates of approximately 0 to 9 t CO2 per hectare per year and rates soil pH changes and crop yield gains as less reliable indicators of CDR than alkalinity export or cation mass balance. A yield gain is a reason to spread basalt, not evidence of removal.

## Permanence gets a number

A [Nature Climate Change paper](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-026-02785-9) proposes carbon at risk, a probabilistic measure of the shortfall between contracted removals and realized storage, modeled on value at risk in finance. A single forest carbon project in California shows a 95% carbon at risk of more than 70% over 200 years, from Monte Carlo simulations driven by satellite fire data. Geological storage ranges from 0.15% for well-regulated offshore sites to 17% for poorly regulated onshore ones. The authors say early applications will rely on assumptions and abstractions. The 17% is the figure I would remember, because it shows geological storage is not a single risk class. A [CDRXIV commentary](https://cdrxiv.org/preprint/562) arrives at a compatible recommendation from the buyer side: pair temporary removals with investments in durable engineered storage, instead of treating every credit as one fungible tonne.

## Overshoot is official, removal targets are not

COP31 opened in Antalya on 9 November, the first climate summit since a United Nations Environment Programme report stated that overshooting 1.5 °C is inevitable, [Nature reports](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-026-03631-w). The same report confirms the need to remove hundreds of billions of tonnes of CO2 this century. Yet no country has included a clearly quantified removal target in its commitments to the United Nations, according to Steve Smith of the University of Oxford. For scale, the [CDR.fyi recap](https://www.cdr.fyi/blog/cdr-monthly-recap-october-2026) counts over 33,000 tonnes of durable CDR contracted in October, including Frontier's 20,932-tonne, $11.7 million offtake with 44.01.
