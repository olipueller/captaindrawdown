Three experiments on three very different coastlines point to the same reassuring pattern, with one clear boundary. At moderate doses, adding alkalinity to seawater barely disturbs the plankton. Push the dose high enough, though, and the spring bloom starts running late. The sharpest version of that boundary comes from Juliane K. Tammen, Leila R. Kittu, Markus Schartau, Ulf Riebesell and colleagues, in a [Communications Earth & Environment paper published 21 September 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s43247-026-03986-y). It caught my eye because it gives a number, roughly 900 µmol kg⁻¹, rather than a general caution.

## Where this sits

Ocean alkalinity enhancement (OAE) has long been framed as one of the more promising ocean CDR routes. GEOMAR [ranks it alongside artificial upwelling](https://www.geomar.de/en/research/fb2/fb2-bi/research-topics/tipping-points-2) as particularly promising, and the [Chemical Reviews overview](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/10.1021/acs.chemrev.5c00433) notes it could locally mitigate ocean acidification as a cobenefit. The open question has been ecological. A [new marine CDR report covered by Columbia](https://news.climate.columbia.edu/2026/09/21/new-report-ocean-carbon-removal-shows-promise-but-big-questions-remain/) and [WHOI](https://www.whoi.edu/press-room/news-release/mcdrreport/) this September summarizes the field as "promising, but big questions remain," and says significant scale-up is needed. Dose-response data from real plankton communities is exactly what that scale-up conversation lacks.

## The three experiments

**North Sea (Tammen et al., 21 Sept 2026).** This is a large experiment. The team ran twelve 6-m³ mesocosms for 39 days during the spring 2023 bloom in the German North Sea. They tested a calcium-based alkalinity gradient from ~0 to 1150 µmol kg⁻¹ under two dilution scenarios:

- **Immediate dilution**, standing in for well-mixed water.
- **Delayed dilution**, standing in for poorly mixed water.

The results:

- From ~0 to 700 µmol kg⁻¹, bloom and particulate organic matter dynamics were largely unaffected in both scenarios.
- At ≥~900 µmol kg⁻¹, bloom development was delayed by ~6–9 days, and particulate organic phosphorus dropped significantly in both scenarios.
- Particulate organic carbon and nitrogen fell only under delayed dilution.

That last point is the practical one for me. How quickly added alkalinity disperses changes where the threshold bites.

**NW Iberian upwelling (Fontela et al., preprint posted 2 Oct 2026).** Marcos Fontela, María Froján, Belén Arbones and colleagues at IIM-CSIC [sampled a Chaetoceros-dominated diatom bloom in the Ría de Vigo](https://doi.org/10.5194/egusphere-2026-5658) on 9 May 2025. They ran nine microcosms (two hydroxide-based OAE treatments plus a control, n=3 each) for 20 days.

- **The diatom bloom** collapsed from nutrient exhaustion in every treatment, regardless of OAE.
- **Small cells** were where the treatments differed:
  - Nanoeukaryotes and heterotrophic bacteria were unaffected.
  - Autotrophic picoeukaryotes were negatively influenced by alkalinization.
  - *Synechococcus* increased in the OAE treatments.

This is a subtler signal than a delayed bloom, and it appears in the size classes that bulk measurements tend to miss.

**North China Sea (Xin et al., preprint posted 3 Sept 2026).** Xiaoke Xin, Jihua Liu and colleagues at Shandong University [tested a ΔTA of ~500 µmol kg⁻¹ in eutrophic North China Sea water](https://doi.org/10.5194/egusphere-2026-5281). They compared CO₂-equilibrated and non-equilibrated additions.

- **Ecology:** Neither treatment significantly changed any of the measured plankton variables:
  - phytoplankton or microzooplankton abundance
  - chlorophyll a
  - taxonomic composition and biodiversity
  - particulate organic carbon
- **Chemistry:** The non-equilibrated treatment lost substantial alkalinity. The authors read this as reduced alkalinity retention, which matters for carbon accounting as much as ecology.

## What's genuinely new

To me, the most important contribution is the convergence across systems. A 500 µmol kg⁻¹ addition in a eutrophic Chinese shelf sea and a 0–700 µmol kg⁻¹ range in a North Sea diatom bloom both came back largely benign. The Iberian work shows that even when bulk biomass looks unchanged, the picoplankton can shift.

The Xin et al. study was also run under the OAE Pelagic Impact Intercomparison Project (OAEPIIP), which was set up to standardize microcosm protocols across ecosystems. That is what will eventually make cross-site comparisons like this one rigorous rather than impressionistic.

The North Sea result adds a timing dimension. Spring blooms anchor food webs, and earlier work flagged the North Atlantic spring bloom as [possibly the largest biological carbon sequestration mechanism](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2020/04/200401092615.htm). A [2026 JGR Oceans study](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1029/2026JC024018?af=R) emphasizes that shifts in bloom timing ripple into trophic dynamics. A 6–9 day delay therefore isn't trivial, even if the bloom eventually happens.

## How it's being received

The journal's own summary of Tammen et al. leads with "critical thresholds, beyond which phytoplankton bloom development is delayed." That framing is a threshold to design around, not a stop sign. I haven't yet seen broader discussion of the two preprints, which is unsurprising given they posted in September and October.

## Who this touches

These results bear directly on anyone adding alkalinity to coastal water, including hydroxide-based approaches like the Iberian treatments and OAE developers such as [Planetary](https://www.planetarytech.com/). I'm not aware of any company involvement in these studies. The relevance is practical:

- Dilution rate and peak local concentration near the discharge point look like the variables that decide whether a deployment stays in the "largely unaffected" range.
- Equilibration state affects how much of the added alkalinity sticks around.

## What I'll watch next

- **Doses between 700 and 900 µmol kg⁻¹ in the North Sea**, where the threshold actually sits.
- **Whether the picoeukaryote and *Synechococcus* shifts** seen off Vigo show up in other OAEPIIP sites.
- **Retention losses under non-equilibrated additions**, and whether those are tracked as closely as the ecology, since they affect both the risk and the carbon math.
