Three preprints landed on CDRxiv within months of each other, each tackling the same stubborn problem from a different angle: how do you actually *prove* that enhanced rock weathering removed CO₂ from a specific field in a specific year? Together they mark a real inflection in ERW's MRV toolkit — moving the conversation past "cations in, carbon out" first principles and toward operational frameworks that a buyer, a registry, or a skeptical scientist can interrogate. That's why this cluster caught my eye.

## What we already had

Until recently, most published ERW field evidence has leaned on solid-phase cation depletion or reactive transport modeling, and the field has been open about the gaps. The recent *Nature Reviews Earth & Environment* review of ERW scaling flags MRV as one of the central bottlenecks, and Project Drawdown's own explainer lists "complex monitoring and measurement" among the pathway's principal disadvantages. A 2025 Frontiers in Climate field trial noted that "despite a dramatic increase in academic and commercial research, there remain relatively few published examples of field evidence." And just this month, a three-year trial reported in *ES&T* found CO₂ removal rates less than 10% of modeled expectations — a reminder that the gap between predicted and demonstrated removal is still very much alive.

Into this landscape, three preprints:

## What's new in the cluster

**Lithos Carbon's mass-balance methods paper.** [Baum, Liu, Schacht, Schneider, and Yap](https://cdrxiv.org/preprint/379) lay out the mass-balance approach — tracking base cation stocks in soil over time to infer weathering — with unusual pedagogical clarity. They walk through experimental design, release a software package for uncertainty analysis and trial planning, and, most notably, argue for Bayesian inference as the natural statistical frame. Their example Bayesian model, fit to simulated data, recovers the ground-truth answer with an explicit uncertainty envelope. That last piece matters: uncertainty quantification has been treated as an afterthought in a lot of ERW reporting, and putting it at the center changes what a credit can honestly claim.

**Yale + NC State's direct gas-phase measurement.** [Milliken, Woodley, and Planavsky](https://cdrxiv.org/preprint/352) do something I hadn't seen in an agricultural ERW trial: they park continuous in-soil CO₂ sensors in a basalt-amended soybean plot in Southeast Virginia and watch the soil CO₂ profile in real time. They report a CO₂ flux reduction equivalent to **1.04 tCO₂/ha/yr**, concentrated in the growing season and pulsing after rain events. This is a fundamentally different observable than cations or DIC — it's the gas phase itself, measured where the reaction is happening.

**Eion's direct-measurement methodology.** [Mooshammer and colleagues](https://cdrxiv.org/preprint/309) — a team spanning Eion, with co-authors who've since moved to Lithos and Terradot — put forward a field-scale framework built on U.S. farmland and federal datasets, framing loss accounting for ERW by analogy to hydrologic losses in subsurface carbon reservoirs. It's an attempt to make ERW legible to existing carbon accounting conventions.

## Why it matters together

What's striking is the *complementarity*. Mass balance (Lithos) infers removal from the solid phase. Gas-phase monitoring (Yale) observes the atmospheric side directly. The Eion methodology tries to knit field measurement to registry-grade accounting. None of these on its own resolves ERW's MRV question, but read together they sketch a triangulation strategy — cations, gases, and downstream losses — that is much harder to argue with than any single line of evidence.

The company footprint here is unusually visible: Lithos authors the mass-balance work, Eion authors the direct-measurement framework, and the co-author trail touches Terradot. It's a rare moment where three of the pathway's most active developers (alongside Alt Carbon, InPlanet, CREW Carbon, and CarbonRun operating in adjacent variants) are publishing methods openly rather than treating MRV as proprietary moat.

## What I'll watch next

Three things. First, whether the Yale in-soil CO₂ approach reproduces across soils, climates, and feedstocks — 1.04 tCO₂/ha/yr from one soybean plot is a compelling proof-of-concept, not a general result. Second, whether Lithos's Bayesian tooling gets adopted (or adapted) beyond Lithos — the software being released matters. Third, how registries respond to the Eion framework, and whether the recent low-removal field trials get reanalyzed with these newer methods. If direct measurement and mass balance converge on the same numbers at the same sites, ERW's credibility problem gets a lot smaller.
