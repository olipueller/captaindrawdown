Brazil crushes roughly 600 million tonnes of sugarcane annually, generating about 180 million tonnes of bagasse. Most of it gets burned for energy. A new study by Sebastian Nosenzo on arXiv asks a sharper question: what if you pyrolyzed that bagasse into biochar instead, and does the math actually work?

The study uses scenario-based economic modeling to evaluate sugarcane bagasse biochar across agricultural productivity gains and carbon sequestration value in the Brazilian context. This matters because Brazil sits on one of the largest single-feedstock biochar opportunities on the planet, yet most bagasse biochar research has focused on agronomic or chemical properties, not unit economics.

What makes this interesting: biochar from agricultural residues like bagasse occupies a sweet spot. The feedstock is already aggregated at mills (no collection logistics nightmare), it's a genuine waste stream, and Brazil's tropical soils are often acidic and carbon-depleted, meaning biochar's liming and water retention benefits hit harder there than in temperate systems.

The BICRS sector (biomass with carbon removal and storage, tracked across 474+ companies in our directory, including firms like American BioChar Company and Milestone Environmental) needs exactly this kind of regional economic analysis. Too much biochar discourse is "it works in a lab" without "someone will pay for this at scale."

The real bottleneck for bagasse biochar in Brazil isn't feedstock or demand.
