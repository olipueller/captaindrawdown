**One figure, two ledgers.** Carbon-removal advocates say Canada could attract $8 billion in investment if its projects could sell credits abroad, per an analysis in [Canada's National Observer](https://www.nationalobserver.com/2026/10/09/analysis/canada-wants-export-carbon-credits-who-gets-climate-gains). The same analysis asks the question that decides whether that money is cheap or expensive for Canada: who gets the climate gains? Under the Paris Agreement's rules for internationally transferred credits (Article 6), a tonne sold to another country is generally a tonne the seller stops counting toward its own target. So the $8 billion and Canada's own climate claim draw on the same tonnes.

## The policy at a glance: Canada wants to sell credits across borders

The push covers trading both emissions reductions and removals internationally, not removals alone. The $8 billion is the advocates' estimate of investment that could follow, not a government projection, and the National Observer piece frames it as an open allocation question rather than a done deal. Who is bound: Canadian project developers, the federal government as the party keeping the national inventory, and the buying country. When it bites: at the moment a credit is used for a foreign claim.

## The mechanism: an exported tonne is a transfer, not just a sale

Think of two ledgers. A DAC plant or biochar producer in Canada removes a tonne. If Canada keeps it, the tonne sits on Canada's ledger. If a foreign government buys it for its own target, Article 6 generally requires a "corresponding adjustment," which in plain terms means Canada adds the tonne back to its own emissions so the buyer can subtract it. The cash arrives, the climate claim leaves.

That changes the economics for a developer in a specific way. The same physical tonne has at least three possible buyers with different counting rules: a Canadian compliance buyer (tonne stays home), a foreign government (tonne transfers), or a corporate voluntary buyer (no sovereign transfer at all). Price per tonne will not be the only term that matters. Who claims the tonne will be written into the contract, or it will be fought over later.

The voluntary channel already works without any of this machinery. Gevo sold and delivered 10,000 CDR credits through ClimeFi to a corporate buyer, per [Energy Digital](https://energydigital.com/globenewswire/3377243), with no sovereign transfer mentioned. That is the baseline. An export regime adds a layer on top.

## The current state: ambition on the record, counting rule not yet

The National Observer analysis makes clear the allocation question is unsettled. I have no source showing Canada has published a rule stating whether exported removal tonnes carry a corresponding adjustment. Until that rule exists, the $8 billion is a number without a denominator.

## What practitioners are saying: rules first, then trust

Other countries are writing national rulebooks. Octavia Carbon, a DAC developer in Kenya, framed [Kenya's new national carbon market guide](https://www.linkedin.com/feed/update/urn:li:share:7513920776677478401/) as the basis for buyer confidence: "trust is built on clear rules." That is a supplier-country voice saying the counting rule is a precondition for the money, not an afterthought.

Governments are also appearing as buyers. The UK Department for Science, Innovation and Technology bought £783,749 (about $1M) of Climeworks removal credits, per [Carbon Herald](https://carbonherald.com/uk-govt-invests-1m-in-carbon-credits-by-climeworks/?utm_source=rss&utm_medium=rss&utm_campaign=uk-govt-invests-1m-in-carbon-credits-by-climeworks). The source says nothing about whether those tonnes go on the UK inventory or stay on the host country's books. That silence is the point. A public buyer paid roughly a million for removals and the public record does not say which ledger they landed on. Canada's export framework would be selling into exactly that kind of deal.

## The next decision point: Canada's counting rule, date unknown

The sources handed to me give no date and no named decision body beyond the federal government. The decision itself is clear: does an exported Canadian removal tonne carry a corresponding adjustment, or not? If yes, Canada trades inventory progress for investment and must plan to replace those tonnes at home. If no, the buyer cannot use the tonne for a national target, and the "export" collapses into the voluntary channel Gevo already uses, which is a smaller and cheaper market.

Removals have one more constraint any Canadian rule should state outright. They are for hard-to-abate residual emissions, not a substitute for cutting fossil emissions, whether the tonne stays in Canada or moves abroad.

## What to track: whether the rule names the ledger

The leading indicator is a single sentence in any Canadian export framework: a statement of whether removal tonnes sold abroad are deducted from Canada's own accounting. If that sentence appears, developers can price contracts. If it does not, the $8 billion stays an estimate and every offtake deal carries an unpriced counting risk. Developers should write the claim allocation into term sheets now, before a rule is written for them.
