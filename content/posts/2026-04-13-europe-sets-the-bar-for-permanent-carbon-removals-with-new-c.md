The European Commission has adopted the first certification methodologies under its Carbon Removals and Carbon Farming (CRCF) framework, making the EU the world's first jurisdiction-wide, government-led certification scheme for CDR. The new standards cover three permanent removal pathways: direct air capture with carbon storage (DACCS), biogenic emissions capture with carbon storage (BioCCS), and biochar. And here's the kicker: the Commission plans to start buying credits itself once CRCF-certified supply becomes available.
## Why it matters
The CDR market has long suffered from a credibility gap. Buyers want to know that a removal is real, additional, and durable. Sellers want consistent rules so they can attract capital. Until now, the answer to both problems has been a patchwork of voluntary standards with no government authority behind them. CRCF changes that equation by creating a single, public, EU-backed framework that independent certifiers must follow. When a government of 450 million people says "these are the rules," it becomes a reference point that other jurisdictions can adapt rather than building from scratch.
## What the standards actually do
Published as a Delegated Act under the CRCF Regulation, the new methodologies tell independent certifiers exactly how to assess whether CO₂ removed from the atmosphere is genuinely permanent and sustainably captured. They apply to three pathways:
**DACCS** pulls CO₂ directly from ambient air and stores it geologically. **BioCCS** captures CO₂ from biogenic sources (think biomass combustion or fermentation) and locks it underground. **Biochar** converts biomass into a stable carbon-rich solid that can persist in soils for centuries.
For each pathway, the methodologies address the questions that have stalled market growth for years. Is the removal real and additional? Is storage durable in the way "permanent" implies? Does the activity avoid unacceptable sustainability trade-offs? Can a third party verify the claims consistently across the EU?
Chris Sherwood, Secretary-General of the Negative Emissions Platform, put it plainly: "As the world's first jurisdiction-wide, government-led certification scheme for CDR, CRCF will give investors and buyers greater confidence to back permanent removals."
## The Commission as buyer
This is the detail that should make project developers pay close attention. The European Commission has stated it intends to compensate its own residual emissions with high-quality carbon removals by 2030. It is exploring the feasibility of purchasing credits, potentially through a joint purchase platform. In its December 2025 progress review on "Greening the Commission," it noted that pilot purchases would only happen once CRCF-certified credits become available.
That sequencing is deliberate: standards first, then credible demand. It's the kind of signal that de-risks early-stage projects. If you're a DACCS developer trying to close financing, knowing that a sovereign buyer is waiting on the other side of certification changes your conversation with investors entirely.
A quick but important note: CDR is meant for residual emissions that can't be eliminated through direct decarbonization. The Commission framing it this way, as compensation for what remains after reduction efforts, is the right framing. Carbon removal should never become an excuse to delay cutting fossil fuels.
## What this changes for the field
For buyers across Europe, CRCF creates a common language. A DACCS credit certified under CRCF in Finland should meet the same bar as one certified in Spain. That comparability is essential for building liquid markets. It also gives corporate compliance teams something to point to when justifying removal purchases to boards and auditors.
For other governments, the EU has done the expensive, politically difficult work of writing the first draft. Countries considering their own certification frameworks now have a concrete template to study, critique, and adapt. That's far more useful than another set of voluntary principles.
For the voluntary carbon market, CRCF raises an interesting tension. Will voluntary standards converge toward the EU benchmark, or will we see a two-tier market where CRCF-certified credits trade at a premium? My bet is on convergence over time, but the transition could be messy.
## Caveats
Publishing methodologies is not the same as having a functioning market. The Negative Emissions Platform itself flagged several open challenges: verification capacity needs to scale alongside project pipelines, methodologies will need updating as real-world experience accumulates, and sustainability and permanence requirements must hold up in practice, not just on paper.
There's also the question of how quickly CRCF-certified credits will actually materialize. DACCS and BioCCS projects have long development timelines. Biochar is closer to market but faces its own measurement challenges around long-term carbon stability in different soil conditions.
And the Commission's pilot purchase program remains exploratory. "Exploring the feasibility" is not the same as a committed procurement budget. The market signal is encouraging, but it's not yet a binding commitment.
Still, the direction is clear. Europe has chosen to lead with governance and verification rather than aspirational targets. For a field that desperately needs trusted infrastructure, that's exactly the right order of operations.
