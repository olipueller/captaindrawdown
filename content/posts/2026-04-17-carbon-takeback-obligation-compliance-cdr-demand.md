# The Voluntary Carbon Market Cannot Scale CDR. A Compliance Obligation Might.
The voluntary carbon market has contracted roughly 44 million tons of carbon removal to date. The world needs billions of tons per year by mid-century. That gap will not close through corporate goodwill alone. A compliance-based mechanism called the Carbon Takeback Obligation, or CTBO, offers a structural alternative worth serious attention.
## The Voluntary Market Is Too Small and Too Fragile
Wil Burns of the Institute for Carbon Removal Law and Policy laid out the core problem in an April 2026 analysis. Microsoft alone accounts for about 80% of all contracted durable carbon removal volume to date. And Microsoft recently told several suppliers it is pausing purchases. When one buyer can reshape an entire market by stepping back, that market is not a foundation for gigatons of removal.
Even before that pause, projections showed voluntary buyers might retire only 30 to 50 million tons of carbon removal per year by 2030. That figure represents less than 5% of what IPCC scenarios say is needed by 2030 to stay consistent with 1.5 degrees Celsius. The lowest credible estimates call for four gigatons of annual removals by 2050. The central range across multiple studies is seven to nine gigatons per year. Some projections for the second half of the century reach twenty gigatons annually.
Voluntary demand is not on a trajectory to meet any of these numbers.
## What a Carbon Takeback Obligation Actually Does
The CTBO rests on a simple principle: if you take carbon out of the ground, you have to put it back. It targets the upstream segment of the fossil carbon supply chain, meaning the companies that extract oil, gas, and coal, plus cement producers. Rather than placing the burden on downstream consumers, it applies extended producer responsibility to fossil fuel emissions. This follows the "polluter pays principle" that already underpins many pollution regulations worldwide.
Here is how it works in practice. A government creates a storage certificate system. Extractors and importers of fossil fuels must either develop carbon removal projects that recapture and store a share of their emissions, or purchase certificates from others who do. Those certificates function as a tradeable asset, allowing regulated companies to find the lowest-cost compliance pathway.
Most CTBO proposals use a phased mandate. A typical design starts at 1% of emissions in the base year and ramps to 100% by mid-century. This phased approach matters for two reasons. First, CDR infrastructure is nowhere near the scale needed to handle 40 billion tons of unabated fossil fuel emissions overnight. Second, a predictable ramp sends investment signals that let capital flow into removal projects in an orderly way.
California's proposed Carbon Dioxide Removal Market Development Act follows this model. It would require companies covered by the state's cap-and-trade system to purchase negative emissions credits on a rising schedule.
## Why Compliance Demand Changes the Economics
The fundamental problem with voluntary markets is uncertainty. A CDR developer building a facility today cannot know whether buyers will show up in five years. That uncertainty raises the cost of capital and slows deployment. A compliance obligation creates guaranteed, growing demand. Investors can underwrite projects against a regulatory requirement rather than a forecast of corporate sentiment.
This distinction matters enormously for capital-intensive removal methods like direct air carbon capture and storage, or bioenergy with carbon capture and storage (where biomass energy generation is paired with CO2 capture). These approaches require large upfront capital expenditure and long payback periods. Without demand certainty, financing them at scale is extremely difficult.
Burns notes that pledges made by countries under the Paris Agreement already fall more than 50% short of what is needed to hold warming below 2 degrees Celsius, with a much larger gap for 1.5 degrees. Many of those pledges lack legislation or legally binding mechanisms behind them. A CTBO converts aspirational targets into enforceable obligations.
## A Critical Caveat
A CTBO is not a license for continued fossil fuel extraction. The obligation exists to address residual emissions that persist during the transition away from fossil fuels, not to justify slowing that transition. Burns emphasizes that the larger the temperature overshoot, the greater and riskier the CDR commitment becomes. Aggressive emissions reductions remain the first priority. CDR handles what reductions alone cannot.
## The Bottom Line
Relying on voluntary purchases to build a gigatons-scale CDR industry is like funding a national highway system through donations. The CTBO offers a compliance framework that creates durable demand, sends clear investment signals, and places financial responsibility on the companies whose products generate the emissions. The voluntary market proved the concept. Scaling it will take regulation.
