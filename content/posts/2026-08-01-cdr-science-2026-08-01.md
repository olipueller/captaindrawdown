Three preprints landed on CDRXIV that, read together, force a hard question I keep circling back to: does the CDR accounting stack we're building actually deliver the climate outcomes we say it does? Rebecca Neumann traces [how the "10 Gt by 2050" durable removal target lost its conditionality](https://cdrxiv.org/preprint/330). Alexandra Ringsby and coauthors show [how project-level crediting can book removals while leaving upstream emissions unattributed](https://cdrxiv.org/preprint/303). Bodie Cabiyo and coauthors quantify [how much near-term warming we add when CDR drawdown lags the emissions it's meant to neutralize](https://cdrxiv.org/preprint/302). Different entry points, same underlying concern: targets, attribution, and timing each contain hidden assumptions that, if left implicit, can turn earnest removal into weaker climate outcomes than the numbers suggest.

### What we already knew

The integrity conversation is not new. ICVCM's Core Carbon Principles, Sylvera's durability framing, and the ongoing LCA-vs-net-flux debate (see the [companion CDRXIV piece on accounting methods](https://cdrxiv.org/preprint/514)) have all been pushing on how to define a high-quality tonne. The status quo picture is roughly: pick a robust MRV method, price in reversal risk with buffer pools, insist on durability, and scale toward gigatonne-class deployment by mid-century as DOE and others have framed it. What has been largely missing is scrutiny of the *reference points* that sit above the methodology layer — the target itself, the atmospheric boundary of a project, and the temporal alignment between a removal and the emission it claims to offset.

### What's genuinely new here

Neumann's contribution is a piece of intellectual archaeology. She traces the 10 Gt yr⁻¹ figure across ~50 sources in five sectors and finds it originated in IPCC scenario ensembles where CDR magnitude tracked *near-term mitigation ambition*, not temperature targets. As ambition slipped in later scenarios, modeled CDR didn't slip with it — pathways kept emitting and kept removing. A single number above the ensemble median got extracted, propagated through NGO and industry channels, and lost its conditionality along the way. Lower-CDR pathways existed in the same ensembles; they just didn't travel. That's a warm, generous reframing rather than a takedown: the target isn't wrong so much as unmoored from the mitigation assumptions that gave it meaning.

Ringsby and coauthors name something practitioners have felt but not formalized: an "attribution gap" where credited removals coexist with uncounted upstream emissions. Their Objective Atmospheric Basis (OAB) is a technology-agnostic, mass-balance ledger — emissions as persistent liabilities, removals as assets, tracked in a single atmospheric reference frame. Applied to biochar feedstocks, they show system-boundary and allocation choices can flip a project between net removal and net emissions reduction. Critically, they estimate the gap could reach *gigatonne scale annually* in biomass-based CDR at maturity. That's the number that stopped me.

Cabiyo and coauthors take the timing question into FaIR and quantify it. Four archetypal lag structures — including cases where excess emissions precede drawdown — consistently raise near-term warming and delay neutralization, *even when cumulative removals equal cumulative emissions*. Under continuous deployment in a low-emissions scenario, some lag types raise the probability of peak temperatures exceeding 2 °C by up to 6% across 841 ensemble members. Cumulative tonnes are not the same as temperature outcomes, and they've put a number on the gap.

### Who this touches

The Cabiyo author list is notable — CarbonPlan, Stripe, Amazon Sustainability, Cal Poly Humboldt, Stanford — a coalition sitting at the interface of buyers, methodology developers, and academia. That signals the temporal-accounting question is moving from theory toward buyer-side diligence. The Ringsby framework, by design, is meant to plug into Article 6 and national inventories rather than compete with existing registries.

### What I'll watch next

Three things. Whether crediting standards (Isometric, Puro, and the EU's CRCF) begin formally distinguishing lag structures rather than treating all issued tonnes as equivalent. Whether OAB-style ledgers get piloted on biomass BECCS or biochar projects where the attribution gap is largest. And whether the field can hold onto Neumann's deeper point: that a target divorced from its mitigation context becomes a permission structure rather than a plan.
