The U.S. Department of Energy just restored roughly 2,000 awards that had been frozen or cancelled under the new administration. But don't mistake that for a full reversal. About 94% of the projects that were previously cut remain dead. Meanwhile, new research finds that REDD+ forest-protection credits overstated their climate impact by more than tenfold. Both stories share a common thread: the gap between what gets announced and what actually delivers measurable carbon outcomes is enormous.

## DOE Restores Awards, But Most Cancelled Projects Stay Gone

DOE confirmed it is keeping approximately 2,000 awards that originated during the Biden era. On the surface, that sounds like good news for CDR and clean energy projects that depend on federal funding. Look closer and the picture is far less reassuring. Roughly 94% of the projects that were cancelled in earlier rounds of cuts are not coming back.

For CDR specifically, this matters because many early-stage removal projects, including DAC hubs and other demonstration-scale efforts, were counting on federal dollars to bridge the gap between lab results and commercial operation. The awards that survived may be enough to keep some work moving. But the overall funding landscape for carbon removal in the U.S. is significantly smaller than it was a year ago.

The practical question now is which projects made the cut and which didn't. The DOE has not released a detailed breakdown. Until that list is public, CDR developers and their partners are operating with real uncertainty about whether their specific awards are among the survivors.

## REDD+ Credits Miss the Mark by 10.7x

A new study found that REDD+ credits, which are issued for protecting forests that would otherwise be cut down, overstated avoided deforestation by a factor of 10.7. In plain terms: for every ton of CO2 these credits claimed to prevent, only about a tenth of a ton was actually kept out of the atmosphere.

This is not a CDR story in the strict sense. REDD+ is an avoided-emissions mechanism, not a removal technology. But it matters deeply to the CDR world for two reasons.

First, corporate buyers who got burned by low-quality offset credits are increasingly turning to engineered removal as a more verifiable alternative. Every study that exposes inflated offset claims strengthens the case for CDR approaches where measurement, reporting, and verification (MRV) can be grounded in physical and chemical evidence rather than counterfactual baselines about what "would have happened" to a forest.

Second, this kind of finding reinforces why rigorous MRV is non-negotiable for any climate credit, removal or otherwise. CDR methods are not automatically immune to overcounting. Enhanced rock weathering, soil carbon, and even some biochar projects face their own measurement challenges. The REDD+ experience is a warning: if you build a market on shaky baselines, the science will eventually catch up.

The 10.7x overstatement figure comes from comparing credited reductions against observed deforestation rates in and around project areas. The methodology is straightforward, which makes the result hard to dismiss.

## What's Next

Two things to watch. First, whether DOE publishes a project-level list of surviving versus cancelled awards. CDR developers, investors, and state agencies all need that clarity. Second, whether the REDD+ findings accelerate the shift in voluntary carbon markets toward removal credits with stronger MRV. Early signs suggest corporate buyers are already moving in that direction, but the pace matters. Every quarter that low-quality credits circulate is a quarter where real atmospheric outcomes fall short of what the ledger claims.
