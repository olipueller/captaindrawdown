Carbon180, the Washington-based nonprofit that has shaped US carbon removal policy for years, has released what it calls the first Responsible CDR Framework, a set of standards meant to align carbon removal projects with community consent, equity, and ecosystem protection. The document tries to answer a question the field has mostly ducked: what does "good" look like when a project drills, spreads rock, plants trees, or pipes CO2 underground near someone's home?
## Why this matters
CDR is moving from pilots to permits. Projects are siting air capture plants, spreading crushed basalt on farms, and securing Class VI injection wells, often in communities that were never asked. Without shared norms for consent and environmental care, the field risks repeating the trust failures that have dogged fossil infrastructure and even some renewables buildouts. A framework from Carbon180, which sits close to US federal policymaking and to buyers, gives developers, funders, and regulators a reference point they did not have before.
## What the framework does
The document is pitched as a first-of-its-kind standard, drawing a line between CDR that simply stores tons and CDR that is accountable to the people and places where it happens. According to Carbon180, the framework aligns projects with three things: community consent, equity, and protection of ecosystems.
Translated into practical terms, that means developers are expected to engage affected communities early and meaningfully, share benefits with them rather than extract value, and avoid damage to land, water, and biodiversity in the process of removing carbon. It applies across removal approaches, from direct air capture to land and ocean-based methods, rather than singling out one pathway.
Carbon180 is positioning this as guidance the broader field can adopt, not a government rule. That is an important distinction. The framework has no legal force. Its weight will come from whether buyers, registries, and public funders decide to reference it in their own procurement and certification decisions.
## Implications for developers and buyers
For project developers, the framework raises the floor on what counts as a defensible community and environmental plan. "We talked to some neighbors" will not cut it against a published standard that spells out consent and equity expectations. Developers that already do serious community engagement now have a document to point to. Those that do not have a clearer target to hit, and a clearer reputational risk if they miss it.
For buyers, especially the corporate purchasers writing multi-million dollar offtake agreements, the framework offers a due diligence anchor. Frontier, Microsoft, Google, and Stripe-backed purchasers have all said they care about responsible deployment. A named external framework is easier to cite in a purchase contract than a bespoke internal checklist. Expect procurement teams to start asking whether suppliers conform.
For public funders, including the US Department of Energy programs that have directed significant dollars toward DAC hubs and measurement work, the framework is a ready-made reference for grant conditions and community benefit plan requirements. Whether any agency formally adopts it is a separate question.
## What to watch next
Three things will determine whether this framework matters or gathers dust.
First, adoption by registries. If Puro.earth, Isometric, Verra, or Gold Standard fold Responsible CDR criteria into their methodologies, projects will have to comply to sell credits. If not, the framework stays advisory.
Second, uptake by large buyers. A single named reference in a Frontier or Microsoft offtake contract would do more to change developer behavior than a hundred op-eds.
Third, community reception. A framework written to protect communities is only as good as the communities it actually protects say it is. Watch for whether environmental justice organizations and Indigenous groups endorse, amend, or reject the approach.
## Caveats
A few things the framework does not do, based on what has been shared.
It is not a certification. There is no auditor, no stamp, no enforcement. Projects can claim alignment without anyone checking.
It does not replace CDR quality standards around durability, additionality, or measurement, reporting, and verification (MRV, the process of proving how much carbon was actually removed and for how long). Responsible siting and storage permanence are separate problems, and a project can do well on one and poorly on the other.
It does not resolve the harder political fights. Whether a Class VI well belongs in a given county, whether ocean alkalinity enhancement should proceed in a specific bay, whether a biomass facility is genuinely additional — those decisions still require case-by-case judgment. A framework can structure the conversation. It cannot end it.
And the usual reminder. CDR, done responsibly or otherwise, is for residual emissions from genuinely hard-to-abate sectors. It does not justify slower fossil phase-out, and Carbon180's framework does not change that math. Responsible removal of a ton is still more expensive, more uncertain, and more community-intensive than not emitting the ton in the first place.
Still, if CDR is going to scale to the gigaton range that climate models call for, it will have to do so in places where people live. A published standard for how to do that, from an organization with credibility in the field, is a useful step.
