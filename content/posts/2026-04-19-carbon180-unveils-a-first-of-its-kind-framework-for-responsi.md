Carbon180 just published a 97-page framework for how CDR projects should be designed, built, and held accountable. The CORE (Community-Informed, Open Access, Reviewed, and Evaluated) Carbon Removal Framework is not a technical standard for measuring tonnes. It is a social, environmental, and governance standard for measuring whether CDR projects deserve to exist.

## What CORE actually says

The framework is built on two layers: 8 principles and 11 practices.

The principles: justice, equity, transparency, accountability, enforcement, inclusion, additionality, and net negativity. These are not aspirational words in a press release. Each one maps to specific failure modes that Carbon180 has documented across the CDR field.

The practices are where it gets concrete:

- **Knowledge Building and Management**: Communities must have access to information before decisions are made, not after. Data cannot be hidden behind NDAs or proprietary labels.
- **Community Agency Mechanisms**: Real decision-making power, not consultation theatre. Communities must be able to influence project design, not just comment on it.
- **Community Benefits Mechanisms**: Benefits agreements must be enforceable, not aspirational. They must be co-designed with communities, not drafted by developers.
- **Dignified Workforce Development**: Local hiring, fair labor standards, protections for undocumented workers. Not just "job creation" as a talking point.
- **Full System Carbon Accounting**: No narrow system boundaries that conveniently exclude supply chain emissions or land use change. If the full lifecycle is not net negative, the climate claim is not valid.
- **Monitoring of Carbon Storage**: Durability treated as ongoing probability of reversal, not a one-time threshold.
- **Environmental Health**: Air, water, soil, biodiversity, and habitat impacts assessed and monitored. CDR cannot add another layer of industrial burden to communities already carrying pollution costs.
- **Human Health and Safety**: Emergency preparedness, leak protocols, chemical exposure standards. Carbon180 specifically flags risks to workers and nearby residents from CO2 storage, chemical processes, and equipment failures.
- **Independently Verified**: Verification must be free of financial conflicts of interest. Developer-funded verifiers with opaque methods do not meet the standard.
- **Design for Additional Impact**: Co-benefits (soil health, biodiversity, water quality) must be intentionally designed, not assumed to happen automatically.
- **Enforcement Mechanisms**: If there is no way to hold a developer accountable when commitments are broken, the framework treats the commitment as meaningless.

## Why this matters now

Carbon180 names four systemic problems the CDR field must confront:

1. **Structural inequities** that determine who can participate. Many communities where CDR projects are proposed, including Tribal lands, rural areas, and coastal towns, lack the staff, funding, or technical capacity to evaluate proposals. Farmers are financially stretched, land prices are rising, and federal programs often exclude Traditional Ecological Knowledge.

2. **Governance failures** that concentrate power with developers. Community engagement is frequently one-way, timed to justify decisions already made. When communities are brought in after locations are chosen and contracts are signed, participation is performative.

3. **Environmental and health risks** that fall hardest on already burdened communities. Each CDR pathway carries specific risks: land-based approaches may alter soil biodiversity, ocean-based approaches interact with marine food webs, and technological approaches involve chemicals, energy, and underground CO2 storage. These risks are often undermeasured in carbon-focused monitoring systems.

4. **Climate accounting failures** that inflate removal claims. Narrow system boundaries, unrealistic baselines, inconsistently defined additionality, and uncertainty that is rarely disclosed. "These gaps make it difficult for host communities, policymakers, and the general public to understand the true impact of interventions," the report states.

## The honest assessment

This is the most comprehensive social and environmental framework for CDR published to date. The 97 pages are detailed, well-sourced, and specific. Carbon180 has done the hard work of turning principles into practices with concrete characteristics. They acknowledge that early-stage projects may not meet every standard immediately, but they are clear that the standard exists and should be used as a design guide from day one.

The critical question remains adoption. CORE is voluntary. No developer is required to follow it. The framework becomes meaningful only if credit buyers require it, if government agencies reference it in funding criteria and permitting, and if CDR registries incorporate its principles into certification standards.

Carbon180 shaped key CDR provisions in the U.S. Bipartisan Infrastructure Law and the Inflation Reduction Act. They have the policy credibility to turn this framework into something with real teeth. Whether they do depends on whether the CDR industry treats CORE as a benchmark or a shelf document.

One thing the framework gets right above all else: "The single greatest risk to carbon removal's success is the loss of public trust." Every DAC facility that ignores community concerns, every biochar project that overstates its climate claims, every enhanced weathering deployment that degrades local water quality makes it harder for the entire field. CORE is Carbon180's attempt to prevent that from happening.
