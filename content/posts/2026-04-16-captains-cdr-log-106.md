The CDR industry's most important pivot isn't happening in boardrooms reacting to Microsoft. It's happening in labs and concrete plants where CO₂ is becoming a building material, not a line item on a credit ledger.

Microsoft's decision to [pause all carbon removal purchases](https://carbonherald.com/microsoft-pauses-all-carbon-removal-purchases/) sent shockwaves through the voluntary carbon market last week. And the numbers behind that pause are brutal. As Rico Grimm noted: "36,4 Mio Tonnen hat Microsoft bei CDR-Startups bestellt. Geliefert: 91.000. Ein Viertel Prozent. Der Kapitalstrom versiegt jetzt." - Rico Grimm (@ricogrimm.de on Bluesky). Translation: Microsoft ordered 36.4 million tonnes. Companies delivered 91,000. A quarter of one percent. The capital stream is drying up.

That delivery gap should terrify every CDR company whose entire business model is selling tonnes-on-paper to a single mega-buyer. But here's what's easy to miss in the panic: the same week Microsoft stepped back, a different kind of CDR company stepped forward. And they don't need Microsoft at all.

## The materials pathway is having its moment

Consider what happened in the span of days. ETH Zurich published research on a [living construction material that captures carbon](https://carbonherald.com/eth-zurich-develops-living-material-that-captures-carbon/) using an enzyme that converts CO₂ into solid calcium carbonate. The material cures in hours, is strong, repairable, and recyclable. It's not a credit. It's a product you can hold in your hand and build a wall with.

Meanwhile, [CarbonCure was named Climate Technology Company of the Year](https://carbonherald.com/carboncure-named-climate-technology-company-of-the-year/), validating a business model we've been watching closely. CarbonCure injects CO₂ into fresh concrete during mixing, where it mineralizes permanently. The company earns revenue two ways: from carbon credits and from selling stronger concrete that uses less cement. That dual revenue stream matters enormously right now.

And in Africa, [Zen Carbon was selected for the Africa Carbon Removal Accelerator](https://www.linkedin.com/feed/update/urn:li:share:7449997888644034560/), embedding carbon removal directly into concrete production at the plant level. This isn't a lab curiosity. It's a company scaling mineralization in a region where construction demand is surging and concrete is the dominant building material.

The pattern is clear. CDR companies that turn CO₂ into something people already buy, concrete, building panels, mineral aggregates, are attracting recognition and capital at the exact moment that credit-only companies are losing their biggest customer.

## Why materials beat credits in a downturn

The fragility Microsoft exposed isn't just about one buyer pausing. It's structural. As we explored in [our analysis of why carbon removal needs more than trees](https://www.captaindrawdown.com/posts/why-carbon-removal-needs-more-than-trees/), CDR approaches that depend entirely on voluntary credit demand are vulnerable to shifts in corporate procurement, political sentiment, and verification disputes. When your revenue is a certificate, your business lives or dies by someone else's willingness to buy that certificate.

Materials-pathway CDR flips this. A concrete producer doesn't buy CarbonCure's technology because they want carbon credits. They buy it because it makes better concrete with less cement, which saves money. The carbon removal is a co-benefit, not the sole product. This means the demand signal comes from the $600 billion global concrete market, not from a handful of tech companies making voluntary climate pledges.

[Cella's new research and technology collaboration with TotalEnergies on in situ mineralization](https://www.linkedin.com/feed/update/urn:li:share:7449684774966837248/) reinforces this point. A major energy company is investing in turning CO₂ into stable minerals underground, not because the voluntary carbon market told them to, but because mineralization offers permanent storage with industrial applications. The capital is flowing from industrial logic, not offset logic.

Dirk Paessler put it simply: "This is how climate technology becomes part of everyday infrastructure." - Dirk Paessler (@dpaessler.bsky.social on Bluesky). That framing is exactly right. When CDR becomes infrastructure, it stops depending on the goodwill of corporate sustainability departments.

## The Darwinian sorting

What we're watching is a bifurcation. On one side: companies that sell only removal credits, dependent on buyers like Microsoft, exposed to the 0.25% delivery problem, and now facing a capital drought. On the other side: companies that mineralize CO₂ into products with independent market value, companies whose CDR revenue is a bonus on top of a functioning business.

This doesn't mean credit-only models are dead. Companies like Climeworks, which we covered in their [10-year partnership with Tapestry](https://www.captaindrawdown.com/posts/airmyne-eneos-dac-partnership/) and their biochar diversification strategy, have long-term offtake agreements and diversified portfolios that provide some insulation. And as we noted in our look at [how direct air capture can scale](https://www.captaindrawdown.com/posts/how-can-direct-air-capture-scale-to-deliver-real-carbon-remo/), DAC has applications beyond credits, including synthetic fuels and enhanced oil recovery alternatives.

But the Microsoft pause will accelerate a sorting that was already underway. Investors will increasingly ask: "If your biggest credit buyer disappears tomorrow, do you still have a business?" For CarbonCure, the answer is yes, because concrete producers need concrete. For Zen Carbon, the answer is yes, because African construction isn't slowing down. For the ETH Zurich spinout, the answer could be yes, if they can move from lab to market.

For companies whose only product is a tonne of removal on a registry? The answer is much harder.

## The tension worth naming

There's a real risk in this narrative. Materials-pathway CDR is not a replacement for dedicated removal. The world needs companies whose sole purpose is pulling CO₂ out of the atmosphere and storing it permanently, even if that removal has no secondary product. We need DAC. We need enhanced rock weathering, which we've explained in detail. We need ocean alkalinity enhancement, which is showing early results. These approaches exist to address hard-to-abate residual emissions that cannot be eliminated through decarbonization alone. They are not a license to delay fossil fuel phase-out.

The tension is real: the market is rewarding CDR that makes products while the planet needs CDR that just removes carbon. Both must survive. But right now, only one has a business model that doesn't collapse when a single buyer pauses.

## What to watch

The signal to track over the next 12 months is whether CarbonCure, Zen Carbon, and emerging mineralization startups can sign offtake agreements directly with construction companies, bypassing the voluntary carbon market entirely. If concrete producers start buying mineralized CDR technology on its own merits, not for credits but for better, cheaper materials, that creates a parallel demand channel for carbon removal that no single corporate buyer can switch off.

Microsoft's pause didn't kill CDR. It revealed which business models were built on sand and which were built on mineralized rock.
