<p>One of the most persistent arguments against direct air capture is the energy one: DAC is too energy-intensive, too greedy, to be a serious climate tool. It&rsquo;s repeated often enough that many people have accepted it as settled wisdom.</p><p>It isn&rsquo;t.</p><p>A study published in <em>Environmental Research: Energy</em> modeled exactly how much additional primary energy demand a serious CDR buildout would require to hit a 1.0°C temperature target. The answer: 12.0–37.5% additional primary energy compared to a baseline scenario without ambitious CDR.</p><p>That&rsquo;s the entire buildout. The full deployment of direct air capture at scale sufficient to keep warming under one degree. Between 12 and 37.5 percent more primary energy.</p><h2 id=context-matters>Context Matters<a hidden class=anchor aria-hidden=true href=#context-matters>#</a></h2><p>To be clear about what that number means: we&rsquo;re not talking about 37.5% more coal. The same energy transition that enables CDR to scale — cheaper solar, cheaper wind, hydrogen for hard-to-abate sectors — is exactly the infrastructure that would power DAC facilities. They&rsquo;re complements. DAC scales as renewables scale.</p><p>The figure also puts the concern in perspective against what we already accept without much discussion. Global gas flaring — burning off natural gas as a waste product of oil drilling — wastes roughly 150 billion cubic meters of gas annually. The energy inefficiencies baked into the current system are enormous. A 12–37.5% additional demand for a 1°C ceiling isn&rsquo;t a wild ask in that context.</p><h2 id=the-real-constraints>The Real Constraints<a hidden class=anchor aria-hidden=true href=#the-real-constraints>#</a></h2><p>None of this means DAC is easy. The actual constraints are deployment speed and capital, not energy physics.</p><p>Building DAC capacity fast enough matters. Supply chains for sorbents and contactors matter. Cost curves matter — current DACCS costs sit in the $300–1,000/tonne range, and the IEA&rsquo;s pathway to scale requires that to come down to roughly $100 by 2050.</p><p>These are engineering and financing problems. They&rsquo;re hard. But they&rsquo;re categorically different from &ldquo;it uses too much energy&rdquo; as a dismissal. One is a challenge to be solved through investment and scale. The other is a physics argument that doesn&rsquo;t hold up.</p><h2 id=what-the-study-covers>What the Study Covers<a hidden class=anchor aria-hidden=true href=#what-the-study-covers>#</a></h2><p>The modeling covers scenarios ranging from moderate CDR ambition (1.5°C compatible) to aggressive removal rates required for a 1.0°C target. The 12–37.5% range brackets that full spectrum. More aggressive CDR deployment sits at the higher end; scenarios where CDR supplements strong mitigation land at the lower end.</p><p>The conclusion isn&rsquo;t that DAC is easy or cheap. It&rsquo;s that the energy demand argument, stated in absolute terms, fails the basic test: it doesn&rsquo;t account for what energy system we&rsquo;d be drawing from when this infrastructure exists.</p><p>The dismissal dies on contact with the actual numbers.</p><hr><p><em>Source: <a href=https://bsky.app/profile/kevinleecaster.bsky.social/post/3mi2oywgzzk2v>Environmental Research: Energy study on CDR energy demand</a>.
