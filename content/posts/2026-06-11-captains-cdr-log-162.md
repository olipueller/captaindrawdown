## Why this matters now

The Greenhouse Gas Protocol is the accounting rulebook that decides whether a tonne of carbon a company buys can sit inside its emissions inventory or only outside it as a separate claim. That distinction sounds technical. It is actually the single biggest demand lever for durable carbon removal. On November 14, a senior scientist on the protocol's technical working group resigned in protest, and an obscure standards fight became the most consequential governance story in CDR.

## What is it?

The Greenhouse Gas Protocol, or GHG Protocol, is a voluntary corporate accounting standard. It defines Scope 1 (direct emissions a company makes), Scope 2 (purchased electricity), and Scope 3 (everything else in the value chain). Almost every corporate net-zero claim on Earth is built on it.

The current revision, called the Land Sector and Removals Guidance, will decide one specific question: can a company report a purchased durable removal tonne, say from direct air capture or enhanced rock weathering (the process of grinding silicate rocks to speed natural CO2 uptake), as a reduction inside its inventory? Or must it be reported separately, the way old-style offsets were? In-inventory reporting drives procurement budgets. Separate-claim reporting does not.

## Who's involved?

The protocol is co-managed by the World Resources Institute and the World Business Council for Sustainable Development. Around it sit three other rulebooks that are moving faster:

- The Science Based Targets initiative (SBTi), whose Net Zero Standard 2.0 draft is shifting toward removals-only neutralization.
- The European Commission's Carbon Removal Certification Framework (CRCF), which has already defined permanence for Brussels.
- National law, including Switzerland's new corporate CDR milestone mandate.

## What just happened?

A scientist on the GHG Protocol's technical working group resigned in protest, citing what they described as industry capture of the revision process. [Heatmap broke the story](https://heatmap.news/carbon-removal/greenhouse-gas-protocol-protest). The resignation forced a private fight into public view at exactly the moment Robert Höglund argued that SBTi's removals-only turn has effectively ended the traditional voluntary carbon market. If GHG Protocol follows SBTi, corporate net-zero spend gets channeled into durable removals. If it does not, the protocol risks being routed around by the EU's CRCF and national law.

David Ho ([@davidho.bsky.social on Bluesky](https://bsky.app/profile/davidho.bsky.social/post/3mnwc7br3we2l)) frames the obligation bluntly: "CDR is waste management and a bummer. Rich countries created this CO₂ mess and it's our responsibility to clean it up." The protocol governs accounting in exactly those rich-country jurisdictions. Glen Peters ([@glenpeters.bsky.social on Bluesky](https://bsky.app/profile/glenpeters.bsky.social/post/3mnxkwdihic2m)) adds the technical caution: atmospheric inventory uncertainty is "about 10%." The protocol is arguing over how to count things that are not yet measured with high confidence.

A reminder on framing: removals are for residual emissions only. None of this licenses delay on fossil phase-out.

## Open questions to track

- Does the Land Sector and Removals Guidance publish on schedule, or slip again?
- Does it permit in-inventory reporting of high-durability removals, or punt to a separate claims framework?
- Does its permanence definition align with the EU CRCF, or diverge and create two incompatible rulebooks for multinational buyers?
- Do Swiss-headquartered companies reconcile to national law or to the protocol if the two conflict?
