<p>🌍 From China: biochar&rsquo;s biggest limitation might have a cleaner solution than anyone expected.</p><p>New research from <a href=https://www.tsinghua.edu.cn/en/>Tsinghua University</a> identifies an approach that could simultaneously solve biochar&rsquo;s biomass supply problem and make it one of the lowest-cost CDR pathways available: <strong>growing bioenergy crops on China&rsquo;s abandoned agricultural land and converting them to biochar</strong>.</p><p>The numbers are significant. The research estimates this approach could support approximately <strong>25.8 million tonnes of CO₂ removal per year</strong> — roughly comparable to what biochar can currently deliver using agricultural and forestry residues, which are the conventional feedstock. This isn&rsquo;t a marginal improvement; it&rsquo;s a doubling of the potential.</p><h2 id=the-core-problem-biochar-cant-scale-without-biomass>The Core Problem: Biochar Can&rsquo;t Scale Without Biomass<a hidden class=anchor aria-hidden=true href=#the-core-problem-biochar-cant-scale-without-biomass>#</a></h2><p>Biochar&rsquo;s climate logic is elegant. Plants absorb CO₂ from the air as they grow. Pyrolysis converts some of that plant carbon into a stable, charcoal-like form that persists in soil for hundreds to thousands of years rather than decomposing back into CO₂. Net result: atmospheric carbon that stays out of the atmosphere.</p><p>The problem is feedstock. Most biochar today depends on agricultural residues (crop stalks, husks) and forestry residues (sawdust, bark). These are abundant in aggregate but diffuse — spread across landscapes, available in irregular quantities, often costly to transport. You can&rsquo;t just scale biochar indefinitely by collecting more residues without running into logistics ceilings.</p><p>This is the supply wall that has kept biochar from reaching its theoretical potential despite its cost advantages over DAC and ocean CDR.</p><h2 id=abandoned-cropland-changes-the-math>Abandoned Cropland Changes the Math<a hidden class=anchor aria-hidden=true href=#abandoned-cropland-changes-the-math>#</a></h2><p>China has substantial areas of abandoned agricultural land — formerly cultivated ground that&rsquo;s no longer in active food production for various economic and demographic reasons. This land is available, distributed through existing agricultural regions, and — crucially — <strong>not competing with food production</strong>.</p><p>The Tsinghua team&rsquo;s insight: if you grow <strong>bioenergy crops</strong> specifically for biochar production on this abandoned land, you create a dedicated, reliable, location-optimized biomass supply chain that doesn&rsquo;t depend on the vagaries of residue availability.</p><p>&ldquo;Our work shows that integrating bioenergy crops into the system can significantly expand its potential while keeping costs low,&rdquo; said corresponding author <strong>Wei Li</strong> of Tsinghua University.</p><p>The team modelled this against real-world constraints: the locations of China&rsquo;s existing biomass power plants, transportation networks, and realistic supply chain conditions — not idealized scenarios. The 25.8 million tonne estimate reflects what&rsquo;s actually achievable given existing infrastructure.</p><h2 id=the-co-benefits-stack>The Co-Benefits Stack<a hidden class=anchor aria-hidden=true href=#the-co-benefits-stack>#</a></h2><p>Biochar&rsquo;s appeal to farmers has always extended beyond carbon. It:</p><ul><li>Improves <strong>soil organic carbon</strong>, making soils more productive over time</li><li>Helps soils <strong>retain nutrients</strong>, reducing fertilizer requirements</li><li>In some contexts, reduces <strong>nitrous oxide emissions</strong> from agricultural soils — a potent greenhouse gas</li></ul><p>This means the economics aren&rsquo;t pure carbon play. Farmers and landowners who adopt biochar production on marginal or abandoned land get an agricultural improvement story, not just a climate story. That&rsquo;s the kind of double justification that actually drives adoption.</p><h2 id=where-policy-comes-in>Where Policy Comes In<a hidden class=anchor aria-hidden=true href=#where-policy-comes-in>#</a></h2><p>The study is candid about what makes this work at scale: <strong>carbon market incentives</strong>. Growing bioenergy crops specifically for CDR only pencils out if there&rsquo;s a reliable price signal. The 25.8 million tonne potential isn&rsquo;t self-executing — it requires a functional carbon credit market where biochar removal credits trade at prices that reward the investment.</p><p>China&rsquo;s national carbon market (ETS) has historically focused on emissions trading rather than removal credits. But international voluntary market demand — the same demand driving Microsoft and Google&rsquo;s purchases this week — creates a revenue pathway for projects that meet international verification standards.</p><h2 id=why-this-matters-beyond-china>Why This Matters Beyond China<a hidden class=anchor aria-hidden=true href=#why-this-matters-beyond-china>#</a></h2><p>China&rsquo;s agricultural footprint is enormous, but the concept generalizes. Other countries with significant abandoned cropland — including parts of Eastern Europe, sub-Saharan Africa, and Central Asia — could apply the same logic: dedicated bioenergy-to-biochar supply chains on non-food-competing land.</p><p>If the Tsinghua approach proves economically viable at scale, it could shift biochar from &ldquo;promising but constrained&rdquo; to a genuinely large-scale CDR pathway — one that&rsquo;s cheaper than DAC, more verifiable than many natural solutions, and buildable with existing technology.</p><p>The supply bottleneck has always been the limiting factor for biochar. This research suggests it doesn&rsquo;t have to be.</p><hr><p><em>Source: <a href=https://www.earth.com/news/biochar-could-unlock-low-cost-carbon-removal-using-abandoned-cropland/>Earth.com</a> | Tsinghua University research, corresponding author Wei Li</em></p><hr><h3 id=-related-reading>🔗 Related Reading<a hidden class=anchor aria-hidden=true href=#-related-reading>#</a></h3><ul><li><a href=/posts/nature-cdr-resource-limits/>New Nature Paper: Scaling CDR to Gigatons Could Strain Global Mineral and Nutrient Supplies</a></li><li><a href=/posts/biochar-nanotube-antibiotic-water-treatment/>Biochar Just Got Another Job: Destroying Antibiotics in Water</a></li><li><a href=/posts/cdr-census-geography/>The CDR Brain Map — Where the Researchers Are (and Aren&rsquo;t)</a></li><li><a href=/posts/why-carbon-removal-needs-more-than-trees/>Why Carbon Removal Needs More Than Trees</a></li></ul>
