## Japan's first offshore storage drill is a test of the CCS Business Act, not just the geology
Japan has approved the country's first offshore CO2 storage exploration drilling, off Kujukuri in Chiba prefecture. The approval is a step toward Japan's stated target of injecting 6 to 12 million tons of CO2 per year into geological storage by 2030, under the framework created by the CCS Business Act.
### Why this one well matters more than it looks
Japan is not a natural fit for onshore CO2 storage. The country is seismically active, densely populated, and short on the kind of large, quiet sedimentary basins that make places like the North Sea or the US Gulf Coast obvious storage candidates. That pushes almost any serious Japanese storage plan offshore, which in turn makes the Kujukuri exploration a live test of whether the geology off Japan's Pacific coast can actually hold injected CO2 safely and at scale.
The Kujukuri approval is also the first real exercise of the CCS Business Act, the law Japan passed to license and regulate CO2 storage operators. A law without a project is theory. Drilling a well under that law starts generating the permitting precedents, monitoring expectations, and liability norms that every future Japanese storage project will inherit.
### The 6 to 12 million ton target in context
Six to twelve million tons of CO2 per year by 2030 is a meaningful number, but it is worth sizing honestly. Japan emits on the order of a billion tons of CO2 per year from energy. A 6 million ton storage target is well under 1 percent of national emissions. The 2030 figure is a starter rung, not a climate solution on its own.
Where it matters more is in the industrial base. Japan has a heavy concentration of steel, cement, chemicals, and refining capacity clustered along its coasts. Those are hard-to-abate sectors where capture plus storage is one of the few routes to deep decarbonization. An offshore storage hub within pipeline or shipping distance of Chiba's industrial corridor is the kind of infrastructure that unlocks capture projects which otherwise have nowhere to send their CO2.
This is also where the CDR connection comes in. Storage wells drilled for point-source CCS are the same wells that can eventually take CO2 from DAC or BECCS. Japan's DAC ambitions, and any future bioenergy with carbon capture work, depend on having permitted, monitored storage sites that regulators and buyers trust. Kujukuri is upstream of all of that.
### What exploration drilling actually tells you
Exploration drilling at this stage is about characterization, not injection. Operators are looking for a reservoir rock with enough porosity and permeability to accept CO2 at commercial rates, a caprock above it with low permeability to keep the CO2 contained, and a structural setting that will not leak through faults or prior boreholes. They also need baseline data on pressure, salinity, and the surrounding geology so that later monitoring, reporting, and verification (MRV) work has something to compare against.
A single well near Kujukuri will not answer all those questions on its own. It will confirm or kill specific geological hypotheses about the site, and it will generate the data that decides whether a full-scale injection project gets built there or the operator moves on.
### Implications for the region
Japan, South Korea, and Singapore have all been circling the same basic problem: heavy industry, limited domestic storage options, and a growing appetite for cross-border CO2 shipping. If Kujukuri proves up as a viable storage site, Japan gains leverage in that regional picture. It reduces the country's reliance on exporting CO2 to storage hubs in Southeast Asia or Australia, and it gives Japanese emitters a domestic option that is easier to regulate and verify.
For CDR specifically, a functioning Japanese storage site would let Japanese buyers credibly contract for removal tonnage that is stored under Japanese law, by Japanese operators, inside Japan's own monitoring regime. That matters for corporate procurement and for any future compliance market that cares about jurisdictional accounting.
### Caveats worth keeping in mind
A few things this approval does not prove.
It does not prove the geology works. Exploration wells regularly come back with disappointing results, and the Kujukuri site still has to clear those tests.
It does not prove the economics. Offshore storage is expensive, and Japan's cost base for CCS is not yet benchmarked against mature projects in Norway or the Gulf of Mexico. Until injection rates, well costs, and monitoring costs are known, the per-ton storage cost in Japan is an estimate, not a number.
It does not replace emissions cuts. Storing 6 to 12 million tons per year leaves the overwhelming majority of Japan's emissions to be addressed by efficiency, electrification, and a shift off fossil fuels. CCS and CDR are tools for the residual and hard-to-abate fraction. Treating an offshore storage approval as cover to slow that broader transition would be a serious misread.
The honest framing is narrower and more useful. Japan just started drilling the first well under a law that, until now, had no projects to govern. What happens at Kujukuri over the next couple of years will shape whether Japan's 2030 storage target is a real plan or a slide in a strategy document.
