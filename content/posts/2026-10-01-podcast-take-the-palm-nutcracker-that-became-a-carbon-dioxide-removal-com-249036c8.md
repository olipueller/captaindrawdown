> Take on a podcast episode from **Reversing Climate Change**, originally published Thu, 24 Se.
> Listen: <https://podcasters.spotify.com/pod/show/reversingclimatechange/episodes/The-palm-nutcracker-that-became-a-carbon-dioxide-removal-companyw-Uzoma-Ayogu-of-Biochar-Industrial-Group-e3p96kt>

**TL;DR**

- BIG's model: don't build greenfield biochar plants; bolt pyrolysis onto existing agroprocessors who already have feedstock, permits, weighbridges and farmer trust. Sound framing.
- Value split is 20/60/20 of carbon *profit* (BIG / partner / capex funder); 80/20 early when BIG carries the risk. Rare to hear the terms stated.
- Target fully loaded removal cost of $50–70/t. Stated as a goal, not a demonstrated number — treat accordingly.
- Pyrolyzer uptime went 38% → 87% in a year; two larger systems due end-2026 and Q1 2027.
- Cassava trials: 21–50% yield gains on acidic soils (pH 4.5–5.9). Promising but small, self-reported, and only ~20% of physical biochar sold so far.

Ross Kenyon hosts Uzoma Ayogu, CTO of Nigerian palm-kernel processor Releaf and cofounder of its spinout Biochar Industrial Group (BIG), which just closed a pre-seed round. [The episode](https://podcasters.spotify.com/pod/show/reversingclimatechange/episodes/The-palm-nutcracker-that-became-a-carbon-dioxide-removal-companyw-Uzoma-Ayogu-of-Biochar-Industrial-Group-e3p96kt) traces how a decade-old agri-supply-chain business ended up issuing Nigeria's first industrial biochar credits, why the carbon dioxide removal (CDR) arm had to be a separate entity, and what BIG now offers other West African processors.

The structural argument is the useful part. Releaf's existing investors "understand ag, understand supply chains," but the voluntary carbon market (VCM) was a risk profile they hadn't signed up for, so scaling biochar inside Releaf stalled. BIG was created with a separate board, an arms-length SPV, and a commercial agreement identical to what it offers any third-party processor. Ayogu points to Exomad and NetZero as precedent — large operating businesses with CDR layered on top. His thesis is that biochar scales only on top of existing physical, regulatory and *social* infrastructure: a processor that already buys the farmer's crop and employs their relatives can get a 50 kg trial plot approved where a stranger cannot. The sales pitch to processors deliberately skips registries and audits: "we can make your residue an exportable product you get paid USD for."

On economics, the hurdle rate is capex payback within 24 months, profit growth over revenue growth, and projects that must stand on carbon revenue alone before agronomic demand is counted. Diligence on partners is counterfactual-first: is the residue genuinely underutilized, is feedstock traceable, is supply seasonal. Timber residue is off the table because Nigeria has no Forest Stewardship Council (FSC) certification. Pipeline partners are sized at 20,000–60,000 t/yr each; Ayogu notes the ninth-largest CDR supplier by deliveries sits around 30,000 t, so a handful of large hosts could matter. The remote-location inversion is worth noting: strong monitoring, reporting and verification (MRV) turns distance-from-port from a liability into irrelevance, since the exported product is digital.

Context: BIG grew out of the AirMiners Launchpad, and Milkywire was the first credit buyer — Kenyon calls a Milkywire sign-off the best single quality heuristic in CDR, which is roughly right given Robert Höglund's track record of public critiques. The processor-adjacent model mirrors what Captain Drawdown has previously flagged in Exomad's forestry-residue plays and NetZero's coffee-husk operations, and Ayogu cites the new mobile-unit methodologies from [Isometric](https://isometric.com) and Rainbow as what makes rice-husk and cocoa-husk projects viable later. One partner in northern Nigeria runs fertilizer programmes for ~500,000 farmers; BIG's proposed deal there is fertilizer-savings-sharing, which is a clever way to dodge last-mile application costs that would otherwise erode thin margins.

Useful for biochar developers weighing greenfield vs. host-site models, buyers diligencing West African supply, and anyone structuring a CDR spinout from an operating business.
