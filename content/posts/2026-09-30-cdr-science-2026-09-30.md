Three papers landed within about a week of each other this September, and together they make a stronger case than any one of them could alone. Adding alkalinity to land and rivers removes CO2, and it shows up in the field at scales from a single river to the continent's largest basin. What caught my eye is the range. One study covers a century of data, one covers a six-month growing season, and one covers a 98-day river trial. All three point the same direction.

## Where the field stood

Enhanced rock weathering has had a bumpy stretch in the published record. Project Drawdown lists ["unpredictable effectiveness for carbon removal"](https://drawdown.org/explorer/deploy-enhanced-rock-weathering) and complex monitoring among its challenges. A three-year field trial covered by [C&EN in December 2025](https://cen.acs.org/environment/climate-change/enhanced-rock-weathering-co2-sequestration-climate-change/103/web/2025/12) found removal rates below 10% of what was expected. A [Midwestern US trial in Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/climate/articles/10.3389/fclim.2025.1657058/full) found evidence of CDR with steel slag but not with basalt. The open question hasn't been whether weathering chemistry works. It has been whether we can see it happen, at useful rates, on real farms and in real watersheds.

## The century-scale view: liming as a sink

[Suhrhoff, Reinhard, Kanzaki, Planavsky and colleagues, published in *Nature* on 23 September 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-11040-2), take on a long-standing accounting assumption: that agricultural lime is a CO2 source. They use century-scale records of liming and anthropogenic acidity inputs across the Mississippi River Basin. River alkalinity fluxes suggest that about 90% of the ideal removal potential of lime added since 1900 has been realized at the catchment scale. That ideal potential is roughly 0.44 GtCO2.

The removal arrives with a decadal lag, which the authors attribute to soil cation exchange and solute transport. Their reactive transport modelling shows why. There is an initial emissions pulse while lime neutralizes existing soil acidity pools, and net removal emerges after that.

The conceptual move is the part I find most useful. The authors argue that current frameworks use an incomplete counterfactual. Those frameworks blame the lime for CO2 that is really driven by anthropogenic acidity, such as fertilizer inputs. Measured against that acidity baseline, liming is a net sink on decade-to-century timescales.

## The farm-scale view: basalt dissolving fast

[Milliken, Chang, Rioux and colleagues posted to CDRXiv on 25 September 2026](https://cdrxiv.org/preprint/551) and share authors with the liming paper, including Planavsky, Reinhard and Kanzaki. They report ultrafine basalt weathering from more than 1,800 samples across 24 applied fields in Southern Virginia and North Carolina, compared against 24 unapplied fields. They used a paired sample-resample design to handle between-field heterogeneity, which is exactly the problem that has muddied earlier trials.

Averaging three soil mass-balance approaches, they found:

- **Feedstock weathered:** 21.23 ± 7.44% (SEM) in about six months
- **Potential removal:** 3.79 ± 1.36 tCO2 ha⁻¹
- **Model agreement:** the result matches a first-order kinetic model driven by feedstock-specific properties

That last point may matter as much as the headline rate. It suggests weathering on working farms can be predicted from what you know about the rock.

## The river view: quantified CDR from an existing liming programme

[Bahler, Trueman, Vogt, Tucker, Brenan and Sterling posted to CDRXiv on 17 September 2026](https://cdrxiv.org/preprint/557), and the paper is submitted to *ES&T*. They ran a 98-day trial on Norway's Kvina River, inside a liming programme that already exists to protect salmon. Adding 1,480 t of calcite raised pH from 6.0 to 6.8. They compared the rise in alkalinity export at the river mouth against a limed control site.

The trial produced 250 ± 30 tCO2e of gross CDR. That works out to 0.17 ± 0.02 t per tonne of calcite, or 54% of the theoretical maximum. Phosphate was the only nutrient or water-quality indicator that rose significantly. The framing is practical: modestly raising pH targets at facilities that already exist.

## How the field is reacting

The liming paper got a Nature news piece calling it ["an unexpected CO2 sink"](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-026-02646-7), plus [Yale News](https://news.yale.edu/2026/09/23/adding-limestone-mississippi-river-basin-farmlands-acts-major-carbon-sink-study-finds) and wider syndication. One outlet framed it as ["undercutting the enhanced weathering market"](https://www.intelligentliving.co/agricultural-liming-carbon-sink/). I read it the other way. A century of watershed data showing that alkalinity added to soils reaches rivers is supporting evidence for the whole pathway. It also puts a real number on the time lags that crediting has to handle.

## Who this touches

- **Lithos:** Elliot Chang and Ben Brodin of Lithos are co-authors on the basalt paper, so that work bears directly on Lithos's ultrafine-basalt approach.
- **CarbonRun:** Most of the Kvina authors are affiliated with CarbonRun, and river alkalinity enhancement is its core pathway.

## What I'll watch next

I want to see whether the Southeastern basalt fields hold these rates over multiple seasons, and how much of that dissolution shows up downstream as alkalinity. The liming paper suggests the answer takes years to arrive. I'll also watch whether carbon accounting frameworks take up the acidity counterfactual, because that could change how agricultural lime is treated.
