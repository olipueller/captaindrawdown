Salesforce just made one of the most diversified CDR purchases on record: 12,492 tonnes of durable carbon removal from 19 different suppliers, spanning 6 methods and 15 countries. The deal, facilitated by intermediary Milkywire, is part of Salesforce's pledge to spend $100 million on durable CDR by 2030.
## Why it matters
This is a portfolio approach to CDR procurement, and it's exactly the kind of buying behavior the field needs right now. Most early CDR purchases have been concentrated in a handful of well-known suppliers. Salesforce is spreading its bets across nearly 20 companies at once, which sends demand signals to a much wider range of projects and geographies. That kind of breadth helps de-risk the buyer's commitment while simultaneously funding earlier-stage suppliers who desperately need revenue to prove out their models.
## The details
The 19 suppliers named in the deal read like a cross-section of the CDR landscape: Arbon Corp, The Carbon Removers, Carbonsate, Flux, InPlanet, INTERHOLCO, Kairos Carbon, Karbonetiq, NeoCarbon, Norma, Octavia Carbon, Planboo/Carboneers, Planeteers, PyroCCS, Releaf Earth, Takachar, Ucaneo, WasteX, and ZeroEx.
Six durable CDR methods are represented. While the announcement doesn't break down the exact tonnage per method, the supplier list gives us strong clues. InPlanet works on enhanced rock weathering (ERW), which accelerates the natural process of minerals absorbing CO2. PyroCCS and Takachar are in the biochar and biomass carbonization world. Octavia Carbon and others point to direct air capture. INTERHOLCO suggests forestry-based approaches with durable storage characteristics. The geographic spread across 15 countries means this isn't just a North America or Europe story. Suppliers like Octavia Carbon (Kenya), InPlanet (Brazil), and Takachar (India) are building CDR capacity in regions that have historically been left out of climate tech investment.
These are pre-purchase agreements, meaning Salesforce is committing to buy tonnes that will be delivered in the future. This is the standard structure for early-stage CDR deals. Buyers pay in advance (often at a premium) to give suppliers the capital they need to build and operate. It's a bet on future delivery, which carries risk, but it's also the mechanism that has funded nearly every significant CDR project to date.
Milkywire, the Swedish nonprofit that facilitated the deal, has now moved to 14th on the CDR.fyi Suppliers Leaderboard with over 44,400 tonnes facilitated. Salesforce climbs to 50th on the Purchasers Leaderboard. Milkywire also announced it's opening its next global call for proposals for durable CDR and will make additional catalytic purchases for Salesforce in 2026.
## What this changes
Three things stand out.
**First, the portfolio model is gaining traction.** Frontier (the Stripe-led advance market commitment) pioneered the idea of buying from many suppliers at once. Milkywire is now doing something similar but with a different structure, acting as an intermediary that scouts, vets, and bundles suppliers for large corporate buyers. This lowers the transaction cost for companies like Salesforce that want diversification without having to negotiate 19 separate contracts.
**Second, the $100 million commitment is starting to show real tonnage.** Pledges are cheap. Actual purchase agreements are not. With 12,492 tonnes now contracted through this single deal, Salesforce is converting its headline number into real demand. The company still has a long way to go to hit $100 million by 2030, but the pace and scale of this purchase suggest the commitment is more than marketing.
**Third, geographic diversity matters.** CDR has a concentration problem. Most investment flows to the US and Europe. By funding suppliers in Kenya, Brazil, India, and elsewhere, this deal helps build CDR infrastructure in places where it can also create local economic benefits. That's important for the long-term political viability of CDR as a global climate tool.
## Caveats
A few things to keep in mind. Pre-purchase agreements are not delivered tonnes. Some of these 19 suppliers are early-stage companies. Not all of them will deliver on time, at cost, or at the quality promised. That's the nature of advance market commitments, and it's a risk Salesforce is presumably pricing in by diversifying so broadly.
We also don't know the price per tonne for this deal, or how it breaks down across methods. ERW tonnes and biochar tonnes and DAC tonnes have very different cost profiles. Without that granularity, it's hard to assess whether this deal is pushing the cost curve or paying current market rates.
And the obligatory but essential point: CDR is for residual emissions that can't be eliminated through decarbonization. Salesforce, like every company buying CDR, should be judged first on how aggressively it's cutting its own emissions. CDR purchases are not a substitute for phasing out fossil fuels. They're a complement for the emissions that remain after you've done everything else.
Milkywire's upcoming call for proposals will be worth watching. If Salesforce continues buying at this pace and breadth through 2026, it could become one of the most important demand signals in the CDR market.
