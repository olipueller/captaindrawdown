First-generation REDD+ projects, the flagship forest-protection credits traded on the voluntary carbon market, claimed 10.7 times more avoided deforestation than independent evaluations can justify. That's not a rounding error. That's an order-of-magnitude gap between what was sold to buyers and what actually happened in the forest. A new synthesis published in *Nature Communications* by Tom Swinfield, Andrew Balmford, and colleagues at the University of Cambridge and other institutions lays out exactly where the inflation came from, and it's not the satellite data.
## Why it matters
REDD+ (Reducing Emissions from Deforestation and Degradation) credits have been one of the most widely purchased offset types on the voluntary carbon market. Companies bought them believing each credit represented one tonne of CO₂ kept out of the atmosphere. If the real climate benefit was roughly a tenth of what was claimed, that means millions of tonnes of supposed carbon savings never existed. For anyone building CDR portfolios or relying on nature-based credits to cover residual emissions, this study is a blunt reminder: the counterfactual, what would have happened without the project, is everything.
## The details
The research team combined six independent, after-the-fact evaluations of avoided deforestation covering 44 REDD+ projects. These weren't the projects' own assessments. They were external analyses using consistent methods to measure how much forest was actually saved compared to credible baselines.
The headline number: projects collectively claimed 10.7 times more avoided deforestation than the independent estimates support. Most projects did reduce deforestation to some degree. The problem wasn't that they did nothing. It was that the credits issued vastly overstated the benefit.
The critical finding is about *why* the over-crediting happened. The authors tested whether the discrepancy was driven by the choice of forest cover datasets, things like which satellite imagery or tree-cover thresholds were used. It wasn't. The real culprits were selection bias in projects' control areas and their modeling approaches.
In plain terms: when a REDD+ project developer needs to show what deforestation would have looked like without their intervention, they pick comparison areas and build models to predict the baseline rate of forest loss. The study found that projects systematically chose control areas and modeling techniques that inflated the predicted deforestation rate. A higher predicted baseline means more "avoided" deforestation on paper, which means more credits to sell.
This is a structural incentive problem. Project developers were, in effect, grading their own homework. They had financial motivation to pick the most alarming baseline scenario, and the methodologies gave them enough flexibility to do so.
## Implications
The authors note that recent reforms in the voluntary carbon market, particularly initiatives that transfer baseline assessment to unconflicted third parties and restrict methodological flexibility, are critical steps. But the paper is clear: those reforms are not sufficient on their own.
What's needed, according to the study, is ex post certification against credible counterfactuals. That means credits should be verified after the fact, based on what actually happened in comparable areas, not issued upfront based on projections made by the people selling them. This is a fundamental shift from how most REDD+ crediting has worked.
For CDR buyers and policymakers, the implications are direct:
**Nature-based credits need the same rigor as engineered removal.** The CDR field has spent years building measurement, reporting, and verification (MRV) systems for technologies like direct air capture. Forest credits deserve equivalent scrutiny, especially when they're being used to offset hard-to-abate emissions.
**Additionality can't be assumed.** The study confirms that most of these projects did conserve some forest. But "some" and "what was claimed" are very different numbers. Buyers who treated these credits as tonne-for-tonne equivalents to emission reductions were misled, even if unintentionally.
**Methodological flexibility is the enemy of accuracy when the assessor has skin in the game.** The finding that control area selection and modeling choices, not data quality, drove over-crediting points to a governance failure, not a technology failure.
## Caveats
This study covers first-generation REDD+ projects. The voluntary carbon market has evolved since many of these projects were designed, and several standard-setting bodies have tightened their methodologies. Whether newer projects perform better is an open question the paper doesn't fully answer.
The 10.7x figure is an aggregate across 44 projects. Individual projects varied. Some may have been closer to accurate; others may have been worse. The study does not name specific projects or developers.
It's also worth noting what this study does not claim: it does not say forest conservation is worthless or that REDD+ as a concept is broken. The authors are explicit that most projects did reduce deforestation. The problem is quantification and crediting, not the underlying conservation work.
Finally, this paper reinforces something the CDR field already knows but sometimes forgets: credits for avoided emissions (preventing deforestation) are fundamentally different from credits for carbon removal (pulling CO₂ out of the atmosphere). Both have roles to play, but conflating them, or letting inflated avoidance credits substitute for genuine removal, undermines the integrity of the entire market. Residual emissions need real tonnes removed, not optimistic projections about forests that might have been cut down.
