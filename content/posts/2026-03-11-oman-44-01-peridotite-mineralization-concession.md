<p>The Hajar Mountains in Oman contain one of the world&rsquo;s largest exposed formations of peridotite — a rock from Earth&rsquo;s mantle that naturally reacts with CO₂ and locks it away as solid mineral carbonate. The Semail Ophiolite alone has sequestered over <strong>one billion tonnes of CO₂</strong> through this process, over millions of years.</p><p>Now someone&rsquo;s trying to speed it up.</p><p><strong>44.01</strong>, an Omani startup named after the molecular weight of carbon dioxide, has signed a concession with Oman&rsquo;s Ministry of Energy and Minerals for the <strong>world&rsquo;s first commercial-scale peridotite mineralization project</strong> at Al Qabil in the Hajar Mountains.</p><h2 id=how-it-works>How It Works<a hidden class=anchor aria-hidden=true href=#how-it-works>#</a></h2><p>44.01 dissolves captured CO₂ in water and injects it into peridotite deep underground, where it reacts with the rock and turns to solid mineral carbonate — permanently. An independent life cycle assessment found the process delivers a <strong>carbon removal efficiency of 88–91%</strong> across its entire supply chain.</p><p>Columbia University&rsquo;s Peter Kelemen has calculated that if the natural mineralization process could be accelerated a millionfold through engineering, a single cubic kilometre of peridotite could theoretically absorb a billion tonnes of CO₂ per year. That&rsquo;s the kind of number that makes geologists and CDR investors sit up.</p><h2 id=why-oman>Why Oman<a hidden class=anchor aria-hidden=true href=#why-oman>#</a></h2><p>Most CDR innovation happens in North America and Europe. Oman is emerging as a dark horse. The country has both the geology — massive peridotite formations pushed to the surface by tectonic forces — and the political will to position itself as a carbon storage hub as it diversifies beyond oil.</p><p>The concession model is interesting: it treats subsurface carbon storage rights like mineral extraction rights, creating a legal framework other countries could adopt.</p><h2 id=new-research-adds-context>New Research Adds Context<a hidden class=anchor aria-hidden=true href=#new-research-adds-context>#</a></h2><p>A new paper in <em>Nature Communications</em>, led by researchers from Keele University and the University of Manchester, has shed new light on how carbonated peridotite forms. The study confirms that these rocks represent a <a href=https://www.manchester.ac.uk/about/news/carbon-trapping-rocks-demonstrate-earths-natural-ability-to-store-carbon-dioxide>significant hidden carbon sink</a> in Earth&rsquo;s long-term carbon cycle — validating the geological premise behind 44.01&rsquo;s approach.</p><h2 id=whats-next>What&rsquo;s Next<a hidden class=anchor aria-hidden=true href=#whats-next>#</a></h2><p>Commercial scale is still a few years out. But having a government concession — the legal right to inject and store CO₂ — is the prerequisite everything else depends on. If 44.01 can prove commercial viability in Oman, the model could spread to other peridotite-rich regions in the Middle East, Papua New Guinea, and parts of the western Mediterranean.</p><p>For a field dominated by DAC and biochar headlines, in-situ mineralization is the quiet pathway worth watching.</p><p><strong>Sources:</strong> <a href=https://www.zawya.com/en/economy/gcc/oman-the-future-of-carbon-removal-is-in-the-hajar-mountains-g7rei26d>Zawya</a> · <a href=https://www.manchester.ac.uk/about/news/carbon-trapping-rocks-demonstrate-earths-natural-ability-to-store-carbon-dioxide>University of Manchester</a></p><hr><h3 id=-related-reading>🔗 Related Reading<a hidden class=anchor aria-hidden=true href=#-related-reading>#</a></h3><ul><li><a href=/posts/frontier-2026-cdr-innovation-program/>Frontier Opens 2026 Carbon Removal Innovation Program</a></li></ul>
