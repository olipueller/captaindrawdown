Not all forests cool the planet. A new study from ETH Zürich shows that tropical forests in the Amazon, West Africa, and Southeast Africa act as giant air conditioners, combining CO2 absorption with powerful evaporative cooling. But forests planted in northern latitudes like Alaska or Siberia can actually warm their surroundings enough to cancel out their carbon storage benefits entirely. Where you plant trees matters at least as much as how many you plant.
## Why it matters
Reforestation is one of the most popular tools in the CDR toolkit. Governments, corporations, and offset programs pour money into tree-planting initiatives worldwide, often counting success by hectares covered or saplings planted. This study challenges that approach head-on: planting trees in the wrong place might deliver zero net climate benefit, or worse, contribute to local warming. For anyone investing in nature-based carbon removal, the location question just became unavoidable.
## The physics behind the finding
Lead author Nora Fahrenbach and her team at ETH Zürich looked beyond the familiar chemistry of photosynthesis (trees absorb CO2, store carbon, good) and factored in the physics of how forests change land surfaces.
Two physical effects matter most:
**Evaporative cooling.** In hot, humid regions, forests release enormous amounts of moisture. This evaporation cools the surrounding area, the same principle behind why sweating cools your skin. Tropical forests "sweat" heavily, and this cooling effect works both locally and globally.
**Albedo warming.** In snowy northern regions, the story flips. White snow reflects sunlight back into the atmosphere, keeping surfaces cool. Dark tree canopies absorb that sunlight instead. The result: forests in places like Siberia or Alaska can warm their surroundings, counteracting the cooling benefit of the carbon they store.
The study compared three different reforestation scenarios while accounting for both effects. The conclusion is stark: tropical reforestation delivers a double climate dividend (CO2 removal plus physical cooling), while boreal reforestation can break even or worse.
Living trees store roughly 108 tonnes of carbon per hectare of forest, not counting soil, shrubs, and deadwood. That carbon benefit is real everywhere. But when the warming albedo effect in northern forests is strong enough, it can fully offset that storage benefit.
## "It's not just the area, it's the location"
Fahrenbach put it plainly: "It really isn't just the area that counts, but also the location of planting." Strategic reforestation could achieve a global cooling effect on a significantly smaller total area than blanket tree-planting campaigns.
Julia Pongratz, a researcher at Ludwig Maximilian University of Munich (LMU) who studies interactions between land use and climate, confirmed the gap in current policy. "International climate policy thinks in the currency of CO2," she said. Climate conferences negotiate emissions budgets, and forests are valued almost exclusively as carbon stores. But locally, the biophysical effects like albedo and evaporation "are often even dominant."
This is a meaningful critique. The Paris Agreement framework and most carbon credit methodologies treat a tonne of CO2 absorbed by a boreal forest the same as a tonne absorbed by a tropical one. This study says that equivalence is wrong once you account for the full climate picture.
## The ceiling on what forests can do
Even with perfectly strategic reforestation, the ETH study found a hard upper limit: large-scale tree planting could lower global average temperatures by a maximum of 0.25°C. That's meaningful but modest. It will not save us on its own.
Fahrenbach was explicit: reforestation is "an important part of the fight against climate change" but "can in no way replace decarbonization, meaning the reduction of emissions." This point deserves emphasis. Tree planting is a complement to cutting fossil fuel use, never a substitute. Anyone framing forests as a reason to delay emissions cuts is misusing the science.
The study also highlights practical constraints. Land is finite. It's needed for food and feed production. Financial budgets for reforestation are politically limited. Fahrenbach recommends international coordination of future reforestation efforts, though she notes no global institution currently exists to do this.
## Protecting what we already have
The study's implications extend beyond new planting. Existing forests face mounting threats from drought, fire, and insect outbreaks. Pongratz warned that with Earth currently heading toward 2-3°C of warming, "it will be much harder to maintain existing forests and create new forested areas."
Germany offers a cautionary example. Several drought years since 2018 damaged German forests so severely that they became net CO2 sources, releasing more carbon than they absorbed. Only in 2025 did Germany's forests finally flip back to being net carbon sinks.
When forests die, their storage effect reverses. They release stored carbon back into the atmosphere, accelerating the very warming that killed them. This feedback loop makes forest protection urgent, not just forest planting.
## What to watch
This study doesn't resolve every question. It doesn't provide granular guidance on which specific sites within the tropics deliver the highest returns, or how climate change itself will shift these cooling and warming zones over time. The 0.25°C ceiling is a modeled estimate, not a field measurement. And the political challenge of directing reforestation investment toward tropical regions, many of which face governance and land-tenure complexities, remains enormous.
Still, the core message is clear and actionable: stop counting trees and start counting climate impact. Location is everything.
