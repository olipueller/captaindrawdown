First-generation REDD+ projects, the flagship forest conservation credits traded on the voluntary carbon market, claimed 10.7 times more avoided deforestation than independent evaluations actually support. That's not a rounding error. That's an order-of-magnitude gap between what was sold and what was delivered.
## Why it matters
REDD+ (Reducing Emissions from Deforestation and Degradation) credits have been one of the most popular categories on the voluntary carbon market for years. Companies buy them to offset their emissions, trusting that the credits represent real tons of CO₂ kept out of the atmosphere. If those credits are inflated by a factor of nearly 11, it means a huge volume of claimed climate benefit simply didn't happen. For anyone working in CDR or nature-based climate solutions, this finding is a flashing warning sign about how baselines and counterfactuals get constructed, and how easily they can go wrong.
## The details
A team led by Tom Swinfield and Andrew Balmford at the University of Cambridge, along with researchers including Thales West and Julia Jones, synthesized six independent ex post (after-the-fact) evaluations covering 44 REDD+ projects. The paper, published in *Nature Communications* in April 2026, combines these evaluations to estimate how much additional forest conservation first-generation REDD+ projects actually achieved.
The headline number: most projects did reduce deforestation to some degree. That's worth noting. These weren't complete failures. But the credits they issued vastly overstated the benefit. In aggregate, the projects claimed 10.7 times more avoided deforestation than independent estimates justify.
The critical question is *why*. The researchers found the answer isn't about which forest cover dataset a project used. It's about two specific methodological problems: selection bias in control areas and flawed modeling approaches.
Here's what that means in plain language. To calculate how much deforestation a REDD+ project prevented, you need a counterfactual: what would have happened to this forest without the project? Projects typically pick "control areas," nearby forests that aren't protected, and measure deforestation there. The difference between the control area's deforestation rate and the project area's rate is supposed to represent the project's impact.
The problem is that projects got to choose their own control areas. And they tended to pick controls that had higher deforestation rates, making their own performance look better by comparison. This is classic selection bias. If you compare your protected forest to a forest that was already under heavy pressure from logging or agriculture, you'll look like a hero even if your project had only a modest effect.
The modeling approaches compounded this. Projects had wide latitude in how they built their deforestation projections, and the methodological flexibility allowed them to generate inflated baselines.
## Implications
The paper argues that recent reforms, like transferring credit assessment to independent third parties and restricting methodological flexibility, are necessary but not sufficient. The researchers are clear: ex post certification against credible counterfactuals is the minimum standard needed if carbon markets are going to represent real, causal reductions in deforestation.
This matters well beyond REDD+. Any CDR or carbon credit methodology that relies on counterfactual reasoning faces the same structural risk. Enhanced rock weathering field trials, soil carbon projects, and even some biochar crediting approaches all depend on comparing observed outcomes to a baseline scenario. If the entity generating credits also gets to define the baseline, the incentive to inflate is baked in.
For buyers, the takeaway is uncomfortable but important: due diligence on methodology matters more than the label on the credit. A "forest carbon credit" that sounds good in a sustainability report is worthless if the underlying counterfactual is fiction.
For standard-setting bodies like Verra and Gold Standard, the pressure to move toward genuinely independent, ex post evaluation just got stronger. The 10.7x figure is hard to explain away.
## Caveats
A few things this paper does not say. It does not say REDD+ projects are useless. Most of the 44 projects evaluated did reduce deforestation. The conservation impact was real, just far smaller than claimed. The problem is quantitative, not existential.
The study covers first-generation projects specifically. Newer methodologies and standards may perform better, though the authors are skeptical that reforms alone will close the gap without mandatory ex post verification.
The 10.7x figure is an aggregate across 44 projects drawn from six evaluations. Individual project performance varied. Some projects may have been closer to accurate; others were likely worse.
Finally, this is about avoided deforestation credits, not carbon removal. REDD+ prevents emissions rather than pulling CO₂ out of the atmosphere. But the lessons about baseline integrity, selection bias, and the danger of self-assessment apply directly to CDR credit markets that are scaling up right now. If the CDR field doesn't learn from REDD+'s mistakes on additionality and counterfactual rigor, it risks repeating them at scale.
