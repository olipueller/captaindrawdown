## Google Goes All-In on Waste-to-Biochar

The biggest deal today: Google signed an agreement with AMP to remove 200,000 metric tons of CO₂ by 2030. AMP's approach is elegantly circular — AI-powered sorting pulls organic waste from municipal landfill streams, then pyrolysis converts it into stable biochar. First deployment site: Virginia's largest recycling project.

The numbers matter. 200,000 tons is one of the largest single carbon removal purchases we've seen from a tech company. And the waste-to-biochar pathway carries a double benefit: the carbon stays locked in biochar for centuries while diverting organics that would otherwise decompose into methane.

## India's Enhanced Weathering Moment

From Darjeeling to corporate balance sheets: Indian farmers are spreading basalt dust on rice paddies, and each verified tonne of CO₂ removed is worth $150–600 on voluntary markets. The country now has 50 registered CDR projects and 214,000 verified removal credits.

Startup Alt Carbon gives quarry-waste basalt to farmers for free, turning mining byproducts into climate action. India's combination of low labor costs, massive arable land, and abundant basalt could make it a CDR powerhouse — particularly for enhanced weathering, where tropical soils and monsoon rains accelerate mineral dissolution.

## Biomass Burial Gets Organized

Seventeen companies — including Graphyte, Carba, Puro.Earth, Isometric, and Vaulted Deep — launched the Direct Storage of Biomass Coalition under the Carbon Business Council. The concept: take waste wood, crop residues, or other biomass and store it underground before it decomposes and releases its carbon.

It's low-tech, uses existing supply chains, and the feedstock is abundant. The coalition aims to develop shared standards and push for policy recognition. Worth watching — this pathway could scale faster than most high-tech alternatives.

## DAC Gets a Solar Upgrade

Researchers developed a new direct air capture sorbent made from upcycled wood waste. The key innovation: lignin in the material enables photothermal heating, meaning sunlight alone can release captured CO₂ — no external energy needed for regeneration.

That matters because sorbent regeneration is typically DAC's biggest energy cost and expense. Early-stage research, but the combination of cheap feedstock and solar-powered regeneration could reshape the economics of direct air capture.

## CDR Meets Critical Minerals

An insightful analysis from Carbon Based Commentary highlighted three structural parallels between carbon removal and critical minerals: both are hard-tech with long development timelines, both compete against cheaper but riskier alternatives, and both need the same policy tools — procurement commitments, strategic reserves, and supply chain coordination — to scale.
