Direct ocean capture might outperform direct air capture over long time horizons, and a new mathematical modeling paper from Alamin, Cruz, Hernandez, and Mendoza helps explain why.

The core insight: CO2 concentrations in seawater are roughly 150 times higher than in ambient air. That thermodynamic advantage means DOC can potentially extract more carbon per unit of energy. But the real question isn't just efficiency at the point of capture. It's what happens to the ocean-atmosphere carbon cycle over decades and centuries after you remove CO2 from one reservoir versus the other.

The researchers use chemical reaction network theory to model how DAC and DOC differ in their long-term equilibrium effects on atmospheric CO2. When you pull CO2 from air (DAC), the ocean partially outgasses to re-equilibrate. When you pull it from seawater (DOC), the ocean draws down atmospheric CO2 to compensate, creating an indirect atmospheric removal effect. The net long-term atmospheric impact depends heavily on ocean buffer chemistry and timescales that most CDR analyses gloss over.

This matters because the DAC industry (159 companies tracked in our directory alone) has gotten the lion's share of attention and funding. DOC is earlier stage but companies and researchers are starting to take it seriously. If the math holds, DOC's indirect atmospheric drawdown effect could make it a more capital-efficient path to permanent removal at scale.
