The most useful piece of ERW writing I read this week was not a preprint. It was a LinkedIn post from a small German nonprofit lab, calmly explaining why the number the entire enhanced rock weathering industry sells is the number almost nobody is measuring at field scale.

The post was [Part 4 of the XXL Lysimeter Experiment](https://www.linkedin.com/feed/update/urn:li:share:7496189729466572801/), from the Carbon Drawdown Initiative. Its framing sentence is the whole critique in one line: "Alkalinity is the number we actually care about, and it's slow and expensive: pump the leachate, ship it to a lab, titrate." That is the Carbon Drawdown Initiative in a nutshell right now. Not polemic. Just a lab bench and a titration burette, quietly refusing to pretend the shortcut works.

**The arc.** The Carbon Drawdown Initiative has spent years running a long-duration outdoor lysimeter experiment on enhanced rock weathering, a large controlled soil column that lets you catch the water leaving the root zone and actually measure what came out. Most ERW field data on the market is generated by suppliers with credits to sell. The Carbon Drawdown Initiative is one of the few groups running independent, multi-year field work with no tonnes on the shelf. That independence is why the Part 4 post lands the way it does.

**The work.** In the Carbon Drawdown Initiative's own framing, Part 4 asks "can we shortcut alkalinity measurement." That question is the critique. Most ERW crediting today leans on proxies. Cation release from the feedstock, solid-phase dissolution estimates, modeled transport to the ocean. The Carbon Drawdown Initiative's position is that none of those are the number a buyer is actually paying for. The number a buyer is paying for is exported alkalinity, or the dissolved inorganic carbon it carries. Everything else is inference. Part 4 tests, in the lysimeter, whether cheaper signals track the expensive titration closely enough to substitute for it. Treating that as an open question, rather than a solved one, is itself a rebuke to how the category currently issues credits.

**The lens.** Let me steel-man the counter-argument first. Direct alkalinity measurement at every field is not going to happen. It is slow, it is expensive, and if you demand it universally you slow ERW to a crawl at exactly the moment gigatonne-scale removal needs to grow. Proxies with conservative discounts are, on this view, how any real-world MRV, meaning measurement, reporting, and verification, has to work.

That defense is fair as far as it goes. But this week the field itself is drifting toward the Carbon Drawdown Initiative's position. A CDRXIV preprint on [direct in situ alkalinity export measurement](https://cdrxiv.org/preprint/456) names alkalinity export quantification as "a key bottleneck" for ERW MRV. A [direct-measurement framework preprint](https://cdrxiv.org/preprint/309) argues dissolved inorganic carbon must be the anchor, not a downstream check. A [spatial-uncertainty framework](https://cdrxiv.org/preprint/334) shows the solid-phase proxies people would use instead carry large, awkward error bars of their own. And a [river photosynthesis preprint](https://cdrxiv.org/preprint/329) shows that alkalinity and dissolved inorganic carbon generated in soil can be consumed downstream before it ever reaches the ocean, which means the modeled tonne and the delivered tonne are not the same tonne.

Put those four together and the Carbon Drawdown Initiative's stubbornness looks less like perfectionism and more like the ceiling of what proxy MRV can honestly claim.

**Where they're heading.** The bet is that within a protocol cycle or two, serious ERW crediting will require exported alkalinity or dissolved inorganic carbon measurement as the anchor, with proxies calibrated to it rather than substituted for it. If that happens, the Carbon Drawdown Initiative's negative and inconvenient lysimeter results, published in real time, will look like the discipline the category needed. If it does not happen, a lot of tonnes issued in 2024 and 2025 are going to age poorly. For a primer on the underlying process, my earlier post on what enhanced weathering is covers the chemistry the argument turns on.

**Why the voice matters.** Every category in carbon removal eventually meets the person who will not let it skip the hard measurement. For direct air capture it was the energy accountants. For ocean alkalinity enhancement it is the counterfactual crowd. For ERW, right now, the loudest honest signal is a small lab pumping leachate and titrating it. Watch whether Isometric, Puro, and the Carbon Standards CRCF submission cite Part 4 when they revise. That will tell you whether the industry is ready to be disciplined by its own field data.
