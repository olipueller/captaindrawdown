The European Union will price imported goods under its Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM) based on the EU Emissions Trading System (ETS) carbon price. This means foreign producers of cement, steel, aluminum, and other carbon-intensive materials will need to pay the same carbon costs that European manufacturers already face. It is a direct attempt to prevent "carbon leakage," where production simply moves to countries with weaker climate rules.
## Why it matters for CDR
This policy doesn't directly fund carbon dioxide removal, but it reshapes the economics around the hardest-to-abate industrial sectors. Cement, steel, and aluminum are exactly the industries that will likely need CDR to offset their residual emissions. By raising the effective carbon price on imports, the EU is making the cost of emitting more uniform across borders. That, in turn, strengthens the business case for investing in deep decarbonization and, eventually, CDR for whatever emissions remain after process improvements max out.
A higher and more consistent carbon price also makes CDR credits more competitive relative to simply paying to pollute. If imported goods face the same carbon costs as domestic ones, there is less incentive to dodge the price signal by relocating production. The carbon price becomes harder to escape, which is precisely the condition under which CDR procurement starts to look like a rational investment rather than a voluntary nice-to-have.
## What we know about the framework
The source details are limited, but the core mechanism is straightforward. The EU is anchoring CBAM to the ETS market price. The ETS is Europe's cap-and-trade system, where companies buy allowances for each ton of CO2 they emit. That price fluctuates but has traded in the range of 50-70 euros per ton in recent periods.
Under CBAM, importers of covered goods will need to purchase certificates reflecting the embedded emissions in their products, priced at the ETS rate. If a foreign producer has already paid a carbon price in their home country, that amount can be deducted. The covered sectors, cement, steel, aluminum, fertilizers, electricity, and hydrogen, represent some of the most emissions-intensive supply chains on the planet.
The transitional reporting phase of CBAM has been running since October 2023, with the full financial obligations set to phase in gradually through 2026 and beyond. This latest move to formally peg the border price to the ETS market cements (no pun intended) the connection between Europe's internal carbon market and the cost of doing business with Europe from abroad.
## Implications for the CDR field
Three things stand out.
**First, demand signals for industrial CDR get stronger.** When steel and cement producers worldwide face a real carbon price on their exports to Europe, the calculus for investing in carbon capture or purchasing removal credits shifts. A ton of CO2 avoided or removed has a clearer monetary value. This is especially relevant for technologies like direct air capture and storage (DACCS) or enhanced rock weathering (ERW), which could eventually supply high-quality removal credits to offset residual industrial emissions.
**Second, the policy creates a floor for carbon pricing ambition globally.** Other countries exporting to the EU now have an incentive to implement their own carbon pricing. If they do, their producers can deduct those costs from CBAM obligations. This could accelerate the spread of carbon markets worldwide, each of which could eventually incorporate CDR as a compliance pathway.
**Third, it puts pressure on the definition of "embedded emissions."** As CBAM matures, questions about how to account for CDR within product carbon footprints will become urgent. If a cement producer uses carbon capture to reduce the emissions embedded in their product, how is that reflected in the CBAM calculation? The measurement, reporting, and verification (MRV) standards for these claims will need to be precise and credible. This is an area where the CDR community should be paying close attention and pushing for rigorous protocols.
## Caveats
CBAM is a trade and climate policy instrument, not a CDR policy. It does not directly fund or mandate carbon removal. Its effect on CDR is indirect, working through the carbon price signal and the incentive structures it creates for heavy industry.
There is also the question of enforcement and scope. CBAM currently covers a limited set of sectors. Many carbon-intensive products and supply chains remain outside its reach. The effectiveness of the mechanism depends heavily on accurate emissions reporting from foreign producers, which is a significant data challenge.
It is also worth noting that a carbon border price, no matter how well designed, is not a substitute for phasing out fossil fuels. The point of CBAM is to level the playing field so that European climate policy doesn't just push dirty production elsewhere. CDR enters the picture only for genuinely residual emissions that cannot be eliminated through efficiency, electrification, or fuel switching. That distinction matters. A border carbon price that simply makes pollution more expensive everywhere is useful, but it does not on its own guarantee that removal technologies get built.
The next thing to watch is how the EU handles CDR credits within the ETS and CBAM frameworks. If removal credits eventually become eligible for compliance, the market dynamics could shift dramatically. For now, CBAM is a price signal. Whether it becomes a CDR demand driver depends on decisions that haven't been made yet.
