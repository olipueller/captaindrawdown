The University of Regina has signed a partnership with the Thai Cement Manufacturers Association (TCMA) to bring its carbon capture expertise to bear on one of the world's most emissions-intensive industries: cement production. The collaboration aims to reduce CO2 output from Thailand's cement sector, a move that could serve as a template for decarbonizing heavy industry across Southeast Asia.
## Why it matters
Cement manufacturing is responsible for roughly 7-8% of global CO2 emissions. Unlike power generation, where you can swap in renewables, cement production generates CO2 as a fundamental part of its chemistry. When limestone is heated to make clinite, the calcium carbonate releases carbon dioxide no matter what fuel you use. That makes cement a textbook case of hard-to-abate emissions, the exact category where carbon capture technology has its strongest justification.
Thailand is a significant cement producer, and the TCMA represents the industry's collective interests there. Partnering with a university that has deep carbon capture research credentials signals that the Thai cement sector is looking seriously at post-combustion capture as part of its decarbonization strategy.
## What we know about the partnership
The details available are limited. What we know is that the University of Regina, based in Saskatchewan, Canada, is bringing its carbon capture research capabilities to this collaboration. The university has a long track record in CO2 capture research, particularly through its work on amine-based solvent systems and other capture technologies suited to industrial flue gas streams.
The TCMA, for its part, represents Thailand's cement manufacturers and has been the industry body coordinating sustainability efforts in the sector. This partnership appears designed to apply the university's technical knowledge to the specific emissions profile of Thai cement plants.
The exact scope of the work, whether it involves pilot projects, feasibility studies, technology transfer, or workforce training, hasn't been detailed in the available reporting. But the direction is clear: take proven carbon capture science and adapt it to a major industrial emitter in a developing economy.
## Implications for cement decarbonization
Cement is one of those industries where carbon capture isn't a luxury or a distraction. It's likely a necessity. You can improve energy efficiency, switch to alternative fuels, and use supplementary cementitious materials to reduce emissions per ton. But the process emissions from calcination, the chemical breakdown of limestone, can only be addressed through carbon capture or a wholesale shift to alternative binder chemistries that remain far from commercial readiness.
This makes partnerships like the University of Regina-TCMA collaboration genuinely important. If carbon capture can be made technically and economically viable for cement plants in Thailand, the lessons will be transferable across the region. Southeast Asia is urbanizing rapidly, which means cement demand isn't going away. The question is whether the industry can produce it with a dramatically smaller carbon footprint.
For the University of Regina, this is also a chance to demonstrate that its research has real-world applicability beyond the Canadian oil and gas context where much of its capture work has historically been deployed. Applying the same fundamental science to cement in a tropical, developing-economy setting is a meaningful expansion.
## A note on moral hazard
It's worth being explicit: carbon capture on cement is not the same thing as carbon capture on fossil fuel power plants. When applied to cement, capture addresses emissions that genuinely cannot be eliminated through fuel switching or demand reduction alone. This is the residual-emissions use case where carbon capture technology is most defensible. It should not be confused with arguments that we can keep burning coal and gas as long as we bolt capture equipment onto the exhaust stack. Cement process emissions are a different beast entirely.
## Caveats
The available information on this partnership is thin. We don't know the funding level, the timeline, or the specific technologies being considered. We don't know whether this will lead to a pilot capture facility on a Thai cement plant or remain at the study phase. The economics of carbon capture on cement remain challenging everywhere, and Thailand doesn't currently have the kind of carbon pricing or regulatory framework that would make capture financially attractive without external support.
There's also the question of what happens to the captured CO2. Permanent geological storage infrastructure doesn't exist at scale in Thailand. Without a clear plan for sequestration or utilization, capture technology alone doesn't solve the problem.
Finally, university-industry partnerships are announced regularly, and not all of them produce meaningful outcomes. The real test will be whether this collaboration leads to concrete (no pun intended) technical progress and, eventually, measurable emissions reductions at operating cement plants.
What's encouraging is that the right parties are talking to each other. A research institution with genuine capture expertise connecting with an industry association representing major emitters is the kind of pairing that can move the needle, if the follow-through matches the ambition.
