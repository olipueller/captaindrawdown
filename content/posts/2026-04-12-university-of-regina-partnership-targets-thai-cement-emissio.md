The University of Regina has signed a partnership with the Thai Cement Manufacturers Association (TCMA) to bring its carbon capture research to one of Southeast Asia's most carbon-intensive industries. The deal pairs a Canadian university with decades of CO2 capture expertise with an industry group representing Thailand's major cement producers, a sector responsible for roughly 8% of global CO2 emissions.
## Why it matters
Cement is one of the hardest industrial sectors to decarbonize. About 60% of its emissions come not from burning fuel but from the chemical process of turning limestone into clinite, the calcium silicate compound at the heart of Portland cement. You can't switch to renewable energy and solve that problem. You need to either capture the CO2 at the source or fundamentally change the chemistry. That makes cement a prime candidate for carbon capture, and it makes partnerships like this one worth watching closely.
Thailand is a significant cement producer in the ASEAN region. The TCMA represents the country's major manufacturers, giving the University of Regina a direct line to the facilities where capture technology would actually need to be deployed.
## What the University of Regina brings
The University of Regina, based in Saskatchewan, Canada, has been working on carbon capture technology for over 30 years. Its International Test Centre of CO2 Capture (ITC) is one of the longest-running research facilities of its kind. The university's researchers have focused heavily on post-combustion capture, the approach where CO2 is stripped from flue gas after fuel is burned or after process emissions are released. This is the most relevant method for retrofitting existing cement plants.
The university's work has included developing and testing amine-based solvents and other chemical processes that absorb CO2 from industrial exhaust streams. Saskatchewan's own economy, heavily tied to oil, gas, and potash mining, has given the university a practical testing ground. Now the question is whether that expertise translates to a different industrial context halfway around the world.
## The cement problem in numbers
Global cement production generates around 2.7 billion tonnes of CO2 per year. To put that in perspective, if the cement industry were a country, it would be the third-largest emitter on the planet, behind only China and the United States.
Thailand produces roughly 35-40 million tonnes of cement annually. Even at the lower end, that represents tens of millions of tonnes of CO2. The TCMA has publicly committed to reducing the sector's carbon footprint, and this partnership appears to be one concrete step toward that goal.
The challenge is cost. Carbon capture on cement plants is technically feasible. Heidelberg Materials (formerly HeidelbergCement) is building a full-scale capture facility at its Brevik plant in Norway. But the economics are tough. Capture adds significant capital expenditure (the upfront cost of building the equipment) and operating expenditure (the ongoing energy and maintenance costs). For cement producers in developing economies, where margins are thinner and carbon pricing may be absent or low, the financial case is even harder to make.
## What this partnership likely involves
Based on the University of Regina's track record, this collaboration will probably focus on feasibility studies, pilot testing, and knowledge transfer rather than immediate deployment of full-scale capture systems. The university has a history of conducting techno-economic assessments, essentially figuring out what capture approaches would work at a given facility and what they would cost.
For the TCMA, the value is access to expertise without having to build that research capacity from scratch. For the University of Regina, it's a chance to apply its technology in a new industrial and geographic context, which strengthens the research and potentially opens doors to further international partnerships.
## Implications for the field
This is a useful signal that carbon capture for cement is gaining traction beyond Europe and North America. Most of the high-profile cement capture projects, Brevik in Norway, the LEILAC projects in Europe, are concentrated in wealthy countries with strong policy support. If capture technology is going to make a real dent in cement's global emissions, it needs to work in places like Thailand, India, Vietnam, and Indonesia, where cement production is growing fastest.
University-industry partnerships like this one can help bridge the gap. They lower the risk for manufacturers by providing independent technical analysis before anyone commits serious capital. They also build local expertise, which is essential for long-term deployment.
It's worth noting that carbon capture on cement is a point-source capture application, not CDR in the strict sense. The CO2 being captured was released from industrial processes, not pulled from the ambient atmosphere. However, when paired with permanent storage or utilization, it prevents emissions that would otherwise be very difficult to eliminate. And some cement capture configurations, particularly those using bioenergy or combined with direct air capture, can cross into net-negative territory.
## Caveats
A partnership announcement is not a deployment announcement. No capture equipment is being installed tomorrow. Feasibility studies and pilot projects can take years, and many never progress to commercial scale. The financial and regulatory environment in Thailand will ultimately determine whether capture gets built on Thai cement plants.
There's also the question of what happens to the captured CO2. Permanent geological storage requires suitable geology and regulatory frameworks. Utilization pathways, like using CO2 in concrete curing, are promising but still limited in scale. Without a clear plan for the captured carbon, the technology is incomplete.
Finally, carbon capture should never become a reason to delay other decarbonization measures in cement, like clinker substitution, alternative fuels, or energy efficiency improvements. Capture is for the residual emissions that remain after those steps are taken. The best outcome here would be a Thai cement industry that pursues all available levers, with capture handling the emissions that nothing else can address.
