The University of California San Diego research, published in Nature Communications, offers a stark, if somewhat optimistic, framing for direct air capture (DAC) deployment. The core argument is that "crisis-level" government funding, mirroring wartime or pandemic spending, could scale DAC to meet significant climate goals. Specifically, the study posits that an annual investment of 1.2-1.9% of global GDP, commencing in 2025, could remove a cumulative 570-840 gigatons of CO2 by 2100. This is a staggering figure, aligning with the upper bound of CO2 removals IPCC scenarios suggest are needed to meet Paris Agreement targets. For context, this program would remove 2.2-2.3 GtCO2 by 2050 and 13-20 GtCO2 by 2075.

However, the findings are not a silver bullet. Even with this massive, sustained investment, the globe would still likely see temperature rises of 2.4-2.5°C by 2100 without concurrent, deep cuts in global emissions. This underscores a crucial point: DAC is an essential *complement* to decarbonization, not a replacement. The authors highlight DAC's appeal to policymakers due to its controllability, verifiable removals, and limited threat to existing industries – attributes that are certainly attractive in a politically complex landscape. They also point to the "scalability" of the technology as the primary limiting factor once funding is secured, drawing parallels to vaccine distribution challenges during a pandemic.

From a CDR analyst's perspective, this paper, while published in early 2021, remains highly relevant. The idea of "wartime-level" funding for DAC – equating to trillions of dollars annually – is a concept we still grapple with. Current policy mechanisms like the US 45Q tax credit, which offers $180/ton for DAC, or the EU Innovation Fund, while significant, are still orders of magnitude away from the 1.2-1.9% of global GDP suggested. The paper essentially asks: what if we *really* committed? It's a useful thought experiment that highlights the chasm between current support and what's technically feasible under a crisis footing.

The emphasis on scalability as the bottleneck, rather than funding, is particularly insightful for the present moment. Companies like Climeworks, Carbon Engineering, and Heirloom are rapidly iterating, focusing on modularity, energy efficiency, and supply chain development. Their current challenges aren't just about securing capital, but about engineering and operationalizing plants that can capture hundreds of thousands, and eventually millions, of tons annually. The paper's point that "near-term deployments enhance future scalability" is a direct call to action for governments and private entities alike. Every pilot, every demonstration, every small commercial plant, contributes to the learning curve needed for the gigaton-scale future. It suggests that avoiding early DAC deployments due to high initial costs is precisely the wrong approach, as it hinders the experience gain necessary to drive those costs down and achieve scale. This reinforces the value of current initiatives, however modest they may seem against the backdrop of trillions.
