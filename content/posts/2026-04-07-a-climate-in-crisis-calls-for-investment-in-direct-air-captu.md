The latest research from UC San Diego, published in *Nature Communications*, paints a stark picture of the scale required for direct air capture (DAC) to play a significant role in mitigating climate change. The study posits that a "wartime-level" funding commitment, on par with national spending during major crises like wars or pandemics, is necessary to deploy DAC at a scale capable of impacting global temperatures. This isn't just academic musing; it’s a detailed exploration of what an emergency response to climate change, centered on DAC, would look like.

The findings suggest that if an emergency DAC program were to commence in 2025, receiving an annual investment of 1.2-1.9% of global GDP, it could cumulatively remove 570-840 gigatons of CO2 from 2025-2100. For context, this falls within the range of CO2 removals that IPCC scenarios indicate will be needed to meet Paris Agreement targets. More immediately, the program could remove 2.2-2.3 gigatons by 2050 and 13-20 gigatons by 2075. However, even with such a massive undertaking, the globe would still face a 2.4-2.5°C temperature rise by 2100 without deep cuts in global emissions below current trajectories. This underscores a critical point: DAC is not a substitute for emissions reduction, but a vital complement.

From my vantage point, this research offers a crucial framing for the CDR field. It shifts the conversation from whether DAC is *necessary* to *how* we achieve the necessary scale, and what that scale actually entails. The authors highlight DAC’s attractive attributes for policymakers: its deployments are highly controllable, carbon removals are verifiable, and it doesn't directly threaten the economic competitiveness of existing industries – a significant political hurdle for many mitigation policies. The estimated financial resources potentially available, exceeding one trillion dollars per year based on historical US crisis spending, indicate that capital isn't the insurmountable barrier it's often made out to be. We often hear about the high cost of DAC today, but this study suggests that with a dedicated push, costs could fall rapidly as firms gain experience.

The real bottleneck, according to the study, would be the industry's ability to scale up, not necessarily the funding itself. This is a critical insight for the CDR community. It means we need to focus intensely on supply chain development, workforce training, manufacturing capacity, and site identification *now*. Companies like Carbon Engineering, Climeworks, and 1PointFive are already making strides, with projects like Occidental's Stratos plant aiming for 500,000 tonnes per year, but these are still orders of magnitude below the multi-gigaton scale envisioned. The imperative for near-term deployments, even if initially expensive, is to "enhance future scalability." It’s about building the muscle memory for a nascent industry.

This isn't about promoting DAC as a silver bullet, but acknowledging its unique role in a comprehensive climate strategy. The "smart friend" perspective here is that the article provides a sobering, yet actionable, vision. It implies that policymakers and investors should view early DAC projects not just as carbon removal facilities, but as critical infrastructure investments that pave the way for future gigaton-scale deployment. We need to move beyond pilot projects and start building industrial capacity, recognizing that the current high costs are a feature of infancy, not an inherent flaw, and that the long-term societal benefits far outweigh the initial investment.
