## The pathway in one paragraph

Biomass burial is the deliberate isolation of plant material — trees, crop residues, sawdust, algae, manure, bio-oil derived from those inputs — in an environment where it cannot decompose. Photosynthesis has already done the hard work of pulling carbon dioxide out of the atmosphere; the pathway's job is to prevent the carbon in that biomass from returning to it. That means engineering an environment without oxygen, without water, without microbes, or at temperatures and pressures where degradation stalls out. Done well, it is one of the cheapest routes to durable carbon dioxide removal on the current cost curve, with per-tonne prices in the $100–$400 range that already clear the market — which is why it accounts for a disproportionate share of tonnes delivered to date. Done badly, it is a landfill with a good story.

## How it works

The underlying biology is straightforward: lignocellulosic biomass decays because microbes have access to it, and microbes need water, oxygen, and moderate temperatures. Remove any one of those variables reliably enough, for long enough, and the carbon stays put. The pathway splits into several distinct engineering strategies:

**Dry, anoxic burial.** Woody biomass is placed in engineered pits or vaults sealed with low-permeability clay, or in aboveground chambers designed to stay dry. The reference case is the "wood vault" concept articulated by Ning Zeng in [Carbon Balance and Management (2008)](https://doi.org/10.1186/1750-0680-3-1), which argued that wood buried in cold, dry, or anaerobic conditions can persist for centuries to millennia, echoing the natural preservation seen in bog oaks and permafrost logs.

**Thermochemical conversion first, then storage.** Fast pyrolysis converts biomass into bio-oil, which is denser, pumpable, and injectable into geological formations. Hydrothermal liquefaction (HTL) and hydrothermal carbonization (HTC) do similar work on wet feedstocks. The review by [Yang et al. (2021)](https://openalex.org/W3176278271) surveys these conversion routes in the context of bioenergy with carbon capture and storage; the same physics applies when the intermediate is buried rather than combusted.

**Sub-sediment and deep saline injection.** Organic slurries can be injected into deep formations using oilfield-derived well technology, exploiting the same seals that hold hydrocarbons in place over geological time.

**Below-water-table burial.** Peatlands and other waterlogged systems are natural analogs for anaerobic carbon preservation over Holocene timescales. Engineered versions place biomass beneath the groundwater surface to reproduce those conditions.

Feedstock matters as much as burial method. [Fuss et al. and successor life-cycle work](https://openalex.org/W3153554019) on bioenergy with carbon capture and storage (BECCS) pathways in Europe make the point that upstream emissions — harvest, transport, drying — can consume a meaningful fraction of gross removals if the supply chain is not disciplined. Residues from wildfire mitigation, agricultural waste, and encroacher bush all present different LCA profiles from purpose-grown plantation biomass.

## Who's doing it

The pathway is populated by companies pursuing quite different combinations of feedstock, conversion, and storage medium:

- [Charm Industrial](https://charmindustrial.com/) pyrolyzes agricultural residues into bio-oil injected into Class V wells; it has delivered the largest volume of engineered CDR tonnes to date under Frontier and Stripe offtakes.
- [Vaulted Deep](https://vaulteddeep.com/) slurry-injects organic waste — biosolids, manure, paper sludge — into deep formations using oilfield well technology.
- [Graphyte](https://www.graphyte.com/), backed by Breakthrough Energy, dries and compresses timber and agricultural residues into wrapped blocks it calls Carbon Casts, buried in shallow monitored vaults.
- [Mast Reforestation](https://www.mastreforest.com/) buries fire-killed trees from Western US burn scars and pairs the burial with post-wildfire replanting, issuing credits through Puro.earth.
- [Kodama Systems](https://kodama.ai/) harvests small-diameter forest-thinning material with teleoperated equipment and buries it in Nevada desert vaults.
- [Rewind](https://www.rewind.earth/) stores biomass in disused deep mines and anoxic marine basins, with a first commercial project in Georgia.
- [Cowboy Clean Fuels](https://cowboycleanfuels.com/) injects biomass into depleted coalbed methane wells, letting native microbes produce renewable natural gas while CO₂ adsorbs onto the coal.
- [Carbonsate](https://www.carbonsate.com/) and [Tivano Carbon](https://www.tivano-carbon.com/) operate in Southern Africa, burying woody biomass from bush encroachment — a feedstock with strong ecological co-arguments.

Smaller or earlier-stage operators — [Woodcache](https://www.woodcache.org/), [Carbon Lockdown](https://carbonlockdown.net/), [Levitree](https://www.levitree.com/), [InterEarth](https://www.inter.earth/), [Sinkco Labs](https://www.sinkcolabs.com/), [Recoal](https://recoal.net/) — represent variants on vault design, injection mechanics, and marine sub-sediment placement. Not all will survive.

## The durability question

Advertised durability sits at 1,000+ years for most projects, with some claiming geological timescales for bio-oil and coal-formation injection. The physical basis is credible for the deepest storage variants — bio-oil in a Class V well or slurry in a saline formation sits under the same seals that trapped hydrocarbons — and reasonably supported for engineered anaerobic vaults by analog evidence from bog wood and shipwreck timbers.

The harder questions are empirical. Vault-scale measurement of decomposition rates over decades does not yet exist; most MRV (measurement, reporting, and verification) relies on periodic gas sampling (CO₂ and CH₄), moisture and temperature sensors, and modelled decay curves calibrated to short-duration lab studies. Methane leakage is a particular concern: partial anaerobic decomposition produces CH₄, which if it escapes offsets a significant fraction of the CO₂ benefit on a 100-year basis. The [Puro.earth Terrestrial Storage of Biomass methodology](https://puro.earth/services/methodologies/) and [Isometric's biomass burial protocol](https://registry.isometric.com/protocol) both require methane monitoring, but real-world datasets are thin.

Counterfactual accounting is the other soft spot. If the biomass would otherwise have been burned, chipped, or left to decompose in situ, the baseline emissions determine the net removal — and baselines for wildfire residue, encroacher bush, and mill waste are genuinely contested.

## What to watch in the next year

- Multi-year in-situ gas monitoring data from Graphyte, Vaulted Deep, and the wood-vault operators — the first real decomposition curves rather than modelled ones.
