November 2025 was the biggest month for durable carbon dioxide removal (CDR) contracting in recent memory. Over 268,000 tonnes of durable CDR were contracted across multiple deals, anchored by two headline transactions: Boeing's 100,000-tonne offtake agreement with Charm Industrial and Frontier's 96,000-tonne, $41 million deal with BECCS (bioenergy with carbon capture and storage) company Reverion, with deliveries running from 2027 through 2030.

## Why It Matters

These aren't small pilot purchases or symbolic gestures. A single Boeing deal matching the entire annual contracted volume of some earlier years signals that corporate demand for durable CDR is consolidating around larger, longer-term commitments. The Frontier-Reverion deal is notable for its multi-year structure and disclosed price, which works out to roughly $427 per tonne. That's a useful data point in a market where pricing remains opaque and contested. Together, these two deals account for about 73% of November's total volume, showing how a handful of large buyers continue to set the pace.

## The Details

**Boeing-Charm Industrial** is the month's single largest transaction. Charm Industrial converts biomass into bio-oil and biochar, then stores the bio-oil underground to permanently sequester carbon. Boeing has been steadily building its CDR portfolio, and 100,000 tonnes is a serious commitment for any single buyer. No price was publicly disclosed for this deal.

**Frontier-Reverion** is structured as an offtake agreement worth $41 million for 96,000 tonnes. Reverion is a German BECCS company, and the tonnes are scheduled for removal between 2027 and 2030. Frontier, the advance market commitment (AMC) backed by Stripe, Alphabet, Meta, Shopify, and McKinsey, also made a smaller but symbolically interesting move: a $250,000 prepurchase from **Graphyte**, a biomass direct storage company operating in Arkansas. Graphyte's approach, which compresses and stores biomass blocks underground, is one of the simpler CDR pathways, and Frontier's willingness to buy credits from it suggests continued interest in pathway diversity.

**New infrastructure and partnerships** rounded out the month. Nasdaq and Carbon Direct opened pre-registration for a Carbon Issuance Calendar, designed to help buyers and suppliers coordinate transactions with better planning and liquidity. Think of it as a visibility tool so the market doesn't operate entirely on bilateral back-channel deals. Meanwhile, the **OSCAR** (Open Standard Carbon Removal Purchase Agreement) framework launched to standardize CDR contracting terms, addressing a real pain point: every deal currently requires bespoke legal work, which slows everything down and raises transaction costs.

On the project side, several facilities hit milestones:

- **NetZero** opened a biochar plant in Paraguaçu, Brazil, capable of removing over 6,000 tonnes of CO₂ annually while processing 16,000 tonnes of biomass.
- **Deep Sky** launched operations of Airbus's direct air capture (DAC) technology at its Alpha facility, with capacity for 250 tonnes of CO₂ per year. The tech derives from Airbus's life-support systems on the International Space Station, which is a fun origin story but also a reminder of how small these DAC units still are.
- **Kawasaki Heavy Industries** completed one of Japan's largest DAC demonstration units in Kobe, capturing 100-200 tonnes per year.
- **Return Carbon** announced plans for a DAC and storage facility in the Permian Basin in Texas, targeting 100,000-500,000 tonnes per year.
- **Pure DC** committed £24 million to support what would be the UK's largest biochar facility in Wiltshire, operated by A Healthier Earth, targeting 18,500 tonnes of removal per year.

On the policy front, **Germany's parliament** approved €476 million in CDR funding for its 2026 federal budget, including €98 million to scale CDR projects and €11.5 million specifically for CDR credit purchases. That makes Germany one of the most aggressive government buyers of CDR in the world. The **Science Based Targets initiative (SBTi)** also released a draft of its updated Corporate Net-Zero Standard, which proposes a framework for using CDR but stops short of requiring companies to purchase it. That distinction matters: a requirement would have been a demand signal; a framework is just a permission slip.

## Implications

The concentration of volume in a few large deals tells us something important about where this market is heading. We're moving from a phase of many small experimental purchases to one where a smaller number of large, creditworthy buyers are locking in multi-year supply. That's good for suppliers who need revenue certainty to finance capital-intensive projects. It's potentially concerning for smaller buyers who may find themselves priced out or unable to access supply as it gets locked up in long-term offtakes.

The $427/tonne implied price on the Frontier-Reverion deal is worth watching. BECCS has generally been positioned as one of the cheaper durable CDR pathways, and that price point, while lower than most DAC, is still well above what most voluntary carbon market participants are accustomed to paying. The standardization efforts from OSCAR and the Nasdaq/Carbon Direct calendar could help compress transaction costs over time, but the underlying removal costs aren't dropping fast yet.

## Caveats

November's 268,000-tonne figure is impressive, but it's heavily concentrated. Remove the Boeing and Frontier deals and the month looks much more modest. One or two large buyers deciding to pause could make a future month look like a drought. The Reverion tonnes are also forward commitments stretching to 2030, meaning they represent contracted intent, not verified removals. Delivery risk is real, especially for a BECCS company that needs to build and operate facilities over the next five years. And while Germany's budget commitment is encouraging, government CDR procurement remains the exception globally, not the norm. The market still depends overwhelmingly on a small club of tech-adjacent corporations willing to pay premium prices for a product most of the economy doesn't yet value.
