JPMorganChase has purchased more than 85,000 metric tons of premium carbon dioxide removal credits from Anew Climate and Aurora Sustainable Lands, using what's known as a dynamic baseline methodology. It's one of the larger single CDR credit transactions to surface recently, and the "dynamic baseline" label signals a push toward higher-integrity accounting in nature-based carbon markets.
## Why it matters
Voluntary carbon markets have spent the last two years under intense scrutiny. The core criticism: many forestry and land-use credits overstate their climate benefit because they measure carbon removal against a static, often inflated baseline. A dynamic baseline adjusts over time to reflect changing conditions, making it harder to claim credit for carbon that would have been stored anyway. When a buyer the size of JPMorganChase puts real money behind this methodology, it sends a clear signal about where credit quality standards are heading.
## The details
The credits were issued by two entities working together: Anew Climate and Aurora Sustainable Lands. Together they produced more than 85,000 metric tons of what they describe as premium CDR credits. The distinguishing feature is the dynamic baseline approach, which recalculates the counterfactual scenario (what would have happened without the project) as new data comes in, rather than locking it in at the start of a crediting period.
This matters because static baselines have been the Achilles' heel of nature-based credits. A project might assume, say, that a forest would have been cleared at a certain rate, then claim credit for preventing that clearing. But if deforestation rates drop region-wide for unrelated reasons, the baseline is too generous and the credits overcount. Dynamic baselines are designed to fix exactly this problem by updating assumptions as real-world conditions change.
JPMorganChase is the buyer. The bank has been building a portfolio of carbon credit purchases as part of its broader climate commitments. Choosing credits with a dynamic baseline methodology suggests the bank's internal carbon credit evaluation is getting more sophisticated, or at least that its advisors are steering it toward higher-quality instruments.
## Implications
Three things stand out here.
**First, dynamic baselines may become the new table stakes.** When major financial institutions start requiring them, project developers who rely on static baselines will face pressure to upgrade or risk losing access to the highest-value buyers. This is how quality standards propagate through markets: not always through regulation, but through procurement decisions by large buyers.
**Second, nature-based CDR is not dead.** Despite the wave of criticism directed at forestry credits over the past couple of years, deals like this show that buyers are still willing to pay for land-based removal, provided the methodology is credible. The key word is "premium." These aren't bargain-bin offsets. The dynamic baseline label is doing real work to differentiate these credits from the older vintage forestry credits that have drawn so much skepticism.
**Third, the scale is notable.** 85,000 metric tons is a meaningful volume for a single transaction in the voluntary market. It suggests that Anew Climate and Aurora Sustainable Lands have built project portfolios large enough to serve institutional buyers, which is no small feat for nature-based CDR developers.
One important framing note: CDR credits like these are meant to address residual emissions that cannot yet be eliminated through direct decarbonization. They are not a substitute for cutting fossil fuel use. A bank purchasing removal credits still needs to reduce its financed emissions. CDR handles the remainder, not the bulk.
## Caveats
The source information on this deal is thin, so several important questions remain unanswered.
**What kind of projects generated these credits?** "Sustainable lands" could mean improved forest management, afforestation, soil carbon, or something else entirely. The type of project matters enormously for permanence risk. A reforestation project in a fire-prone region carries different risks than soil carbon sequestration on agricultural land.
**What price did JPMorganChase pay?** Credit price is one of the strongest signals of quality in voluntary markets. Premium credits with dynamic baselines should command a significant premium over conventional nature-based credits, which have traded as low as a few dollars per ton. Without price disclosure, it's hard to gauge how much the dynamic baseline methodology actually moved the needle on valuation.
**How exactly is the dynamic baseline calculated?** The term "dynamic baseline" is not yet standardized across the industry. Different registries and methodologies implement it differently. The rigor of the approach depends entirely on the specifics: how often the baseline is updated, what data sources feed into it, and whether independent third parties verify the adjustments.
**What is the permanence guarantee?** Nature-based removal is inherently reversible. Trees burn. Soil carbon can be released. Without knowing the buffer pool size, insurance mechanisms, or contractual permanence commitments, it's impossible to fully assess credit quality.
This deal is a positive signal for the voluntary carbon market's evolution toward better methodologies. But signals are not proof. The details behind the dynamic baseline claim will determine whether this transaction represents genuine progress or just better marketing on a familiar product.
