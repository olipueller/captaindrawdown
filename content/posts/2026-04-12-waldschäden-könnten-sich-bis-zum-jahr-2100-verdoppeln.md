Europe's forests could flip from carbon sink to carbon source by the end of this century. A new study led by Rupert Seidl at the Technical University of Munich (TU München), published in *Science*, finds that fire and insect damage in European forests could more than double by 2100 under a warming scenario above 3°C. That's not a distant abstraction. It's a direct threat to one of the continent's largest natural carbon stores.
## Why it matters for CDR
Forests cover 40% of the EU's land area and bind enormous amounts of carbon. They are baked into the EU's climate targets as a key offset. But if forests stop absorbing CO₂ and start releasing it, the math behind every net-zero plan that relies on land-based carbon removal falls apart. Germany's forests are already emitting more CO₂ than they absorb. This study suggests the problem will get much worse across the continent if warming continues unchecked.
For anyone working on carbon dioxide removal, this is a flashing warning sign. Nature-based carbon sinks are not permanent if the climate keeps destabilizing them. Every ton of carbon we assumed forests would store needs a backup plan.
## The numbers
Researchers from ten European countries, led by Seidl, collected satellite data from 13,000 forest areas across Europe and ran computer simulations across multiple climate scenarios. The worst-case scenario, more than 3°C of warming, produces these projections for the year 2100:
- **Fire and insect damage**: more than double current levels
- **Storm damage**: up 20%
- **Combined effects**: potentially even worse, because these stressors interact
Seidl gives a concrete example of how cascading damage works. Heat and water scarcity weaken trees. Weakened trees can't produce enough resin to fend off bark beetles. So drought leads to pest outbreaks, which leads to dead wood, which leads to fire risk. It's a compounding cycle.
The economic toll is staggering. Forest damage and the cost of restructuring forests could hit the timber industry and forest owners with a bill of 250 billion euros.
## What Europe's forests will look like
If warming exceeds 3°C, the forests we know today simply won't exist. Spruce will disappear. Beech will vanish from nearly everywhere. Already, four out of five trees in Germany are sick.
Henrik Hartmann, a forest ecologist at the Julius Kühn Institute in Quedlinburg, says large forested areas will still exist in Europe decades from now, but they'll look fundamentally different. Shorter trees. Thinner canopy. Species adapted to heat and drought. A walk through the forest in 2100 might mean encountering Douglas fir from North America or cedar from the Mediterranean instead of the spruce and beech that define Central European woodlands today.
Researchers recommend mixed forests with multiple tree species to spread risk. Trial plots are being established across Europe to figure out which combinations work where. The biodiversity will shift too: fewer mushroom species, more raptors and wild boar. Not necessarily fewer species overall, but different ones.
## The good news, if you can call it that
The study does offer a conditional lifeline. If global warming is limited to 2°C, forests could begin recovering by mid-century. Seidl calls this the study's most important message: "Climate protection is forest protection. If we manage to significantly mitigate climate change, we significantly reduce forest damage."
That's a big "if." Current global emissions trajectories don't point to 2°C. But the finding matters because it shows the damage is not locked in. Policy choices made in the next decade will determine whether European forests remain a carbon sink or become a carbon source.
## What this changes for the CDR field
This study should make anyone relying heavily on forest-based carbon credits deeply uncomfortable. The permanence problem with biological carbon storage isn't theoretical. It's playing out right now in German forests that have already flipped to net emitters.
A few implications worth flagging:
**Credit integrity**: Forest carbon credits assume the carbon stays stored. If fire and pest damage double, the reversal risk on those credits rises sharply. Buyers and registries need to price that in.
**Portfolio balance**: This strengthens the case for durable CDR methods, things like direct air capture or mineralization, that aren't vulnerable to ecological collapse. Forests are essential for biodiversity, water, and livelihoods. But counting on them as a reliable, permanent carbon sink in a warming world is increasingly risky.
**Adaptation spending**: The 250 billion euro price tag for forest restructuring is money that competes with other climate investments. Policymakers will face hard choices about where to allocate limited funds.
## Caveats
Models are models. The study simulates 13,000 forest areas under different scenarios, but real-world interactions between fire, pests, storms, and drought are messy and hard to predict precisely. The worst-case scenario requires warming above 3°C, which is avoidable if emissions fall fast enough.
The study also doesn't claim forests will disappear entirely. It says they'll change dramatically. That's an important distinction. Forests will persist, but their composition, structure, and carbon storage capacity will be fundamentally altered.
What to watch: whether EU climate policy starts treating forest carbon sinks as the fragile, conditional asset they actually are, rather than as a guaranteed line item in net-zero accounting.
