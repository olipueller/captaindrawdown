<h2 id=the-myth>The Myth<a hidden class=anchor aria-hidden=true href=#the-myth>#</a></h2><p>&ldquo;Direct air capture consumes insane amounts of energy. It&rsquo;ll never work at scale — we&rsquo;d need entire power grids just for DAC.&rdquo;</p><p>This is probably the most common objection to DAC. And unlike last week&rsquo;s misconception, there&rsquo;s a kernel of truth here. DAC <em>is</em> energy-intensive. But the conclusion — that it can never scale — doesn&rsquo;t follow. Here&rsquo;s why.</p><h2 id=the-energy-numbers-real-ones>The Energy Numbers (Real Ones)<a hidden class=anchor aria-hidden=true href=#the-energy-numbers-real-ones>#</a></h2><p>Current DAC systems use roughly 2,000–3,000 kWh of combined thermal and electrical energy per ton of CO₂ captured. That&rsquo;s a lot. For context, it&rsquo;s about what two American households use in a year.</p><p>But here&rsquo;s what the &ldquo;too much energy&rdquo; crowd gets wrong: most of that energy is <strong>low-grade heat</strong> (80–120°C), not electricity. Solid sorbent systems like Climeworks&rsquo; need only about 500 kWh of electricity per ton — the rest is thermal energy that can come from geothermal, industrial waste heat, or concentrated solar.</p><p>Climeworks&rsquo; Mammoth plant in Iceland runs on geothermal. Carbon Engineering&rsquo;s (now Occidental) approach uses natural gas but is designed to run on renewable electricity. The energy is real; the constraint is solvable.</p><h2 id=the-entire-power-grid-myth>The &ldquo;Entire Power Grid&rdquo; Myth<a hidden class=anchor aria-hidden=true href=#the-entire-power-grid-myth>#</a></h2><p>Critics love to extrapolate: &ldquo;If we need 10 Gt of DAC removal, that&rsquo;s X% of global electricity!&rdquo; This framing has two problems.</p><p><strong>First, nobody is proposing 10 Gt of DAC.</strong> The IEA&rsquo;s Net Zero by 2050 scenario calls for ~980 Mt CO₂ of DAC by 2050 — less than 1 Gt. The rest of the removal portfolio is <a href=/posts/what-is-enhanced-weathering/>enhanced weathering</a>, biochar, ocean-based CDR, and conventional approaches. DAC doesn&rsquo;t need to do everything.</p><p><strong>Second, the renewable energy buildout is absurdly fast.</strong> The world added over 560 GW of renewable capacity in 2024 alone. Solar costs have dropped 90% since 2010. At current growth rates, global renewable capacity will more than triple by 2035.</p><p>Even at today&rsquo;s energy intensity, 1 Gt of DAC would need roughly 2,000–3,000 TWh — about 7–10% of current global electricity. That&rsquo;s significant but not impossible, especially given that:</p><ul><li>Energy intensity is falling with every generation of DAC technology</li><li>Much of the energy need is heat, not electricity</li><li>Dedicated renewable capacity for DAC doesn&rsquo;t compete with the grid</li></ul><h2 id=the-learning-curve-is-just-starting>The Learning Curve Is Just Starting<a hidden class=anchor aria-hidden=true href=#the-learning-curve-is-just-starting>#</a></h2><p>DAC is where solar was in 2005: expensive, small-scale, and easy to dismiss. Solar costs dropped 90% in 15 years. Batteries dropped 90% in a decade. Both followed Wright&rsquo;s Law — costs fall predictably as cumulative production doubles.</p><p>DAC has barely started climbing that curve. Climeworks went from Orca (4,000 t/yr, 2021) to Mammoth (36,000 t/yr, 2024) — a 9× scale-up in three years. Their next generation targets further efficiency gains.</p><p>The DOE&rsquo;s Carbon Negative Shot aims for sub-$100/ton DAC. Current costs are $400–1,000/ton. That&rsquo;s a 4–10× reduction needed — ambitious, but well within what solar and wind achieved.</p><h2 id=whos-betting-real-money>Who&rsquo;s Betting Real Money?<a hidden class=anchor aria-hidden=true href=#whos-betting-real-money>#</a></h2><p>Follow the capital:</p><ul><li><strong>Occidental/1PointFive</strong> — building the world&rsquo;s largest DAC plant in Texas (500,000 t/yr). Backed by $1.2B in DOE funding.</li><li><strong>Climeworks</strong> — raised $800M+, operating Mammoth in Iceland at 36,000 t/yr, planning further scale-up.</li><li><strong>Microsoft</strong> — signed the largest DAC purchase in history with Heirloom Carbon (315,000 tons over 10 years).</li><li><strong>Frontier</strong> (Stripe/Google/Meta/Shopify) — committed $1B+ for CDR, with major DAC allocations.</li></ul><p>These aren&rsquo;t charity donations. They&rsquo;re bets on a cost curve that&rsquo;s heading in one direction.</p><h2 id=the-real-question>The Real Question<a hidden class=anchor aria-hidden=true href=#the-real-question>#</a></h2><p>The question was never &ldquo;does DAC use energy?&rdquo; Of course it does. So does desalination. So does aluminum smelting. So does Bitcoin mining (170 TWh/yr and counting).</p><p>The real question is: <strong>can we generate enough clean energy to power DAC at the scale we need, at a cost that makes sense?</strong></p><p>Given that renewable energy is now the cheapest source of new electricity in most of the world, and that DAC costs are falling with every deployment, the answer is increasingly: yes. Not tomorrow. Not easily. But feasibly.</p><h2 id=the-bottom-line>The Bottom Line<a hidden class=anchor aria-hidden=true href=#the-bottom-line>#</a></h2><p>DAC is energy-intensive. That&rsquo;s a fact. But &ldquo;energy-intensive&rdquo; isn&rsquo;t the same as &ldquo;impossible to scale.&rdquo; The energy transition is producing cheap, abundant clean electricity faster than almost anyone predicted. DAC&rsquo;s job is to ride that wave — and every new plant makes the next one cheaper.</p><p>Saying DAC uses too much energy to work is like saying in 2005 that solar panels cost too much to matter. The cost curve has a way of making skeptics look shortsighted.</p><hr><p><em>CDR Misconception of the Week is a Friday series by CaptainDrawdown. Got a myth you want debunked?
