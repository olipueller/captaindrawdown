The Trump administration's proposed 2027 budget would cancel more than $15 billion in Department of Energy funds, explicitly targeting programs for "removing carbon dioxide from the air" alongside renewable energy and other clean energy technologies. If enacted, this would represent one of the most direct federal attacks on CDR funding in the field's short history.
## Why it matters
Federal dollars have been the single largest catalyst for CDR development in the United States. The DOE's carbon removal programs, including the Regional Direct Air Capture Hubs and related research funding, were designed to pull a nascent industry toward commercial viability. Zeroing out that support wouldn't just slow progress. It would signal to private investors that the U.S. government views carbon removal as part of what the budget document calls the "Green New Scam," lumping it in with technologies the administration considers burdensome to ratepayers and consumers.
## What the budget says
The proposal from the Office of Management and Budget frames the $15 billion-plus cancellation as targeting "unreliable renewable energy, removing carbon dioxide from the air, and other costly technologies that burden ratepayers and consumers." The language is broad and hostile: "The U.S. Government will no longer subsidize intermittent energy forms that destabilize the grid or Green New Scam projects that increase consumer costs and promote radical leftist policies."
Beyond the DOE cuts, the budget would:
- **Redirect $4.7 billion** from Biden's Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act toward "firm baseload power," meaning generation that runs around the clock, which typically excludes wind and solar.
- **Cut roughly $1.1 billion** from the DOE's Office of Science, which funds national laboratories and energy technology research.
- **Slash the EPA's budget by nearly half**, eliminating some Superfund and drinking water program funding.
- **Cut $1.6 billion from NOAA**, targeting climate research and educational grants that the proposal claims "radicalize students against markets" and "spread baseless environmental alarm."
- **Cancel $4 billion** in EV charger programs and end subsidies for electric vehicle battery manufacturers.
- **Eliminate the $4 billion** Low Income Home Energy Assistance Program.
The proposal also shifts U.S. Forest Service priorities toward timber production and wildfire response, and away from conservation and recreation.
## What this means for CDR
Let's be specific about what's at stake. The phrase "removing carbon dioxide from the air" in a federal budget cancellation list is not ambiguous. It covers direct air capture, and likely extends to other engineered removal approaches funded through DOE programs. The Regional DAC Hubs, the Carbon Negative Shot initiative, and related research grants all sit within the crosshairs.
The private CDR market is still tiny. Most companies depend on a combination of advance purchase agreements, tax credits (like 45Q), and federal grants to bridge the gap between current costs and future commercial viability. Pulling $15 billion in DOE funding doesn't just remove grants. It removes the credibility signal that attracts private capital. Investors watch where governments put money. When a government publicly labels your technology a scam, that chills investment even if the budget never passes as written.
The redirection of $4.7 billion toward baseload power is also telling. "Firm baseload" is code for natural gas and potentially coal or nuclear. The administration is making a clear bet: more fossil fuel generation, less investment in the technologies that would be needed to clean up the emissions from that generation.
This is where the moral hazard question becomes unavoidable. CDR exists to address residual emissions that can't be eliminated through decarbonization alone. It is not a substitute for cutting fossil fuel use. But this budget does the opposite of what a coherent climate strategy would look like. It simultaneously boosts fossil fuel infrastructure and kills the removal technologies that might eventually offset the hardest-to-abate slice of those emissions. That's not a plan. That's abandonment.
## Caveats
Presidential budgets are wish lists, not law. The document now goes to Congress, where final spending levels will be negotiated. As the LA Times notes, the proposal is "unlikely to pass in its current form." Some of these programs have bipartisan support, particularly in districts where DAC hubs or clean energy manufacturing facilities are being built. Republican members of Congress whose constituents benefit from DOE-funded projects may resist the deepest cuts.
Oregon Senator Jeff Merkley, the top Democrat on the budget committee, called it "an out-of-touch plea for more money for guns and bombs, and less for the things people need." Environmental groups are already mobilizing opposition.
But even if Congress softens the blow, the direction is clear. The administration views carbon removal as ideological rather than practical. That framing will shape regulatory decisions, grant administration, and interagency priorities regardless of what Congress appropriates. Companies building CDR projects in the U.S. now face a federal government that is openly hostile to their work.
The CDR field has always known that political risk was real. This budget puts a dollar figure on it: $15 billion and counting.
