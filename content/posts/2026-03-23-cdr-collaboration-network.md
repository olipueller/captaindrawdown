<blockquote><p><strong>📊 v2.1 Collaboration Atlas</strong></p><p>This is the collaboration layer of our <a href=/posts/cdr-researcher-census/>CDR Researcher Census</a>. The data reflects <strong>v2.1 paper classifications</strong> (31,234 papers). Co-authorship is a proxy for collaboration, not a perfect measure of it. Some edges are noise (250-author mega-papers), some real collaborations happen without shared papers. I&rsquo;m publishing because the patterns are too interesting to sit on. <strong>Tell me what I&rsquo;m getting wrong</strong> — <a href=https://bsky.app/profile/captaindrawdown.bsky.social>Bluesky</a> or <a href=https://x.com/CaptainDrawdown>X</a>.</p></blockquote><hr><p>CDR research looks like 122,674 individuals. It isn&rsquo;t.</p><p>It&rsquo;s a network. 284,547 co-authorship links connecting 75,234 researchers into a collaboration graph that reveals something the publication counts never could: <strong>who actually works together, and who doesn&rsquo;t.</strong></p><p>I built the co-authorship graph from every CDR paper in the <a href=/posts/cdr-researcher-census/>census</a>. Every pair of authors who share a paper gets an edge, weighted inversely by team size (a 2-person paper counts more than a 200-person mega-study). Then I ran community detection, centrality analysis, and cross-method bridge identification.</p><p>What came out surprised me.</p><h2 id=the-silo-problem>The Silo Problem<a hidden class=anchor aria-hidden=true href=#the-silo-problem>#</a></h2><p>CDR isn&rsquo;t one field. It&rsquo;s seven fields that barely talk to each other.</p><p>The pathway collaboration matrix tells the story. Within-pathway co-authorships dwarf cross-method links:</p><table><thead><tr><th>Pathway</th><th>Within-pathway edges</th><th>% of their total</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>Soil Carbon</td><td>146,950</td><td>~85%</td></tr><tr><td>General CDR</td><td>50,350</td><td>~75%</td></tr><tr><td>Biochar</td><td>59,801</td><td>~82%</td></tr><tr><td>BECCS</td><td>6,315</td><td>~62%</td></tr><tr><td><a href=/posts/what-is-enhanced-weathering/>Enhanced Weathering</a></td><td>12,962</td><td>~71%</td></tr><tr><td>Ocean CDR</td><td>10,180</td><td>~70%</td></tr><tr><td>DAC</td><td>6,278</td><td>~68%</td></tr></tbody></table><p>Soil Carbon researchers write papers with other Soil Carbon researchers 85% of the time. Biochar researchers stay in the Biochar world 82% of the time. General CDR spans methods more (75%), but that&rsquo;s still three-quarters same-method. DAC improved substantially with better classification (68% within-method) but remains relatively isolated. The total cross-method edges — 87,894 — represent about 31% of all collaboration links.</p><p>The strongest cross-method bridge? <strong>Soil Carbon ↔ Biochar</strong> with 9,975 shared co-authorships. That makes intuitive sense — biochar is a soil amendment. The researchers overlap because the science overlaps.</p><p>DAC now maintains 96 co-authors shared with Soil Carbon, 128 with Biochar, and 2,467 with General CDR, reflecting its improved classification. <strong>Ocean CDR ↔ <a href=/posts/what-is-enhanced-weathering/>Enhanced Weathering</a></strong> remains weak at 778 shared co-authors. <strong>BECCS ↔ <a href=/posts/what-is-enhanced-weathering/>Enhanced Weathering</a></strong> is the weakest major link at 19.</p><p>This matters because the hardest CDR problems are cross-method problems. Hybrid systems. Portfolio optimization. Life-cycle comparisons. MRV frameworks that work across pathways. If the researchers aren&rsquo;t talking to each other, those problems don&rsquo;t get solved.</p><h2 id=131-research-communities>131 Research Communities<a hidden class=anchor aria-hidden=true href=#131-research-communities>#</a></h2><p>I ran Louvain community detection on the network and found <strong>131 distinct research communities</strong> — clusters of researchers who collaborate more with each other than with outsiders.</p><p>Each community has a personality. A dominant pathway, a home country, anchor institutions, and key figures who hold the cluster together. Here are some of the most notable:</p><p><strong>The India Soil Carbon Network</strong> (Community 0 — 1,800 researchers). India&rsquo;s massive agricultural research system, concentrated around ICAR institutes. Led by Ram Swaroop Meena (h-index 109) and Avijit Ghosh. 96% Soil Carbon, deeply regional. This is the single largest research community in CDR by raw numbers — and it&rsquo;s almost entirely focused on one method in one country.</p><p><strong>The Kuzyakov Cluster</strong> (Community 1 — 1,191 researchers). Centered on Yakov Kuzyakov at Georg-August University Göttingen, the researcher with the highest network degree in the entire graph (505 co-authors). It&rsquo;s a Sino-German soil carbon community with the Chinese Academy of Sciences and Göttingen as twin anchors. The fact that this community has 1,191 members despite being anchored in one person&rsquo;s network shows the depth of collaboration around soil carbon science.</p><p><strong>The Brazilian Soil Carbon Hub</strong> (Community 3 — 926 researchers). 97% Soil Carbon, 73% Brazil, anchored by the University of São Paulo. Led by Maurício Roberto Cherubin (h-index 50) and Carlos Eduardo Pellegrino Cerri (h-index 93). Brazil&rsquo;s tropical soils research community is world-class and remarkably self-contained.</p><p><strong>The Ocean-Bridge Community</strong> (Community 5 — 808 researchers). Led by Phil Renforth (Heriot-Watt, h-index 43) and Lennart Bach (University of Tasmania, h-index 45). This is genuinely cross-method: 27% General CDR, 17% Ocean CDR. One of the rare communities that bridges pathways intentionally.</p><p><strong>The Ocean CDR Axis</strong> (Community 9 — 652 researchers). Focused on marine biogeochemistry and ocean-based carbon removal. Led by Andreas Oschlies (GEOMAR Kiel) and David P. Keller. 71% Ocean CDR, 68% international collaboration across Europe and North America.</p><h2 id=bridge-researchers-are-rare-and-they-matter>Bridge Researchers Are Rare (and They Matter)<a hidden class=anchor aria-hidden=true href=#bridge-researchers-are-rare-and-they-matter>#</a></h2><p>Out of 122,674 CDR researchers, only <strong>3,264 connect three or more CDR methods</strong> through their co-authorship networks. That&rsquo;s 2.7%.</p><p>The researchers bridging all seven methods remain sparse but meaningful. From the top connectors:</p><table><thead><tr><th>Researcher</th><th>Bridge Score</th><th>Degree</th><th>h-index</th><th>Home Pathway</th><th>Institution</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td><a href=/cdr-researcher-census/researchers/yakov-kuzyakov-a5062508/>Yakov Kuzyakov</a></td><td>4</td><td>505</td><td>142</td><td>Soil Carbon</td><td>Georg-August-Universität Göttingen</td></tr><tr><td>Josep Peñuelas</td><td>6</td><td>269</td><td>183</td><td>Soil Carbon</td><td>CSIC</td></tr><tr><td><a href=/cdr-researcher-census/researchers/pete-smith-a5028843/>Pete Smith</a></td><td>5</td><td>203</td><td>169</td><td>Soil Carbon</td><td>ClimateXChange Scotland</td></tr><tr><td><a href=/cdr-researcher-census/researchers/noah-j-planavsky-a5031830/>Noah J. Yakov Kuzyakov, despite enormous soil carbon focus (community 1 is 95% soil carbon), still reaches into four pathways through his co-authorship network. Josep Peñuelas and Noah Planavsky bridge six methods each. These researchers are the connective tissue of the field.</p><p>Notice: most high-degree connectors are primarily soil carbon or environmental science researchers who naturally encounter multiple CDR pathways. The field&rsquo;s bridges aren&rsquo;t typically specialists who branched out — they&rsquo;re people working in problems that force cross-method engagement.</p><p>The bridge score 5 tier includes researchers with strong cross-method presence. These people matter more than their publication counts suggest. In a network this siloed, researchers who maintain cross-method connections are how ideas flow between communities.</p><h2 id=the-chinaus-axis>The China–US Axis<a hidden class=anchor aria-hidden=true href=#the-chinaus-axis>#</a></h2><p>International collaboration in CDR is dominated by one corridor: <strong>China ↔ United States</strong>, with 11,126 co-authored papers and 8,831 researchers involved. That&rsquo;s the strongest bilateral research relationship in CDR — nearly four times the next largest links (China ↔ UK at 3,201 papers and Australia ↔ China at 3,105 papers).</p><p>The China–US research axis is the backbone of international CDR science. Any disruption to scientific collaboration between these two countries doesn&rsquo;t just affect bilateral projects — it fragments the global network. The 9,088 co-authored papers represent real intellectual infrastructure.</p><p>Notably absent from the strongest collaboration links: any robust intra-European corridor. European CDR researchers collaborate more with China and the US than with each other. Given EU ambitions to lead on carbon removal certification and deployment, that&rsquo;s worth noting.</p><h2 id=the-giant-component-and-the-48-outside-it>The Giant Component (and the 48% Outside It)<a hidden class=anchor aria-hidden=true href=#the-giant-component-and-the-48-outside-it>#</a></h2><p>Of the 122,674 researchers in the census, 75,234 (61%) have at least one co-author in the CDR graph. But connectivity and <em>connectedness</em> are different things.</p><p>The giant component — the single largest connected subgraph where you can trace a path from any researcher to any other — contains <strong>41,828 researchers</strong>. That&rsquo;s 56% of all researchers, or 56% of those with any CDR collaborations.</p><p>The other 44% exists in <strong>thousands of smaller, disconnected clusters</strong>. Research groups that collaborate internally but have no co-authorship link to the main network. These are the islands. Many are country-specific: a Chinese university group working on soil carbon with no international co-authors, or an Indian institute publishing on biochar without connecting to the global biochar community.</p><p>This fragmentation reflects both the silo problem and geographic clustering. It&rsquo;s not just that Ocean CDR researchers don&rsquo;t talk to BECCS researchers. It&rsquo;s that parts of the CDR research workforce operate in their own ecosystems entirely.</p><h2 id=what-this-means>What This Means<a hidden class=anchor aria-hidden=true href=#what-this-means>#</a></h2><p>Three takeaways:</p><p><strong>1. CDR needs more generalists.</strong> The 3,264 bridge researchers who span three or more methods are doing the connective work the field depends on. That&rsquo;s only 2.7% of CDR researchers — far too few. Funding agencies should explicitly support cross-method research programs, not just single-pathway deep dives.</p><p><strong>2. The China–US research axis is the backbone of international CDR science.</strong> The 11,126 co-authored papers represent real intellectual infrastructure. Any disruption to this corridor doesn&rsquo;t just affect bilateral projects — it fragments the entire global network.</p><p><strong>3. Europe has a collaboration gap.</strong> Despite strong national research programs and EU-level CDR ambitions, European countries collaborate more with China and the US than with each other. If the EU wants to build a coherent CDR innovation ecosystem, the research networks aren&rsquo;t there yet.</p><h2 id=explore-the-atlas>Explore the Atlas<a hidden class=anchor aria-hidden=true href=#explore-the-atlas>#</a></h2><p>The full collaboration network is interactive. Edge weight = Σ(1/(n-1)) across shared papers, where n = number of authors per paper. This downweights mega-author papers.</p><p><strong>Community detection:</strong> <a href=https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louvain_method>Louvain algorithm</a> on the full network (75,234 nodes, resolution=1.0). Found 131 communities, with the largest containing 41,828 researchers.</p><p><strong>Bridge score:</strong> Count of distinct CDR pathways represented in a researcher&rsquo;s co-author neighborhood (including their own primary pathway). Score ≥ 3 qualifies as a bridge researcher (3,264 identified).</p><p><strong>Centrality:</strong> PageRank (weighted), betweenness centrality, and degree centrality computed on the network.</p><p><strong>Data source:</strong> <a href=https://openalex.org/>OpenAlex API</a> (CC0 license).
