<p>Most net-zero pathway models assume large amounts of land-based carbon dioxide removal. New forests. Bioenergy plantations. Soil restoration. The assumptions are baked into the models as givens.</p><p>A new study in <em>Nature Climate Change</em> asks the question those models often skip: <strong>where, specifically, does this land come from — and who lives there?</strong></p><p>Ruben Prütz of the Potsdam Institute for Climate Impact Research and colleagues analyzed five widely used 1.5°C pathway models. They mapped the geographic distribution of where land-intensive CDR deployment is expected to occur across the modeled scenarios.</p><p>The findings are specific enough to be uncomfortable.</p><h2 id=what-the-models-assume>What the Models Assume<a hidden class=anchor aria-hidden=true href=#what-the-models-assume>#</a></h2><p>The 1.5°C models examined by Prütz et al. include REMIND-MAgPIE, IMAGE, MESSAGE, WITCH, and GCAM — the integrated assessment models that underpin the IPCC&rsquo;s pathway analysis. These models are used by policymakers, central banks, and major investors to assess climate risk and transition scenarios.</p><p>They are, for the most part, agreed on one thing: reaching 1.5°C with moderate overshoot requires significant land-based CDR. The typical approaches modeled are:</p><p><strong>Bioenergy with carbon capture and storage (BECCS):</strong> Grow energy crops, burn them for power, capture the CO₂ at the point of combustion, and store it geologically. The land required to grow sufficient biomass at scale runs to hundreds of millions of hectares in some models.</p><p><strong>Afforestation and reforestation:</strong> Planting trees on previously unforested land. Land requirements vary widely by scenario but can reach 500 million hectares or more.</p><p><strong>Soil carbon enhancement:</strong> Lower in land impact than BECCS or afforestation, but still spatially concentrated.</p><p>The models treat these land areas as available. The question Prütz et al. asked is: available to whom, and from whom?</p><h2 id=the-geographic-concentration-problem>The Geographic Concentration Problem<a hidden class=anchor aria-hidden=true href=#the-geographic-concentration-problem>#</a></h2><p>The study found that the models concentrate land-use demands in specific regions — and those regions are predominantly in the <strong>Global South</strong>.</p><p>Countries in sub-Saharan Africa, South and Southeast Asia, and Latin America appear frequently as the expected locations for large-scale land-based CDR under 1.5°C scenarios. These are often regions characterized by:</p><ul><li>Existing food security pressures and high agricultural land competition</li><li>Complex land tenure systems, including informal customary rights</li><li>Governance gaps that make free, prior, and informed consent (FPIC) difficult to operationalize at scale</li><li>Lower political capacity to resist international pressure to accept land-use change</li></ul><p>This is not a theoretical concern. Historical carbon offset projects — REDD+ forestry credits in particular — have generated well-documented cases of community displacement and land conflict in exactly these regions.</p><p>The models don&rsquo;t include displacement. They include land availability. These are not the same thing.</p><h2 id=why-this-matters-for-cdr-pathway-selection>Why This Matters for CDR Pathway Selection<a hidden class=anchor aria-hidden=true href=#why-this-matters-for-cdr-pathway-selection>#</a></h2><p>This research does not argue against land-based CDR. Sustainable reforestation, improved land management, and soil carbon enhancement are legitimate and important pathways — when implemented with proper community engagement, benefit-sharing, and governance.</p><p>But the study&rsquo;s spatial specificity reinforces something the CDR field has been slow to internalize: <strong>pathway selection has equity implications.</strong></p><p><a href=/posts/what-is-enhanced-weathering/>Enhanced weathering</a>, ocean alkalinity enhancement, and direct air capture have much lower land footprints per tonne of CO₂ removed. They don&rsquo;t require clearing existing vegetation, converting agricultural land, or negotiating complex tenure arrangements with rural communities. At scale, they face less political and social resistance — and they don&rsquo;t carry the structural risk of replicating historical patterns of resource extraction from the Global South.</p><p>At CDI, our portfolio reflects this logic. We back pathways that are scientifically rigorous and, where possible, land-light. Not because land-based CDR is inherently problematic, but because the risk-adjusted case for land-light pathways looks stronger the more carefully you map the geographic and governance challenges of the alternative.</p><h2 id=the-modeling-blind-spot>The Modeling Blind Spot<a hidden class=anchor aria-hidden=true href=#the-modeling-blind-spot>#</a></h2><p>There is a broader methodological point worth flagging. The models that underpin IPCC scenarios are optimizing for physical outcomes — gigatonnes of CO₂ removed at least cost, subject to climate and energy system constraints. They are not well-equipped to model social and governance variables: community consent, legal uncertainty, political feasibility, or the time required to build the trust that large-scale land-use change requires.</p><p>This means the models systematically underestimate the real-world friction of land-based CDR deployment. That friction is not just an implementation detail — it affects whether the CDR happens at all.</p><p>The Prütz et al. study makes the geographic distribution of that friction visible. That&rsquo;s a useful contribution, and one that policymakers designing CDR support frameworks should take seriously.</p><hr><p><em>Source: Nature Climate Change, Prütz et al., via <a href=https://news.mongabay.com/short-article/2026/04/who-gives-up-land-for-the-worlds-climate-fixes/>Mongabay</a>.</em></p><hr><h3 id=-related-reading>🔗 Related Reading<a hidden class=anchor aria-hidden=true href=#-related-reading>#</a></h3><ul><li><a href=/posts/cdr-census-top-researchers/>The Top Minds in Every CDR Method — And Why I&rsquo;m Showing You</a></li><li><a href=/posts/joule-eu-ets-cdr-integration-68-86mt/>Joule Paper: EU ETS Integration Could Drive 68–86 Mt CDR Per Year by 2050</a></li><li><a href=/posts/mercedes-f1-cdr-portfolio/>Mercedes F1 Goes All-In on Carbon Removal — 7 New Projects Across 6 Pathways</a></li><li><a href=/posts/mercedes-f1-cdr-portfolio/>Mercedes F1 Builds One of Motorsport&rsquo;s Biggest CDR Portfolios — 18,900 Tonnes Across 6 Pathways</a></li></ul>
