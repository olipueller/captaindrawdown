<p>Drax just reported record renewable generation — and is now exploring data centre colocation on the same site where it&rsquo;s trying to build one of the world&rsquo;s largest BECCS installations.</p><p>Sit with that combination for a second.</p><p>Drax is the UK&rsquo;s largest renewable generator by output. Its North Yorkshire plant burns biomass to generate electricity, captures the CO₂ from combustion, and plans to store it permanently — bioenergy with carbon capture and storage. If BECCS works as advertised, every unit of electricity generated is carbon-negative: you pulled CO₂ from the atmosphere (as trees grow), burned it for power, and buried the emissions. Net negative.</p><p>Now they want to power data centres from the same site.</p><p>Data centres are enormous power consumers. A hyperscale facility can run 100-500 megawatts continuously. Colocation with a power generator eliminates transmission losses, can enable direct power purchase agreements, and gives a tech company a clean story about where their electrons come from. For Drax, it&rsquo;s a revenue diversification play — BECCS development is expensive and years from first commercial carbon credits, so anchoring a data centre customer provides near-term cash flow and load factor.</p><p>The interesting question is whether this becomes a template. Carbon-negative power + compute-intensive industrial load, co-sited, integrated economically. There are other potential versions: a DAC facility collocated with a nuclear plant, or enhanced geothermal powering an industrial cluster that includes CO₂ removal. The logic is the same — match high-reliability, low-carbon power generation with loads that need reliability and want the clean credential.</p><p>One caveat that can&rsquo;t be avoided with Drax: the biomass sustainability questions are real and unresolved. Their sourcing practices — particularly around what qualifies as &ldquo;waste wood&rdquo; — have been contested by environmental groups for years. BECCS&rsquo; carbon-negativity depends entirely on the lifecycle accounting of the biomass supply chain. If the wood sourcing is questionable, the carbon math is questionable.</p><p>That doesn&rsquo;t make the colocation model uninteresting. It does mean the model only works if the underlying CDR claim holds up. Watch both.</p>
