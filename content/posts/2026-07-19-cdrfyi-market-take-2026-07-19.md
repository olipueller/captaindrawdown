The number I keep circling back to: [CDR.fyi's report](https://www.cdr.fyi/blog/cdr-pricing-survey-may-2026) with OPIS finds the buyer-supplier price gap for durable CDR has narrowed from $107/t last year to $98/t today, with respondents projecting it will compress to roughly $48/t by 2030.

A $9/t year-over-year narrowing is not a dramatic collapse, but it is directionally the right story. Last year's survey, per CDR.fyi, established the uncomfortable baseline: what buyers called "expensive" was often lower than what suppliers said they needed to book a reasonable margin. A market cannot clear from that starting position without something moving. What appears to be moving is buyer sophistication — more real transactions, more delivered tonnes, more honest engagement with method-level cost structures. That is what "market learning" actually looks like on a spreadsheet.

The more interesting wrinkle CDR.fyi surfaces is the method-mix inversion. Biochar and BECCS — the two pathways doing most of the contracting today — show the narrowest current-year price gaps but the widest projected 2030 gaps. Read that carefully. Buyers of the most mature pathways still expect prices to fall as volumes scale; suppliers in those same pathways increasingly do not. CDR.fyi points to biomass constraints, financing costs, and firmer forward demand as the likely reasons suppliers see cost floors where buyers see a learning curve. If suppliers are right, the "cheap biochar future" a lot of corporate procurement decks quietly assume is not coming. If buyers are right, a lot of BECCS project finance models are too optimistic. Both cannot be correct.

The blocker list in the report is worth sitting with too: budget constraints, delivery risk, internal business case, and lack of policy incentives. Notice that only one of those — budget — is a pure price problem. The other three are contracting, verification, and policy problems, which is why CDR.fyi's blunt conclusion that durable CDR is unlikely to broadly reach $100/t by 2030 does not, by itself, doom the market. Price is one axis; bankability is another.

The honest caveat: this is a survey of stated expectations, not cleared trades. Respondents on both sides have incentives to anchor. A $48/t projected 2030 gap could easily be a $70/t gap in practice, or a $20/t gap if a few large offtakes reset reference prices. CDR.fyi is measuring sentiment converging on reality, not reality itself.

What I will watch next: whether the next wave of multi-year, multi-method offtakes starts pricing off this narrowing band — because that is when survey convergence becomes market convergence.
