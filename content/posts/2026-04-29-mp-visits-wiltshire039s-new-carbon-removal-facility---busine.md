Pure Data Centres is putting £24 million into Royal Wootton Bassett, Wiltshire to build what the company says will become the UK's largest biochar carbon-removal facility. The site got a profile boost this week when the local MP toured the plant, signaling political interest in a CDR project that, if it delivers on its scale claim, would meaningfully shift the UK's biochar production base.
## Why this matters
Biochar is one of the more boring corners of CDR, and that is exactly why it deserves attention. It is a near-term, lower-cost durable removal pathway. You take biomass, heat it without oxygen (pyrolysis), and lock the carbon into a stable form that resists decay for centuries when applied to soils or used in construction materials.
The UK has plenty of biochar pilots and small commercial units, but very little at industrial scale. A £24 million single-site investment, branded as the country's largest, is the kind of step-up the domestic supply side needs if British buyers want to source removals locally rather than importing credits from operators in Scandinavia, North America, or the tropics.
## The details we have
Here is what the source confirms: Pure Data Centres is the developer. The location is Royal Wootton Bassett in Wiltshire. The headline figure is £24 million. The company claims the facility will be the UK's largest biochar carbon-removal plant. A Wiltshire MP visited the site, which is how the story surfaced.
The source content available is thin beyond those facts. We do not have published numbers on annual feedstock throughput, expected tonnes of CO2 removed per year, the pyrolysis technology vendor, the planned end-use of the biochar (agricultural soils, concrete admixture, filtration), or the offtake structure for the resulting carbon credits. We also do not have a stated commissioning date or which registry the project intends to use for issuance.
The Pure Data Centres name is itself worth flagging. The branding suggests a link to data center infrastructure, which would fit a pattern we are seeing elsewhere: compute operators looking for durable removal supply to match the carbon footprint of new AI and cloud build-outs. Whether this Wiltshire facility is captive supply for a data center parent, a merchant project selling to third parties, or some hybrid is not specified in what is publicly available.
## What it could change
If the plant reaches the scale implied by the "UK's largest" claim and a £24 million capital outlay, three things follow.
First, domestic UK buyers gain a credible local source. Corporates with net-zero claims under UK reporting frameworks increasingly prefer credits with a clear domestic provenance. A Wiltshire-based plant producing certified biochar removals serves that demand directly.
Second, biochar pricing in the UK could soften at the margin. Today, durable biochar credits trade in a range that varies widely by methodology and geography, often between roughly £100 and £200 per tonne. More domestic supply does not collapse that range overnight, but it does give buyers options and pressure on premium pricing.
Third, a successful build at this scale gives planners and investors a reference project. The hardest part of UK biochar scale-up is not the chemistry. It is feedstock contracts, planning permission, grid connections for any electrified pyrolysis steps, and offtake. A working £24 million plant with an MP cutting ribbons is exactly the kind of de-risking template that unlocks the next three projects.
## Caveats worth naming
The "largest in the UK" claim is a marketing statement until independent throughput numbers are published. Largest by capital cost, by feedstock tonnes, by biochar output, or by certified removals issued? Each metric tells a different story.
Biochar's permanence depends on how the material is handled after production. Soil-applied biochar in temperate climates is generally credited with century-plus durability under current methodologies, but the exact crediting depends on which protocol the project registers under (Puro.earth, Verra, the European biochar certificate, or others). The source does not say.
Feedstock sourcing is the quiet risk in every biochar project. If the plant pulls from sustainable forestry residues or genuine waste streams, the carbon math works. If it competes with existing biomass uses, the additionality picture gets messier. We do not yet have the feedstock plan.
And the standard reminder: biochar removals at this scale are useful for residual, hard-to-abate emissions. They are not a substitute for cutting fossil fuel use at the source. A 24 million pound plant, even a successful one, removes a small fraction of UK annual emissions. It is a piece of the portfolio, not the answer.
## What to watch
The next disclosures to look for: annual tonnes of CO2 removed, feedstock source and contracts, the certification body, named offtake buyers, and the commissioning timeline. Once those land, we will know whether this is a serious anchor project for UK biochar or a well-photographed ribbon-cutting.
