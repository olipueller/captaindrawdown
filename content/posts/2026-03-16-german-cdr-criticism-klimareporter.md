<p>Peter Droege doesn&rsquo;t mince words. Writing in <a href=https://klimareporter.de/technik/der-gefaehrliche-traum-von-der-co2-entnahme>klimareporter.de</a>, the director of the Liechtenstein Institute for Strategic Development calls Germany&rsquo;s publicly funded CDR research programs — &ldquo;CDR terra&rdquo; and &ldquo;CDR mare&rdquo; — a dangerous distraction from the real drivers of the climate crisis.</p><p>His argument boils down to four points. Each one deserves a straight answer.</p><h2 id=critique-1-the-math-doesnt-work>Critique #1: The Math Doesn&rsquo;t Work<a hidden class=anchor aria-hidden=true href=#critique-1-the-math-doesnt-work>#</a></h2><p>Droege argues that the maximum conceivable CDR capacity in Germany can&rsquo;t even close the gap on the country&rsquo;s so-called &ldquo;unavoidable&rdquo; residual emissions — emissions that remain even under Germany&rsquo;s already <a href=https://klimareporter.de/deutschland/koalition-kaempft-mit-der-klimaluecke>insufficient climate targets</a>.</p><p><strong>He&rsquo;s partly right.</strong> Germany alone won&rsquo;t remove its way to net zero with domestic CDR capacity. No single country will. But that&rsquo;s not actually the argument anyone serious is making. CDR is a global portfolio problem. Iceland stores CO₂ from European DAC plants. <a href=/posts/what-is-enhanced-weathering/>Enhanced weathering</a> works best in tropical soils. The question isn&rsquo;t whether Germany can do it all domestically — it&rsquo;s whether Germany should be investing in CDR science so it can contribute to a global solution. The answer is obviously yes.</p><h2 id=critique-2-cdr-enables-delay>Critique #2: CDR Enables Delay<a hidden class=anchor aria-hidden=true href=#critique-2-cdr-enables-delay>#</a></h2><p>This is the &ldquo;moral hazard&rdquo; argument, and it&rsquo;s the most common one leveled at carbon removal: if companies and governments believe CDR will clean up after them, they&rsquo;ll drag their feet on cutting emissions.</p><p><strong>Fair concern, wrong conclusion.</strong> The IPCC&rsquo;s own scenarios require <em>both</em> deep emissions cuts <em>and</em> large-scale CDR to limit warming to 1.5°C or even 2°C (<a href=https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg3/>IPCC AR6 WGIII, 2022</a>). We can&rsquo;t choose one. Arguing against CDR because some actors might misuse it is like arguing against seatbelts because some drivers might speed. The fix is better regulation, not abandoning a necessary technology.</p><h2 id=critique-3-some-methods-harm-nature>Critique #3: Some Methods Harm Nature<a hidden class=anchor aria-hidden=true href=#critique-3-some-methods-harm-nature>#</a></h2><p>Droege warns that biological CDR methods — afforestation with monoculture plantations, BECCS (bioenergy with carbon capture and storage) — compete with natural ecosystems and damage biodiversity. He&rsquo;s also skeptical of ocean-based approaches.</p><p><strong>On monoculture plantations and BECCS: agreed.</strong> These concerns are well-documented and shared by many CDR researchers. The field has largely moved beyond simplistic tree-planting narratives, and BECCS faces serious land-use constraints (<a href=https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/1748-9326/aabf9f>Fuss et al., <em>Environmental Research Letters</em>, 2018</a>). But lumping all CDR together — <a href=/posts/what-is-enhanced-weathering/>enhanced weathering</a>, DAC, biochar, ocean alkalinity enhancement — under &ldquo;disturbing nature&rdquo; ignores the massive diversity of approaches. <a href=/posts/what-is-enhanced-weathering/>Enhanced weathering</a> on existing agricultural land doesn&rsquo;t compete with forests. DAC on volcanic rock in Iceland isn&rsquo;t displacing ecosystems.</p><h2 id=critique-4-tech-fixes-are-a-dead-end>Critique #4: Tech Fixes Are a Dead End<a hidden class=anchor aria-hidden=true href=#critique-4-tech-fixes-are-a-dead-end>#</a></h2><p>DAC is too expensive, too energy-intensive, and too dependent on resources that should go toward the energy transition, Droege argues.</p><p><strong>Today&rsquo;s costs aren&rsquo;t tomorrow&rsquo;s.</strong> Solar electricity cost $76/watt in 1977. It costs roughly $0.20/watt now. DAC is expensive at ~$400–1,000/tonne today, but costs have already fallen significantly from early prototypes, and every major analysis projects continued decline with scale (<a href=https://www.iea.org/reports/direct-air-capture-2022>IEA, 2022</a>). Writing off a technology because version 1.0 is expensive is a strange position for anyone who&rsquo;s watched renewable energy evolve.</p><h2 id=the-real-takeaway>The Real Takeaway<a hidden class=anchor aria-hidden=true href=#the-real-takeaway>#</a></h2><p>Droege&rsquo;s piece is worth reading because the skepticism is grounded in real concerns — scale limits, moral hazard, ecosystem risks. These aren&rsquo;t strawman arguments. They&rsquo;re the questions that keep honest CDR researchers up at night.</p><p>But his conclusion — that CDR is fundamentally a dangerous distraction — doesn&rsquo;t follow from his premises. The climate crisis demands emission cuts <em>and</em> carbon removal <em>and</em> biosphere restoration. It&rsquo;s a three-legged stool, and kicking out any leg means we fall.</p><p>The strongest move isn&rsquo;t to abandon CDR research. It&rsquo;s to fund it rigorously, regulate it honestly, and never — ever — let it become an excuse to slow down on emissions cuts.</p><hr><p><em>Original article: <a href=https://klimareporter.de/technik/der-gefaehrliche-traum-von-der-co2-entnahme>klimareporter.de</a> (German).
