<p>Here&rsquo;s the problem with most direct air capture: you spend enormous amounts of energy releasing the CO₂ you just captured. Heat the sorbent to 900°C (solid sorbents) or boil a solvent (liquid systems) — either way, the energy cost dominates the economics. That&rsquo;s why DAC still costs $400–$1,000 per tonne.</p><p>But what if you could release captured CO₂ by just&mldr; making the air humid?</p><p><a href=https://www.earth.com/news/scientists-unlock-more-efficient-low-energy-co2-removal/>Researchers at Arizona State University</a>, led by doctoral researcher Gayathri Yogaganeshan, have published new work explaining exactly why certain commercial polymers can capture CO₂ from dry air and release it when exposed to moisture. No heat. No pressure. Just wet/dry cycling. And for the first time, they&rsquo;ve mapped the structural differences that make some materials dramatically better at it than others.</p><h2 id=two-polymers-very-different-performance>Two polymers, very different performance<a hidden class=anchor aria-hidden=true href=#two-polymers-very-different-performance>#</a></h2><p>The team compared two commercially available polymers: IRA-900 and FAA-3. Both use the same humidity-swing principle, but IRA-900 captured significantly more CO₂ and did it faster. The question was: why?</p><p>X-ray analysis revealed the answer is architectural. IRA-900 has a more open internal structure — extra pores, molecular clustering, stacked internal layers that create pathways for both gas and water transport. When humidity rises, both materials swell. When it drops, they relax. But IRA-900 releases CO₂ faster in humid conditions and recaptures it sooner during drying.</p><p>Here&rsquo;s the key insight that matters for DAC engineering: <strong>water access and CO₂ capture don&rsquo;t improve together automatically.</strong> Water entered both materials at the same rate despite their different pore layouts — tight molecular packing controls water uptake regardless of architecture. But CO₂ transport was architecture-dependent. This means you can potentially optimize each property independently — which is exactly what materials designers need to hear.</p><h2 id=the-durability-wall>The durability wall<a hidden class=anchor aria-hidden=true href=#the-durability-wall>#</a></h2><p>Before anyone gets too excited: these materials need to survive roughly <strong>100,000 wet-dry cycles</strong> to make economic sense at scale. Current polymers are brittle and modest in capacity. That&rsquo;s not a small engineering challenge — it&rsquo;s the kind of problem that separates lab curiosities from deployed technologies.</p><p>With only 27 DAC plants commissioned worldwide and atmospheric CO₂ above 427 ppm (<a href=https://www.noaa.gov/>NOAA</a>), the field needs breakthroughs in the underlying science — not just bigger versions of existing systems. Humidity-swing approaches represent a fundamentally different cost structure: instead of paying for energy to regenerate sorbents, you&rsquo;re using ambient weather cycles. If the materials can be made durable enough, the economics shift dramatically.</p><p>This is the kind of fundamental research that determines whether DAC gets cheap enough to matter at gigatonne scale. Not glamorous. Absolutely essential.</p>
