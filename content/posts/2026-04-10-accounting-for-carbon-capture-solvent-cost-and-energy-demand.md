Most climate mitigation scenarios assume we can scale BECCS and DAC without worrying about where all the capture solvents come from. Chanal, Humpage, and Millinger decided to actually check that assumption, and the results should make modelers uncomfortable.

Their work shows that producing chemical sorbents at the scale required for meaningful CDR has significant implications for system cost, energy use, and material demand that most energy system models simply ignore. This is the kind of blind spot that leads to scenarios looking great on paper while hiding real bottlenecks.

Think about it: every ton of CO2 captured requires solvents that degrade over time and need replacement. Multiply that by the gigatons per year in net-zero pathways, and you're talking about a chemical manufacturing challenge that rivals some of today's largest industrial supply chains. The energy to produce those solvents has to come from somewhere, and that energy demand feeds back into the very system you're trying to decarbonize.

This isn't an argument against BECCS or DAC. It's an argument against sloppy accounting. If we're going to bet trillions of dollars on these pathways, the models guiding those bets need to reflect the full cost structure, including the upstream inputs that everyone conveniently leaves out of the optimization.

The researchers are doing exactly what the CDR field needs more of: stress-testing assumptions before they become policy commitments we can't walk back.
