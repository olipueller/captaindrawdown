The Department of Energy has sent Congress a list of roughly 2,000 Biden-era awards it plans to "retain or modify," but the move offers cold comfort to the vast majority of the ~320 projects cancelled last year. Most of those terminated awards, which disproportionately targeted companies in Democrat-led states, remain dead. The largest casualties include the Pacific Northwest Hydrogen Hub and California's Arches Hydrogen Hub.
## Why it matters
For CDR-adjacent clean energy projects that depend on federal funding, this list is the clearest signal yet of which bets the current DOE intends to keep and which it has permanently walked away from. Hydrogen hubs, grid resilience programs, and advanced energy demonstrations were all part of the pipeline that could support future carbon removal infrastructure. When DOE pulls funding from these projects, it doesn't just kill individual awards. It sends a chilling signal to private capital about the reliability of federal co-investment in decarbonization.
## What's on the list, and what isn't
Of the nearly 2,000 retained awards, only 18 appear to have been previously terminated. That's less than 1% of the list. Among the reinstated projects are 13 that fell under the Grid Resilience and Innovation Program, which DOE recently rebranded as "SPARK" and reopened for another funding round.
Most projects on the retained list were never formally threatened with cancellation. Some have already been completed and are simply closing out their awards. In other words, the list is less a rescue operation and more a catalog of what survived.
The real story is what's missing. The bulk of last year's ~320 cancelled projects remain terminated. Most are still stuck in limbo, waiting for DOE to process and respond to informal disputes. The pattern of which projects survived and which didn't is hard to ignore: the Pacific Northwest and California hydrogen hubs are gone, while five remaining hubs in Delaware, Ohio, North Dakota, Maryland, and the Gulf Coast are tagged for retention or modification. Both the Delaware and Maryland hubs had been recommended for termination last spring alongside the Pacific Northwest and California hubs, but were apparently pulled back from the brink.
## Uncertainty everywhere
Even for projects on the retained list, "retained" may not mean "business as usual." The DOE's language is "retain or modify," and it's unclear which projects fall into which category, or what modifications might look like. Reduced funding? Changed timelines? Altered scope? Nobody outside DOE seems to know.
There's another wrinkle. DOE reinstated several projects earlier this year after losing a federal lawsuit led by the City of St. Paul. But most of those reinstated projects don't appear on this new congressional list. Seven of them did receive official reinstatement notices this spring, but their absence from the retained list raises questions about their long-term status.
Then there's the capacity problem. Widespread layoffs and departures of career staff across DOE have hollowed out the offices that manage these awards. Even if a project is "retained," it's fair to ask whether anyone is left at the agency to actually oversee it. The 15-month pause on funding has already done serious damage. Some awardees have abandoned their grants entirely. Others have made layoffs. Sublime Systems, a green cement company, filed for bankruptcy.
## The bigger picture for CDR
This matters for carbon removal in a few specific ways. First, hydrogen hubs were positioned as potential anchors for industrial decarbonization, the kind of hard-to-abate sector where CDR will eventually need to mop up residual emissions. Killing the largest hubs in the Pacific Northwest and California removes infrastructure that CDR projects in those regions might have eventually plugged into.
Second, the chilling effect on private investment is real and compounding. According to an analysis by the DOE Alumni Network, the agency opened just eight new funding opportunities in 2025, compared to a prior average of 50. New obligations have dropped. Some open funding opportunities appear to have been abandoned entirely, with no awardees selected and no notice that the program was being shut down. When the federal government becomes an unreliable funding partner, private co-investors price that risk in. For CDR companies that need blended public-private capital stacks to reach commercial scale, this is a material problem.
Third, the pattern of cancellations concentrated in Democrat-led states raises questions about whether funding decisions are being made on technical and economic merit or on other criteria. Regardless of the answer, the perception alone is enough to make project developers in certain states think twice about building their next facility around a federal award.
## Caveats
This list does not tell us everything. We don't know the full criteria DOE used to decide which projects to retain. We don't know what "modify" means in practice. And we don't know whether retained projects will actually receive their remaining funds on any reasonable timeline, given the staffing crisis at the agency.
It's also worth noting that 2,000 retained awards is a large number. The DOE is not abandoning its entire portfolio. But for the hundreds of projects that remain cancelled or in limbo, and for the broader clean energy and CDR sectors watching from the sidelines, the message is clear: federal funding commitments made under one administration can evaporate under the next. That's a risk factor that will shape investment decisions for years to come.
